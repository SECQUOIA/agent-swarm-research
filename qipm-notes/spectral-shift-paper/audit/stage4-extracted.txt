        The Quantum Cost of Unit-Normalized Spectral Shifting



                                                  Abstract
           We determine the oracle cost of converting a block encoding of a positive semidefinite
      contraction H into a reusable encoding of I − H with normalization exactly one. For spectra in
      [δ, ρδ]∪[c, 1], with fixed ρ > 1 and 0 < c ≤ 1, globally nonnegative polynomial approximation gives
      explicit thresholds Gr . At error Kδ with fixed K > 0, the optimal query count is Θ(δ −1+1/(2ℓ) )
      for ℓ = min{r : Gr ≤ K} ≥ 1, including threshold equality; we also determine the coarse
      tier. An even-polynomial threshold plateau yields a sharp Θ(δ −5/6 ) versus Θ(δ −1/2 ) separation
      from unrestricted conversion at a concrete accuracy, while odd transforms cost Θ(δ −1 log(1/δ)).
      Uniformly for 0 < K ≤ δ β with fixed β > 0, the unrestricted cost is Θ(δ −1 log(1/K)). In the
      intermediate joint regime we quantify a remaining multiplicative gap. The lower bounds cover
      general coherent oracle circuits, and the upper bounds preserve global contractivity. An exactly
      central sparse linear program realizes matched normal-matrix versus factor-access bounds for the
      dual Newton state and transfers the complement hierarchy, with a zero-query coarse exception.
      These are analog oracle separations, not lower bounds for solving the linear program.


1     Introduction
A positive semidefinite contraction H and its complement I − H have equally simple classical
descriptions. Their quantum access costs can be very different. A block encoding supplies H as a
designated block of a unitary. A coherent linear combination with the identity immediately supplies
(I − H)/2, but an algorithm that requires I − H itself cannot discard the factor of two. Near a
small eigenvalue of H, the desired complement eigenvalue approaches the boundary of the unit disk.
Global unitarity then restricts how accurately a finite-query circuit can follow that eigenvalue on a
whole interval.
    This normalization issue already appears explicitly in Orsucci and Dunjko’s positive-definite
linear-system algorithms [15, Section 4.3]. They require a normalized encoding of I − ηA, explain
the limitation of generic linear-combination constructions, and give specialized constructions under
stronger access assumptions. We quantify the conversion cost from a plain block encoding when the
spectrum has a shrinking low interval and a separated high band. The question is about a reusable
coherent operator, so learning an instance and preparing one output state is a different task.

 1.2                    Related work and contribution scope
 Quantum signal processing and QSVT provide the bounded-polynomial implementation tools
 used throughout this paper. Gilyén, Su, Low, and Wiebe [8] establish general singular-value
 transformations, bounded sign approximation, uniform amplification, and pairwise eigenvalue-
 transformation lower bounds. Low and Chuang [13] develop uniform spectral amplification and
 its normalization accounting. Motlagh and Wiebe’s GQSP [14] removes the usual definite-parity
 restriction through bounded unit-circle polynomial synthesis. We use these implementation results,
 including a Laurent-polynomial reduction that works for arbitrary input completions. Neither
 parity removal nor the generic amplification upper bound is a contribution here. Classical phase
 computation and finite-precision circuit synthesis are separate resource questions; Haah [9] gives a
 rigorous algorithmic treatment of such decomposition costs. Our complexity measure counts oracle
 queries and allows exact oracle-independent gates.
     The relationship between analog quantum queries and trigonometric polynomials is established
 prior work. Bessen [1] uses it to compare real-valued query models, and Laneve [11] characterizes
 QSP using state conversion and adversary feasibility. Our scalar-oracle reduction is an instance of
 this general polynomial method. The additional step is to combine global unitarity with a shrinking
 correctness interval: rescaled low-degree Taylor limits of the output defect must be nonnegative on


                                                                        3
the entire real line. This identifies the specific extremal problem Gr and produces lower bounds for
general coherent circuits, including circuits outside a single QSP sequence. The pairwise square-root
obstruction alone does not give the higher tiers.
    Constrained polynomial approximation also has a long history. Taylor [18] studies restricted
ranges, Lewis [12] treats convex constraints, and Campos-Pinto, Charles, and Després [4] develop
algorithms for positive polynomial approximation. Our threshold problem imposes global non-
negativity on R while fitting a linear target only on [1, ρ]. Its solution uses the classical exterior
Chebyshev inequality, as stated for example by Bos, Ma’u, and Waldron [2, Proposition 2.1]; our
quantitative lower bounds also use classical Fejér–Riesz factorization. The odd upper bound uses
established sign approximants from QSVT, with approximation-theory antecedents in Eremenko
and Yuditskii [6, 7]. The gap-times-log-error behavior of sign approximation is therefore not new.
Here it yields a matched comparison for the shrinking-window shift task, with an elementary lower
bound that uses correctness only on the positive low interval.
    Sarkar and Yoder [16] prove density results for QSP-related polynomial families with range
constraints both inside and outside the approximation interval, under necessary endpoint matching
conditions. They also study step-function approximation. Their qualitative approximation results
provide further context for global constraints; our question asks for quantitative degree thresholds
and coherent-query costs as a correctness interval shrinks.
    A particularly close recent comparison is Dong, Larsen, Lin, and Sarkar [5]. They formulate
prescribed-parity QSP design as minimax fitting on a subset subject to a global modulus bound,
study Remez methods with active constraints, and introduce nonlinear Fourier retraction to restore
feasibility without increasing the degree. Their work makes clear why satisfaction of bounds at
sampled points is insufficient, especially near the boundary of the feasible set. We address an analytic
asymptotic question for one target and spectral promise: exact limiting thresholds, optimal all-circuit
query powers, and constructions that attain the threshold error itself. The global contractivity and
contact preservation of our constructions are proved on continuous domains; numerical plots are
only illustrations. We do not propose a general minimax solver or a phase-synthesis algorithm.
    The closest direct motivation remains Orsucci and Dunjko [15]. Their Section 4.3 already
formulates normalized I −ηA construction, invokes QSVT lower bounds against generic amplification,
and gives sparse-value constructions for diagonally dominant matrices and local positive semidefinite
sums. Their factor-based linear-system algorithms also explain square-root condition-number
improvements and the associated support-overlap requirement. Our plain-block analysis does not
resolve their separate general sparse-value construction question. Factor and sum-of-squares access
are likewise central in King et al.’s spectral amplification [10]. Somma and de Wolf [17] prove
joint lower bounds for guided ground-state energy estimation and state preparation, including
sum-of-squares access, with guide-overlap and dimension assumptions. Those output tasks and
hypotheses differ from reusable complement conversion; their bounds are not transferred here. The
LP contribution is the explicit exact-central realization, its fully specified oracles, and the matched
dual-state and compiler comparisons. Its state upper bounds use standard amplitude estimation [3].
    Relative to these works, our contributions are the explicit globally nonnegative shift thresholds
and their complete fixed-accuracy coherent-query staircase; the even-threshold plateau and sharp
concrete parity separation; uniform continuum lower bounds matching high-accuracy amplification
and quantifying the remaining intermediate gap; and the exact-central LP realization under matched
access contracts. To our knowledge, this combination of exact thresholds, equality-attaining
constructions, and optimal shift-query exponents has not appeared in the cited literature. This
claim concerns the stated problem and theorems, not the underlying synthesis, approximation, or
factor-access techniques.

1.3   Proof structure and roadmap
Section 2 connects block encodings to bounded polynomials and represents scalar-query circuits by
trigonometric polynomials. Section 3 separates finite known supports from interval promises. In
Section 4, global unitarity forces a nonnegative Taylor limit; exterior Chebyshev growth computes its
best approximation error; and a polynomial gate pinned at the extremal contacts turns that limiting
solution into a globally contractive converter without exceeding the threshold error. Section 5 replaces
fixed-order compactness by uniform Taylor estimates and an independent Fejér–Riesz argument,
then controls the pinned construction for growing order. Section 6 gives the LP realization and
separates state preparation from coherent compilation. Section 7 states the remaining questions,
and Appendix A describes the reproducible figures and source build.

All norms of operators are spectral norms. A converter is a circuit whose gates may depend on the
stated spectral promise and accuracy, but not on the unknown matrix or on its oracle completion.
It must work for every unitary U with the prescribed compressed block. One controlled call to
U or U ∗ costs one query. Ancillas, inverse calls, and coherently controlled choices of queries are
allowed. The output is a reusable unitary: there is no conditioning on a favorable measurement
outcome. Intermediate measurements with a bounded worst-case query count can be deferred and
their classical controls made coherent. The lower bounds below therefore include that form of
adaptivity. We do not consider expected-cost algorithms with unbounded stopping times.

Lemma 2.1 (Bounded polynomial implementation). If p ∈ R[x] has degree d and |p(x)| ≤ 1 on
[−1, 1], then p(H) has an exact unit-normalized block encoding using O(d) controlled queries to any
exact block encoding of the Hermitian contraction H. If p has definite parity, d queries suffice in
the real-polynomial QSVT convention.

Proof. The definite-parity statement is the real-polynomial singular value transformation theorem
of Gilyén et al. [8, Corollary 18, extended version]. It coherently averages the phase sequences
with opposite signs to extract the real part, using an extra qubit but no normalization loss. For
any Hermitian H, the even transform gives p(|H|) = p(H), while the odd transform includes the
left/right singular-vector signs and gives sign(H)p(|H|) = p(H). On the zero eigenspace the odd
case follows from p(0) = 0.

7    Conclusion
Unit normalization makes complement conversion sensitive to spectral support and requested
accuracy. Known two-point spectra can admit exact constant-query conversion, while a low
continuum forces a sequence of optimal query powers determined by globally nonnegative polynomial
approximation. The Chebyshev thresholds and pinned constructions determine every fixed-relative-
accuracy tier, including its boundary. The even nonnegative constraint changes this staircase: the
exact plateau F1 = F2 produces a concrete exponent-5/6 cost where unrestricted conversion has
exponent 1/2. At polynomial and higher relative accuracy, the coherent conversion cost is instead
Θ(δ −1 log(1/K)).
    The main quantitative question left open is the multiplicative order in the intermediate joint
regime. Away from upper tier boundaries the proved gap is at most quadratic in the threshold
index; approaching those boundaries also requires control of the margin factor. Explicit formulas for
higher even thresholds Fr would further clarify the parity comparison. Another separate problem is
to account for the precision and classical computation needed to synthesize the threshold-attaining
circuits, particularly when no error margin is available.
    The exact-central LP demonstrates why the input and output contracts must be kept explicit.
A dual Newton state, a reusable complement block, and a digital matrix description are different
resources even for a fixed sparse instance family. The separation here quantifies conversion from
specified analog matrix access; deciding when a larger algorithm can avoid that conversion, or obtain
stronger access efficiently, requires its own analysis.


A     Reproducibility
The source archive contains the complete LATEX manuscript, bibliography and generated bibliography
file, required PDF figure, build instructions, and Python scripts. Building the paper with make
requires pdflatex and bibtex, but does not require Python. The target make figures regenerates
Figure 1 and supplementary threshold and pinned-kernel diagnostics using NumPy, SciPy, and
Matplotlib. The scripts use paths relative to their own locations and require no external data or
network access.
     The threshold computation solves the unique stationary equation in Proposition 4.3 in the
offset variable s = 2r(t − log R0 ), avoiding subtraction of nearby roots. It exports the computed
thresholds and the comparison with their leading asymptotic to CSV files. The overview checks the
closed formula for G1 and the inequalities needed for its displayed parity range. The supplementary
diagnostic evaluates the signed-error identity of the pinned construction near its contacts, avoiding
subtraction of nearly equal quantities. These floating-point calculations illustrate the proved
formulas; sampled errors are not certificates of global nonnegativity or contractivity. The archive’s
README states the software versions used for reproduction and the individual script commands.
