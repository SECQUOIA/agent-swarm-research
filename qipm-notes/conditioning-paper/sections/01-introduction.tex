\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The Newton equations of an interior-point method can become increasingly
ill-conditioned as the requested objective accuracy improves. This behavior
is often described through the particular logarithmic barrier or through a
chosen primal--dual linear system. A more basic question is which part of
the behavior is already forced by the feasible set and the objective. If
a different self-concordant barrier follows a different central path, can
it improve the accuracy exponent of the primal barrier Hessian? Which
geometric features determine the individual eigenvalue scales? These
questions matter both when choosing a barrier and when interpreting the
condition-number dependence of a linear-system solver.

We answer them for the equality-reduced primal Hessian in a fixed external
metric. Let $P$ be a compact convex feasible set with nonempty relative
interior and let a linear objective be nonconstant on its affine hull.
For primal objective gap $g$, let $L(g)$ be the feasible sublevel set and
$D(g)=\diam L(g)$. If $\widehat H_F(g)$ denotes the unscaled barrier
Hessian at a central point of gap $g$, our main conclusion is
\begin{equation}
 \kappa\bigl(\widehat H_F(g)\bigr)
 =\Theta\!\left(\frac{D(g)^2}{g^2}\right)
 \qquad(g\downarrow0)
 \label{eq:intro-diameter-law}
\end{equation}
for every fixed finite-parameter self-concordant barrier $F$. Constants
may depend on the fixed instance, metric, and barrier parameter, but not
on $g$. Thus changing the barrier can change constants and the location
of the center, while the accuracy exponent is determined by objective
geometry. The statement also has a uniform version for bounded barrier
parameters and centrality residuals bounded uniformly below one.
Compact objective sublevels suffice for a localized version on an
unbounded feasible set.

The underlying comparison is stronger than
\eqref{eq:intro-diameter-law}. The symmetric difference body
$K_g=L(g)-L(g)$ contains the unit tangent Dikin ellipsoid $E_F$ at an
approximately central point of observed gap $g$, and is contained in
$2C E_F$, where
\[
 C=\frac{\nu+2\sqrt\nu+\rho}{1-\rho},\qquad 0\leq\rho<1.
\]
Here $\nu$ is the barrier parameter and $\rho$ is the Newton decrement
of $F+c^\top x/\mu$, measured using the unscaled Hessian. Consequently,
Hessians of different barriers at the same primal gap are comparable
in Loewner order. This controls all quadratic forms and ordered
eigenvalues, even when the two centers differ. A homothetic interior
ball gives the objective-scale thickness of $K_g$; its diameter then
supplies the matching condition-number law.

\subsection{Relationship to prior work}

The geometry of self-concordant barriers is classical
\citep{nesterov1994,renegar2001,nesterov2018}. Dikin containment,
semiboundedness, and asymmetric containment are the ingredients used
here; we give the short proofs needed for our constants.
In particular, \citet[Proposition 2.3.2(iii), equation (2.3.9)]{nesterov1994}
compares the local Hessian norm with the symmetric chord radius at a
fixed interior point. It therefore already compares different barriers'
Hessians at that \emph{same point}. Our equal-gap result compares Hessians
at generally different centers, through one objective difference body.
The connection
between objective-level geometry and conditioning also has substantial
precedent. \citet{freund2003} studies primal--dual level-set geometry.
More directly, \citet[Section 5.1, Fact 5.2 and Remark 5.1]{xiongfreund2024}
use central-path ellipsoids to relate the diameter and conic radius of
primal--dual sublevels to Hessian eigenvalues. They use these geometric
relations to analyze rescaling for PDHG. Their work is a direct antecedent
to our geometric method. We instead compare the tangent primal Hessian
with a center-independent difference body at a prescribed primal gap,
and obtain the matching law \eqref{eq:intro-diameter-law} and comparison
between barriers.

Classical matrix-conditioning analyses are equally relevant.
\citet{renegar1996} relates the conditioning of barrier linear equations
to problem conditioning and conjugate-gradient solution.
\citet{pena2001,pena2002} develops a primal--dual condition perspective
and implicit-barrier methods. In particular, \citet[Proposition 3.2]{pena2002}
parameterizes the central path by primal objective gap, and his
Section 3.3 bounds the condition number of a Schur matrix
$A\nabla^2F(x)^{-1}A^\top$ by an inverse-square gap rate under its
stated assumptions. Accordingly, neither objective-gap parameterization
nor a generic inverse-square upper bound is a novelty claim here. The
operator, the matching dependence on $D(g)$, and the comparison between
barriers must be specified to identify the additional result.

Spectral splitting of logarithmic-barrier Hessians and its numerical
consequences were studied by \citet{wright1994,wright1998};
\citet{forsgren2002} provides a broader account of interior methods,
and \citet{wrightorban2002} treats degenerate nonlinear programs.
For linear programming, centered endpoint limits and their analytic
properties are classical \citep{adler1991,guler1994,halicka1999}.
Our scaled-Hessian limit at a unique LP optimum, including a degenerate
vertex, is explicitly presented as a corollary of this theory.
\citet{duistermaat2001} analyzes boundary asymptotics of the Hessian
Riemannian structure under additional smoothness assumptions; his
Remark 2.3 gives oscillatory self-concordant perturbations. Thus
oscillation of barrier derivatives is also an established phenomenon.
The additional LP results transfer every ordered spectral scale to
arbitrary finite-parameter barriers and control the weak eigenspace
relative to the fixed optimal-face tangent. This gives an $O(g)$ weak
component of the exact Newton forcing, improved to $O(g^2)$ for the
canonical logarithmic barrier. An explicitly certified barrier shows
that the general rate is sharp.

For semidefinite programming, error bounds and facial reduction connect
singularity degree with fractional powers
\citep{sturm2000,drusvyatskiy2017,lourenco2021}. We use a trace-normalized
version of Sturm's classical $3\times3$ construction, rather than claim
a new source of quarter-power error geometry. SDP spectral analyses
also predate this work: \citet{alizadeh1998} studies a primal--dual Schur
matrix, \citet{wei2010} investigates instances with failure of strict
complementarity, and \citet{sremac2021} relates singularity degree to
primal and dual spectral rates on an external regularization path.
Our operator is the Hessian compressed to the fixed equality tangent,
and our affine constraints do not vary with the path parameter. We
compute its four leading eigenvalue constants and exhibit a section
with the same optimality-system singularity degree but a different
conditioning exponent. The detailed comparison in
\cref{sec:fractional-sdp} distinguishes the relevant paths and matrices.

Finally, spectral condition numbers alone do not determine solver cost.
Cluster-sensitive conjugate-gradient analysis is classical
\citep{axelsson1994}; our explicit polynomial bound states the precise
exact-arithmetic energy-error consequence needed here. Quantum
linear-system results additionally depend on access, normalization,
right-hand-side preparation, and output requirements
\citep{gilyen2019,orsucci2021}. These distinctions remain necessary in
quantum interior-point methods, including the positive LP results of
\citet{apersgribling2026}. We give no end-to-end quantum lower bound
from the geometric conditioning law.

\subsection{Contributions and organization}

The principal developments are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Comparison at equal objective accuracy.}
\Cref{thm:difference-body,thm:diameter-law,thm:radial-spectrum} prove the
explicit difference-body containment, equal-gap Loewner comparison,
matching diameter law, and full-spectrum width bounds. The estimates
hold at an iterate's own gap for every residual below one, and are
uniform over families with bounded barrier parameters and centrality
residuals bounded uniformly below one.
To the best of our knowledge, this equal-gap comparison between
arbitrary finite-parameter barriers, with these residual-dependent
bounds and the resulting matching diameter characterization, has not
previously been stated in this form.
\item \textit{Sharp geometric and spectral examples.}
The law gives bounded conditioning at every unique polytope optimum,
including degenerate vertices, and inverse-square conditioning at a
positive-dimensional optimal face. A near-degenerate simplex has a
uniform transition between a large plateau and an inverse-square
regime. For the trace-normalized classical SDP example,
\cref{thm:fractional-spectrum} derives all four reduced-Hessian scales
and constants. Its paired section proves that singularity degree
alone does not determine the exact conditioning exponent. The
classical endpoint and error-bound ingredients are identified separately
from these explicit calculations and their all-barrier consequences.
\item \textit{Newton forcing and the limits of a condition-number analysis.}
\Cref{thm:lp-all-spectra,prop:oscillatory-barrier} establish the general
$O(g)$ and canonical $O(g^2)$ weak-projector rates and the corresponding
exact-forcing bounds. The sharp example has a globally verified finite
barrier parameter. It also shows that discarding a vanishing relative
right-hand-side component can lose asymptotically all Euclidean solution
mass. The specific rate and solution-error calculation are the
contribution; qualitative nonconvergence of central paths has prior
examples \citep{boltepauwels2022}.
\end{enumerate}

\Cref{sec:setup,sec:geometry,sec:widths} develop the general theory;
\cref{sec:classifications,sec:lp-limits,sec:fractional-sdp} give
classifications and exact examples. \Cref{sec:lp-spectra,sec:clustered-solves}
connect spectra to Newton forcing and linear solves, and
\cref{sec:formulation-scope} identifies the metric and barrier-parameter
boundaries. \Cref{sec:numerics} supplies reproducible numerical
illustrations with exact input certificates and explicit precision
screens. All asymptotic claims used in interpreting those computations
are proved in the preceding sections.
