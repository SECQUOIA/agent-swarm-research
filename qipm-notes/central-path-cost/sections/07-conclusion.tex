\section{Interpretation}
\label{sec:interpretation}

Our results separate three step counts: the steps to an accurate point
by any route, the extra steps needed to stay near the central path,
and the extra steps needed to reach a feasible dual solution. The
objective weights determine when the coordinates, singular values, or
eigenvalues start to move. Because they start in turn, the central path
can be longer than a route that moves them together toward an accurate
point. Requiring a feasible dual solution can add movement absent from
the primal path. On the dyadic linear program, these effects have
different asymptotic orders with matching bounds, in length and in
bounded local steps.

The exact distance to the accurate set matters because the endpoint of
the central path need not be the closest accurate point. It also shows
why a single rank or determinant bound may not suffice: the whole
distribution of objective weights enters the exact distance. Conversely, the sharp bound of
\cref{thm:sharp-prefix} limits how much longer a central arc can be
than the distance between its endpoints. With equal scales,
the worst ratio is of order $\sqrt{\log r}$ in the objective rank $r$,
even when the matrix space is much larger.

A new barrier or formulation requires a new comparison in its own
metric. An optimal barrier parameter alone does not remove the extra
central-path length, as the universal and entropic cube barriers and
the coupled radial barriers show. A cone barrier restricted to a slice
can also have a much smaller primal speed than the full parameter
suggests. \Cref{thm:exposed-rank} and the concrete formulations give
direct lower bounds with freely moving auxiliary variables, instead of
inferring primal cost from a parameter bound.

These conclusions assume a fixed barrier metric, a specified
starting point, a required final accuracy or feasible duality gap, and
steps bounded in the local norm at their start. Under them,
the explicit shortest routes and matching lower bounds separate the
cost of staying near the central path from the cost of a feasible dual
solution.
