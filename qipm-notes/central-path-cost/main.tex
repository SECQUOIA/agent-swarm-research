\begin{abstract}
How much movement does following a central path add to the cost of
reaching an accurate solution? We answer this question in specified
barrier Hessian metrics on boxes, rectangular spectral-norm balls and
Euclidean Jordan spectral intervals. For the standard logarithmic and
log-determinant barriers, distance from the analytic center is exactly
Euclidean in transformed spectral coordinates, and distance
to the entire accurate target set is a one-multiplier convex allocation.
For equal standard barrier scales and active objective rank $r$, the
exact worst central-length-to-endpoint-distance ratio is
$\Gamma_r=[\sum_{i=1}^r(\sqrt i-\sqrt{i-1})^2]^{1/2}$,
where $\Gamma_r^2=\frac14\log r+O(1)$.
A certified scalar comparison gives a same-accuracy bound
$c_\star\Gamma_r$, with $1.37486<c_\star<1.38$.
An explicit dyadic sparse linear program has optimal primal movement
$\Theta(r\sqrt{\log r})$ and central-neighborhood movement
$\Theta(r\log r)$, even for finite sequences with nonmonotone central
labels. This separation persists for normalized scalar barriers,
including the universal and entropic cube barriers, and uniformly for
an explicit coupled family with optimal cube parameter. On the same
linear program, returning any feasible small-gap primal--dual pair in
the product barrier metric instead requires $\Theta(r^{3/2})$ movement.
Weighted exposed-rank bounds and concrete conic formulations clarify
the role of auxiliary variables. The results count bounded local moves;
they make no arithmetic- or oracle-complexity claim.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} interior-point methods; self-concordant barriers;
central path; Riemannian distance; spectral optimization.
