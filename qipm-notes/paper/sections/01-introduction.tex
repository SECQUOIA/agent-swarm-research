\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

An interior-point method (IPM) for a linear or semidefinite program follows
the central path and reduces optimization to a sequence of structured Newton
linear systems.  A quantum interior-point method (QIPM) performs each solve
with a quantum linear-system (QLS) solver, which, in favorable access models,
prepares a quantum state proportional to the Newton direction without writing
down its coordinates.  Sparse problems look like a natural target, because
their data can be queried without reading a dense matrix.  This paper asks:
\begin{quote}
Do quantum interior-point methods beat classical interior-point methods on
sparse problems?
\end{quote}
Sparsity alone does not decide the answer.  A sparse input matrix can have
dense normal equations, null-space basis, factorization, or inverse.
Conversely, a matrix with a large condition number can have a
clustered spectrum that a classical Krylov method exploits.  A quantum state
proportional to a direction is not a classical iterate, and access to a
preconditioned matrix is not free just because that matrix is well
conditioned.  A fair comparison must therefore specify the Newton system, the
input oracle, the normalization of the block encoding, the right-hand side,
the required output, and the classical solver that is given the same
structure.

We study the question at three levels: \emph{geometry} (how much of the
ill-conditioning of the central path is forced by the problem rather than by
the barrier), \emph{access models} (what classical and quantum linear solvers
can extract from the same spectral structure, and what their inputs cost to
build), and \emph{interfaces} (whether the output of one solve can be read
out, checked, and used to set up the next).  The answer is neither a general
quantum speedup nor a general impossibility theorem.  We prove sharp bounds
at each level, state the classes of methods they cover, and so show where a
speedup or a lower bound would still have to be proved.

\subsection{State of play}
\label{sec:introduction-state-of-play}

The first QIPMs inserted a QLS solver into a classical path-following
method.  Kerenidis and Prakash treated LPs and SDPs; Casares and
Martin-Delgado developed a predictor--corrector LP method; Augustino et al.
gave conic and semidefinite variants; and work from Terlaky's group
developed inexact feasible, infeasible, orthogonal-subspace, and
iterative-refinement methods
\cite{kerenidis2020-quantum-interior-point-method-lps,
casares2020-quantum-interior-point-predictor-corrector,
augustino2023-quantum-interior-point-methods-semidefinite,
brandon2023-quantum-interior-point-methods-conic,
augustino2021-an-infeasible-inexact-quantum-interior,
augustino2022-quantum-interior-point-methods-for,
wu2023-inexact-feasible-quantum-interior-point,
mohammadisiahroudi2024-efficient-use-quantum-linear-system,
mohammadisiahroudi2025-inexact-feasible-interior-point-method}.
Their cost analyses exposed the costs of conditioning, block-encoding
normalization, tomography, QRAM, and classical updates, which Dalzell et al.
also stress in their QIPM review and resource analysis
\cite{dalzell2025-quantum-interior-point-methods,
dalzell2023-end-end-resource-analysis-quantum}.

Two newer lines change the algorithm itself rather than only the linear
solver.  The quantum central-path method (QCPM) of Augustino, Leng,
Nannicini, Terlaky, and Wu uses Hamiltonian evolution and potential
evaluation instead of repeated QLS solves followed by tomography
\cite{augustino2024-quantum-central-path-algorithm-linear}.  The
self-concordant Schr\"odinger-operator approach of Gribling et al. likewise
aims to optimize without a factor that depends on the Newton condition number
\cite{gribling2025-self-concordant-schrodinger-operators-spectral}.  Hence a
conditioning theorem must name the Newton matrix it concerns: a lower bound
on the condition number of a barrier Hessian is not a lower bound for every
quantum optimization algorithm.

Apers and Gribling avoid the condition number in a different way.  Their
algorithms for tall LPs use quantum spectral approximation and local-norm
estimation, return an explicit solution, and prove a separation in the number
of row queries when the number of constraints is sufficiently larger than the
number of variables
\cite{apers2026-quantum-speedups-linear-programming-interior}.  Among the
QIPM results compared here, this is the only proved asymptotic
quantum--classical separation.  It concerns queries to a succinct row
oracle, not automatically running time against the best classical sparse
solver whose cost scales with \(\nnz\), and its assumptions and high powers
of the number of variables make it a specific favorable regime, not a
general consequence of sparsity.

The QLS primitive itself has evolved from HHL through the improved precision
dependence of Childs, Kothari, and Somma to pseudoinversion by quantum
singular value transformation (QSVT)
\cite{harrow2009-quantum-algorithm-linear-systems-equations,
andrew2017-quantum-algorithm-systems-linear-equations,
andras2019-quantum-singular-value-transformation-beyond}.  Orsucci and Dunjko
proved upper and lower bounds for positive-definite systems that depend on the
access model.  Mori et al. proved an \(\Omega(\kappa\sqrt{s})\) lower bound
for sparse access at fixed constant error, and restated an
\(\Omega(\kappa\log(1/\epsilon))\) bound for constant sparsity from earlier
unpublished work of Harrow and Kothari.  Dalzell, Li, and Su (DLS)
introduced truncation and filtering parameters that depend on the right-hand
side and can be smaller than the worst-case condition number
\cite{orsucci2021-on-solving-classes-of-positive,
mori2026-sparsity-dependent-complexity-lower-bound,
dalzell2026-faster-quantum-linear-system-solver}.  Somma and de Wolf's
related separation between plain and sum-of-squares access concerns guided
ground-state energy estimation, not QLS (see \cref{sec:spectra-quantum})
\cite{somma2026-optimal-lower-bound-ground-state-energy}.  Hence the cost of
a QLS solve depends on the access (plain matrix, factor, sparse-entry, or a
stronger oracle) and on how much of the right-hand side lies in the
eigenspaces that are hard to invert.

Tomography is the second standard bottleneck.  A path-following method
usually needs a classical direction to update all primal and dual
coordinates, whereas a QLS solver returns only a normalized quantum state.
Tomography with state-preparation unitaries improves the dependence on
accuracy, but still scales with the dimension of the direction
\cite{mohammadisiahroudi2023-exponentially-more-precise-tomography-for,
joran2023-quantum-tomography-state-preparation-unitaries}.  Avoiding this
cost requires a different kind of output and a way to build from it the next
Newton matrix, whose diagonal scaling changes at every iteration.

Classical methods that exploit sparsity set a demanding baseline.  Practical
IPMs choose between normal equations and augmented systems, control fill-in,
reuse symbolic factorizations and preconditioners, and adapt the tolerances
of inner solves
\cite{wright1997-primal-dual-interior-point-methods,
jacek2012-interior-point-methods-25-years,
gondzio2023-general-purpose-preconditioning-regularized-interior-point}.
For problems of bounded treewidth, specialized IPMs with supplied elimination
orders run in nearly linear time in the problem size
\cite{dong2020-a-nearly-linear-time-algorithm,
gu2022-a-faster-small-treewidth-sdp}.  A claimed sparse QIPM speedup must
therefore be compared with a structure-aware factorization or Krylov method,
not with dense Gaussian elimination.

\subsection{What a matched comparison counts}
\label{sec:introduction-ledgers}

The QIPMs above use different Newton systems, state-preparation primitives,
refinement schemes, and outputs, so dimension and sparsity alone do not
compare them.  Throughout the paper we count raw queries (calls to a
precisely defined input oracle) and the QRAM writes needed to build or
refresh QRAM, and we give qualitative end-to-end accounts of construction,
reuse, recovery, and output.  Gate and arithmetic costs are given only for
selected results, so these accounts are not a uniform running-time
analysis.

\paragraph{From sparse input to the solved matrix.}
For an LP, a diagonal scaling of a sparse constraint matrix \(A\) leads to
the normal equations with matrix \(AD^2A^\top\), to an augmented KKT system,
or to an orthogonal-subspace system.  Constant row sparsity of \(A\) does not
imply constant sparsity of any of these matrices: one dense column of \(A\)
creates a clique in the graph of the normal equations, and a null-space basis
can be dense.  Conversely, a well-chosen basis or augmented system can keep
structure that the normal equations lose.  Our notation therefore
distinguishes \(\nnz(A)\), the sparsity \(s_H\) of the matrix \(H\) actually
solved, and the access to a factor \(G\) when \(H=G^\dagger G\)
(\cref{sec:newton-formulations,sec:prelim-quantum-access}).  A claim that
starts from a sparse \(A\) must prove the access properties of the matrix it
actually solves.

\paragraph{Condition number and normalization.}
A block encoding of \(H\) is a unitary whose upper-left block is
\(H/\alpha_H\) (\cref{def:block-encoding}).  The cost of inverting \(H\)
through it is governed by \(\alpha_H\lVert H^{-1}\rVert\), not by the
spectral condition number alone, and rescaling \(H\) changes neither this
product nor the problem.  Multiplying separately encoded factors can multiply
their normalizations, and forming a perfectly preconditioned matrix can
require the same input information that the solve was meant to find
(\cref{sec:preconditioning}).  A classical
preconditioner also costs something, but it can often be reused and can use
explicitly stored entries.  A matched comparison counts preconditioner costs
on both sides and gives neither side a free inverse oracle.

\paragraph{Right-hand sides and solve accuracy.}
The Newton direction solves the system for one particular right-hand
side (RHS), so worst-case QLS complexity is not always the right estimate.
A solver may ignore a hard eigenspace only if the allowed
output error permits losing the part of the solution in that eigenspace.  A
residual bound, a bound on the error of the normalized state, and a bound on
the coordinatewise error of the direction are different requirements on the
output, which we call \emph{output requirements}
(\cref{sec:prelim-output-contracts}).  They are related by inequalities that
involve the condition number, so a quantum state-error bound cannot be
compared with a classical residual tolerance without converting one into the
other.  \Cref{sec:beyond-kappa} evaluates these distinctions on exact IPM
Newton systems.

\paragraph{State preparation and readout.}
A hybrid QIPM that updates a classical iterate must estimate the full
solution vector from QLS output states, recover its norm and
scale, and check that the step stays in the neighborhood of the central path
required by the outer method.  Each state preparation for tomography repeats
every oracle
call and RHS preparation whose result is not stored.  A method that requests
only an expectation value or a compact
output avoids full readout, but must show that this output suffices for
optimization and for setting up the next iteration.
\Cref{sec:prelim-output-contracts} distinguishes classical-vector, compact,
state, density, coordinate-oracle, and heralded outputs, so that a proof for
one kind of output is not silently reused for another.

\paragraph{The classical comparator.}
For a sparse direct method, the cost per iteration is set by the actual
fill-in, the numerical factorization, and the triangular solves; the
symbolic ordering is normally reused, and several predictor or corrector
right-hand sides can share one factorization.  For a Krylov method, the cost
is the number of matrix--vector products and preconditioner applications,
times an iteration count that depends on the spectrum and the tolerance;
clustered eigenvalues can make this count far smaller than the worst-case
\(\sqrt\kappa\) estimate (\cref{prop:two-cluster-cg}).  Graph, treewidth,
block-angular, and low-rank structure can improve the classical baseline
further.  We therefore compare quantum query counts neither with dense cubic
arithmetic nor with the cost of writing a dense classical direction alone.

\paragraph{Setup, refresh, and reuse along the path.}
One-step costs do not automatically multiply by the number of IPM
iterations: a fixed sparse input can be read once, a symbolic factorization
kept, and an oracle can combine stored data with the current scalar path
parameter.  Conversely, a QRAM or block encoding of
the changing primal and slack diagonals may need new writes, and tomography
may be repeated, at every step.  \Cref{def:amortized-accounting} formalizes
this accounting.  Our lower bound for a sequence of solves
(\cref{thm:prec-online-direct-sum}) applies only when each round supplies new
oracle information; the caching argument in \cref{sec:preconditioning-lazy}
shows why, for a fixed LP, no general bound of the form \(T\) times the
one-step cost follows.

\paragraph{Query separation versus time separation.}
Raw coefficient, sparse-entry, row, block, QRAM, and preconditioned-product
queries reveal different amounts of information.  Sublinearly many row queries
can give a real query separation even when generating a queried row or the
local arithmetic prevents a time advantage; conversely, an explicitly stored
sparse matrix may have negligible query cost while repeated solves with it
remain expensive.  The Apers--Gribling result is positive in its row-query model;
our parity and holonomy lower bounds are negative in their raw local-query
models; neither carries over to another model without proof.  This is why
many later theorems bound the total of setup, solve, and recovery costs
rather than give a single power of the condition number.

\subsection{Main results}
\label{sec:introduction-answer}

Let the feasible set \(P\) be compact and convex, let the objective
\(c^\top x\) be linear with optimal value \(\mathrm{OPT}\), and let \(F\) be a
\(\nu_F\)-self-concordant barrier for \(P\).  A central point
\(x=x_F(\mu)\) has objective gap \(g=c^\top x-\mathrm{OPT}\).  Let \(W\) have
orthonormal columns spanning the directions of the feasible affine subspace,
and let \(c_{\mathcal V}\) be the projection of \(c\) onto them.  The reduced
Hessian is \(W^\top\nabla^2F(x)W\), with condition number \(\kappa_{\rm red}\)
(\cref{def:reduced-kappa}), and \(D(g)\) is the diameter of the sublevel set
\(\{y\in P:c^\top y\leq\mathrm{OPT}+g\}\).

Our first result is that part of the ill-conditioning of the central path is
forced by geometry, whatever the barrier.  The sublevel chord law
(\cref{thm:sublevel-chord-law}) states that at every exact central point
\(\kappa_{\rm red}\geq(D(g)\norm{c_{\mathcal V}}_2/(2C_Fg))^2\), where
\(C_F=\nu_F+2\sqrt{\nu_F}\).  \Cref{thm:canonical-achievability} shows that
the canonical log and log-det barriers attain this bound at equal objective
gap, up to explicit instance-dependent factors, provided the dual slack stays
bounded on the tail of the path.  Thus no choice of barrier can in general
remove the divergence.  Its rate depends on the geometry of the sublevel
sets: every barrier has \(\kappa_{\rm red}=\Omega(g^{-2})\) when the optimal
face has positive dimension, whereas the canonical log barrier has
\(\kappa_{\rm red}=\Theta(1)\) at a unique LP vertex, even a degenerate one
(\cref{cor:conditioning-regimes}).  The result does not cover methods that
avoid barrier Newton solves.

Our second result is that a large condition number does not determine the
relative classical and quantum costs.  Near the optimum of a degenerate LP,
the eigenvalues of the reduced log-barrier Hessian lie in two intervals, each
with a bounded ratio of endpoints, whose separation grows as
\(\Theta(\mu^{-2})\) (\cref{prop:two-cluster-hessian}).  Conjugate gradients
can place roots of its residual polynomial in the gap, so its iteration
count is polylogarithmic in this separation (\cref{prop:two-cluster-cg}).
QSVT, by contrast, applies a polynomial to a normalized operator, and the
polynomial must stay bounded on the whole
interval allowed by the access model, not only at the eigenvalues.
For block encodings normalized at the operator norm (norm-tight encodings),
plain block-encoding access has worst-case QLS cost
\(\Thetatilde(\kappa)\), even for two-point clusters, when
\(N_{\rm QLS}\gtrsim\kappa\); the lower bound is due to Orsucci and Dunjko.
With norm-tight factor access \(H=G^\dagger G\), there is always a
polynomial transformation whose degree times amplitude bound (its maximum
absolute value on the allowed interval) is \(\Thetatilde(\sqrt\kappa)\); only
the corresponding end-to-end solve needs the overlap assumptions in
\cref{thm:quantum-cluster-obstruction}.  The right-hand sides that occur on
the central path are easier still: the exact central-path right-hand side
has only an \(O(\mu^2)\) fraction of its norm on the cluster of small
eigenvalues (\cref{prop:newton-rhs-alignment,cor:top-window-solve}).  Hence,
for a fixed relative-residual tolerance \(\eta\), once \(\mu=O(\sqrt\eta)\),
a solve that inverts only the cluster of large eigenvalues meets this
tolerance at a cost independent of \(\kappa\).  A solver should therefore be
chosen by how the right-hand side couples to the small eigenvalues and how
its spectral mass is distributed, not by \(\kappa\) alone
(\cref{sec:beyond-kappa}).

Our third result is that the decisive lower bounds often concern loading and
recovery rather than the linear solve.  In the raw sparse coefficient model
(\cref{def:raw-sparse-access}), the positions of the nonzero coefficients are
public and each query reveals one hidden sign.  The LP families of
\cref{sec:lp-hardness} include ones whose reduced Newton matrices have
condition number one, yet the parity of the hidden signs determines either an affine offset
of the solution or the Newton direction in the
original coordinates, and preparing that direction as a quantum state needs
a number of queries linear in the problem size.  The SDP families of
\cref{sec:sdp-hardness} show that the optimal value, Schur-complement solves,
access to the KKT matrix, and recovery of the solution can have very
different query costs.  By the product bounds of
\cref{sec:preconditioning}, improving the spectral condition number can
worsen the normalization of the factor or the
sensitivity of recovering the physical state, and a state produced by one
solve need not provide the coordinate oracle needed by the next.

Condensation, the concentration of the trace of the central point on a
single block, has both a positive and a negative side.  Consider \(G\)
positive semidefinite blocks coupled by a single trace constraint, one
winning block and \(G-1\) identical losing blocks with fixed
dimension and spectral gaps, and the log-det central point at path
parameter \(\tau=\alpha/G\) (the \(\mu\) of \cref{def:central-path}).  As
\(G\to\infty\), the trace on the block whose
cost matrix has the smallest eigenvalue (the winner mass) tends to a positive
constant when \(\alpha\) is below a critical value \(\alpha_*\), and to zero
at and above \(\alpha_*\) (\cref{thm:condensation-threshold}).  The winner
mass controls at once the reduced-Hessian condition number, the trace
distance between the central state and a public reference state that does
not depend on the hidden input, and the query cost of preparing the state.
On a bounded-incidence holonomy SDP whose \(G\) groups each hide the parity
of \(N\) signs, the transition comes with matching query bounds; for
example, estimating the optimal value costs
\(\Theta(N\sqrt G)\) quantum queries.  For preparing a central state with
\(k\) winners to trace distance \(\varepsilon\), we prove query bounds, as
functions of \(\varepsilon\), that match up to logarithmic factors on the
domain of \cref{cor:sc-s2}; the range where the margin
\(\delta=p-\varepsilon\) between the winner mass \(p\) and the allowed error
is \(o(k/G)\), and the effect of a discrepancy \(\eta_{\rm int}\) between
internal states of losing blocks, remain open
(\cref{sec:state-conversion-open}).  Output hardness is thus proved for an
explicit output requirement, through a public measurement that decodes the
hidden information, not inferred from the
condition number.

Finally, our impossibility results cover stated classes of constructions and
methods; they are not universal.  Convex mixtures, cut and resistance bounds,
bounded-arity Walsh rows, symmetry work identities, scalar congruences, and
products for state interfaces rule out the hardness constructions (gadgets)
that satisfy their hypotheses, and \cref{sec:frontier-synthesis} lists five
ways to escape them.  The positive
modules of \cref{sec:positive-modules} take some of these routes, but they
are conditional and apply per step: they need stable geometry, explicit
access, compressed output, or a low-dimensional border.  No section combines
them into an end-to-end sparse-QIPM advantage.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:introduction-contributions}

The technical contributions follow the order of the paper.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
 \item \textbf{Conditioning geometry for every barrier (\cref{sec:conditioning}).}
 For every self-concordant barrier, we prove the lower bound
 \(\lambda_{\min}\geq1/\ell(x)^2\) on the reduced Hessian at every interior
 point \(x\), where \(\ell(x)\) is the length of the longest segment from
 \(x\) within its sublevel set (\cref{thm:lambda-min-rigidity}), and the
 sublevel chord law (\cref{thm:sublevel-chord-law}).  We refine them to
 two-sided bounds on every eigenvalue by directional widths of the sublevel
 set (\cref{thm:width-spectrum-rigidity}), and show that the canonical LP and
 product-SDP barriers attain the resulting lower bound up to an explicit
 factor at equal objective gap (\cref{thm:canonical-achievability}).
 Corollaries give the rates for
 positive-dimensional optimal faces, degenerate vertices, curved optima, SDPs
 with a given singularity degree, near-degenerate instances, and
 approximately central points
 (\cref{cor:conditioning-regimes,cor:fractional-conditioning-laws,cor:near-degeneracy-plateau,cor:approximate-centrality-transfer}).

 \item \textbf{Clustered spectra and the separation of solvers
 (\cref{sec:spectra}).}  \Cref{sec:spectra} proves the results on clustered
 spectra, solver cost, and central-path right-hand sides described in
 \cref{sec:introduction-answer}
 (\cref{prop:two-cluster-hessian,prop:two-cluster-cg,thm:quantum-cluster-obstruction,prop:newton-rhs-alignment,cor:top-window-solve}).

 \item \textbf{Solver choice beyond \(\kappa\)
 (\cref{sec:beyond-kappa}).}  For norm-tight encodings, exact block
 inversion at central points shows that DLS filtering improves on the
 worst-case condition number if and only if a limiting coupling vector (up
 to sign, the limit of the Newton solution's component on the
 small-eigenvalue subspace, divided by \(\mu\)) vanishes
 (\cref{thm:dls-coupling}).  A truncation transition separates requirements on
 the solution state from requirements on the residual
 (\cref{prop:dls-truncation-transition}).  If the spectral measure of the
 right-hand side has effective dimension \(D\), meaning that its mass below
 \(t\) is of order \(t^{D/2}\), then fixed-accuracy truncation becomes
 independent of the condition number for \(D>4\), and filtering for \(D>8\)
 (\cref{thm:spectral-dimension-laws}).  Given the orthogonal projector \(Q\)
 onto the active range, a stable equilibration removes the divergent part of
 the spectrum and exactly preserves the normalized Newton state for every
 \(f\in\operatorname{range}Q\) (\cref{thm:stable-split-equilibration}).

 \item \textbf{Block log-det condensation
 (\cref{sec:condensation}).}  We derive the exact transition and the
 conditioning phases, which depend on the multiplicity of the smallest
 eigenvalue of the winning block
 (\cref{thm:condensation-threshold,thm:cond-conditioning-phases}); prove
 bounds between winner mass and conditioning for finite \(G\)
 (\cref{thm:cond-mass-conditioning}); and relate the winner mass to the trace
 distance from the no-winner state and to raw-query complexity
 (\cref{thm:cond-visibility,thm:cond-unique-or}).  The transition extends to
 sublinearly many winners and to a stated model of approximate centrality
 (\cref{thm:cond-multiwinner,thm:cond-robust}).  These statements concern the
 Frobenius-metric Hessian with equality constraints eliminated and the
 trace-normalized output, not every preconditioned KKT formulation.

 \item \textbf{A sparse winner-take-all SDP
 (\cref{sec:winner-take-all}).}  A bounded-incidence family of
 qutrit-holonomy SDPs realizes the condensation law, with optimal-value
 estimation costing \(\Theta(N\sqrt G)\) quantum and \(\Theta(NG)\)
 randomized queries and a public starting point where the Hessian in root
 coordinates has condition number one.  A state within trace distance
 \(1/100\) of an approximate optimizer reveals a winning group if one
 exists, so preparing it needs \(\Omega(N\sqrt G)\) queries
 (\cref{thm:wta-robust-optimizer}); a search-then-solve algorithm with compact
 output matches this bound (\cref{thm:wta-search-upper}).
 \Cref{sec:wta-scope} explains why bounded incidence and a condition-one
 ambient formulation are not claimed together.

 \item \textbf{Accuracy curves for hidden-block state conversion
 (\cref{sec:state-conversion}).}  A decoder that verifies sampled labels
 gives a lower bound for mixed-state outputs (\cref{thm:sc-m1}); detuned
 rejection preparation gives the upper bound (\cref{thm:sc-m2}); and parity
 collapse yields the tight curve for preparing, to trace distance
 \(\varepsilon\), a state with winner mass \(p\) on \(k\) of \(G\) blocks
 (\cref{thm:sc-small-success,cor:sc-s2}).  For a batch of uncoupled
 instances, vector output cannot be amortized, whereas a single averaged
 scalar has the usual approximate-counting cost
 (\cref{thm:sc-batch-vector,thm:sc-batch-scalar}).  The discrepancy between
 internal loser states, the very-small-success range, and logarithmic
 fixed-point costs remain explicit limits of these theorems.

 \item \textbf{LP loading and recovery hardness
 (\cref{sec:lp-hardness}).}  The gain--plateau family gives a linear lower
 bound for preparing any primal state with small residual
 (\cref{thm:lp-gain-plateau}).  Prefix rigidification strengthens this to an
 LP whose reduced Newton geometry has condition number one while recovering
 the original coordinates costs linearly many queries
 (\cref{thm:lp-prefix-capstone}).  Variants with complete KKT systems and
 Schur-complement preconditioning show the tradeoffs among a public
 right-hand side, residual accuracy, bounded data, and scale
 (\cref{thm:lp-full-kkt,thm:lp-preconditioned-kkt,thm:lp-bounded-exact,thm:lp-unit-threshold,thm:lp-dual-homogeneous-full}).
 None of them is a lower bound for a condition-one QLS problem with free
 access to transformed oracles.

 \item \textbf{Limits of classes of gadgets
 (\cref{sec:gadget-frontiers}).}  A convex-mixture theorem for single-bit
 flips gives the default obstruction without any graph structure
 (\cref{thm:frontier-single-flip}).  Cut--resistance and Walsh-row theorems
 give the size or scale that signed-copy and bounded-arity constructions
 need (\cref{thm:frontier-cut-resistance,thm:frontier-walsh}); gauge-flow,
 work, and Fourier identities show how the cost appears in multiplier size
 or input incidence
 (\cref{thm:frontier-gauge-flow,thm:frontier-work,thm:frontier-fourier-source}).
 The hypotheses that a construction must drop to escape these theorems,
 listed in \cref{sec:frontier-synthesis}, are part of the result.

 \item \textbf{SDP-native loading and recovery hardness
 (\cref{sec:sdp-hardness}).}  An intrinsic trace-normalized qutrit family has
 an optimal value that reveals a parity, and a public reduced Newton step of
 condition number one whose primal and complete-KKT solution states are
 parity hard (\cref{thm:sdp-intrinsic,thm:sdp-newton-state}).  Schur-complement
 families that are not SOCP-representable give an exact tradeoff between
 loading and accuracy (\cref{thm:sdp-schur,thm:sdp-accuracy-frontier}), and
 tree and group holonomy extend the construction to matrix-valued words
 (\cref{thm:sdp-tree-loading,thm:sdp-holonomy-spectrum,thm:sdp-s3-hardness}).
 The query lower bound for the \(S_3\) family is still proved on an abelian
 restriction; hardness for intrinsically nonabelian words remains open.

 \item \textbf{Preconditioning and state interfaces
 (\cref{sec:preconditioning}).}  Whitening and reusable inverse access trade
 condition number against normalization
 (\cref{thm:prec-whitening-frontier,thm:prec-inverse-access}); scalar
 congruence trades the transformed condition number against the cost of
 recovering the physical state
 (\cref{thm:prec-congruence-frontier,thm:prec-general-scalar-frontier}); and for
 an explicit parity-hard Newton update, the queries for setup, RHS
 preparation, solve, and recovery together are at least linear in the problem
 size for every invertible preconditioner
 (\cref{thm:prec-factor-rhs-hardness,thm:prec-parity-ledger}).  The
 state-to-next-solve and lazy-oracle theorems distinguish a state that can be
 used once from a reusable coordinate oracle
 (\cref{thm:prec-state-diagonal,thm:prec-state-next-solve,thm:prec-lazy-lookup,thm:prec-reusable-endpoints}).

 \item \textbf{Conditional positive modules
 (\cref{sec:positive-modules}).}  We prove a bound on the number of fresh
 solves when a Newton direction is reused along the path until a residual
 check fails (\cref{thm:positive-lazy-refresh}); a constructive stable
 equilibration of the active range and an exact face repair
 (\cref{thm:positive-partition-free-equilibration,thm:positive-face-repair});
 an implicit slack oracle and a conditional dual QIPM with compressed output
 (\cref{thm:positive-implicit-slack,thm:positive-compressed-qipm}); and a
 solver for block-angular systems through their border, whose \(\sqrt N\)
 dependence on the number of blocks is optimal in the block-oracle model
 (\cref{sec:positive-block-angular}).

 \item \textbf{Reusable structural tools
 (\cref{sec:structural-tools}).}  Work identities for involutions and
 compact groups (\cref{thm:structural-involution-work,thm:structural-group-work}),
 a noncommutative sandwich for mixtures of central points
 (\cref{thm:structural-sdp-kantorovich}), and lower bounds on the rank and
 support of Newton steps
 (\cref{thm:structural-rank-progress,thm:structural-support-barrier}) explain
 several of the obstructions abstractly.  Sparse OSS and treewidth-two KKT
 embeddings carry known QLS lower bounds over to these formulations
 (\cref{thm:structural-oss-embedding,thm:structural-treewidth-two}), while
 value hardness at exact treewidth and a light-cone bound for central oracles
 separate query, output, and physical-locality models
 (\cref{thm:structural-exact-treewidth,thm:structural-light-cone}).

 \item \textbf{Corrections and dead ends (\cref{sec:corrections}).}  We show
 that the global potential supremum in the QCPM simulator-norm analysis
 cannot scale as asserted (\cref{prop:corr-qcpm-supremum}), correct the
 separate clock (time-dilation) error (the correct clock is
 \cref{eq:corr-qcpm-clock}), derive the
 corrected norm scale and its tradeoff with speed
 (\cref{thm:corr-qcpm-norm,cor:corr-qcpm-tradeoff}), and give explicit
 counterexamples for densification and hidden starting points
 (\cref{thm:corr-path-simplex,thm:corr-hidden-sign}).  The recorded dead ends
 show why norms of the first inverse, sparse metrics, exact Schur products, or
 one-step bounds cannot be reused as claims they do not prove.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Related work and positioning}
\label{sec:introduction-related-work}

\paragraph{Quantum interior-point methods.}
We do not put the QIPMs of \cref{sec:introduction-state-of-play} into a
single complexity table
\cite{kerenidis2020-quantum-interior-point-method-lps,
casares2020-quantum-interior-point-predictor-corrector,
augustino2023-quantum-interior-point-methods-semidefinite,
mohammadisiahroudi2025-inexact-feasible-interior-point-method,
wu2023-inexact-feasible-quantum-interior-point,
apers2026-quantum-speedups-linear-programming-interior,
dalzell2025-quantum-interior-point-methods,
augustino2024-quantum-central-path-algorithm-linear,
gribling2025-self-concordant-schrodinger-operators-spectral}, because each
uses a different access model, condition parameter, precision, and output
requirement.  Each technical section compares its theorems with the nearest
prior work.

\paragraph{Quantum optimization lower bounds.}
Van Apeldoorn et al. established general quantum SDP upper and lower bounds
and constructions that embed a Boolean function in the optimum;
Brand\~ao and Svore initiated the line of quantum SDP solvers; and
Theorem~8.4 of Apers and Gribling gives a value lower bound for sparse LPs
that is already linear for balanced dimensions
\cite{apeldoorn2020-quantum-sdp-solvers,
fernando2017-quantum-speed-ups-solving-semidefinite,
apers2026-quantum-speedups-linear-programming-interior}.  We do not claim
parity hardness, adversary composition, or the linear exponent as new for our
parity and holonomy families.  What is new is their combination with exact
central paths, formulations of condition number one or bounded condition
number, robust output requirements, and complete accounting of loading and
recovery (\cref{sec:winner-take-all,sec:lp-hardness,sec:sdp-hardness}).

\paragraph{Classical interior-point structure.}
Wright's work on logarithmic-barrier Hessians and numerical error, the
standard primal--dual theory, and the survey of Forsgren, Gill, and Wright
provide the background on conditioning and implementation
\cite{wright1994-properties-hessian-logarithmic-barrier,
wright1998-ill-conditioning-computational-error-interior,
wright1997-primal-dual-interior-point-methods,
forsgren2002-interior-methods-nonlinear-optimization}.  Gondzio and
collaborators document preconditioning, the use of structure, and the
evolution of practical IPMs
\cite{jacek2012-interior-point-methods-25-years,
gondzio2023-general-purpose-preconditioning-regularized-interior-point}.
The sublevel chord law differs from
classical conditioning analyses of the log barrier in holding for all
self-concordant barriers, and \cref{sec:spectra} uses, rather than ignores,
the classical advantage of Krylov methods on clustered spectra.

\paragraph{Established ingredients.}
Many ingredients used below are established: self-concordant containment,
weak-sharp and singularity-degree error bounds, matrix-Burg resolvents,
Rayleigh--Jeans condensation, parity and search, group synchronization,
Kantorovich inequalities, effective resistance, and sparse elimination.  We
credit each where it enters.  The detailed statements of novelty and scope in
\cref{sec:conditioning-overview,sec:condensation-discussion,sec:wta-scope,sec:sdp-synthesis,sec:preconditioning-synthesis,sec:positive-synthesis,sec:structural-synthesis}
are part of the claims.

\subsection{Conventions and corrections}
\label{sec:introduction-verification}

The distinctions of \cref{sec:preliminaries} hold throughout: input sparsity
differs from Newton-system sparsity, \(\kappa_H\) from the block-encoding
normalization, reduced from ambient KKT conditioning, and value,
classical-vector, quantum-state, coordinate-oracle, and heralded outputs are
different output requirements.  Query bounds count the setup and
transformations named by their access model; a stronger oracle supplied at
no cost defines a stronger problem, not an implementation of raw sparse
access.

Several results correct earlier versions of our own analysis and two
independent errors in the QCPM preprint.  The corrections that matter for
reading the paper are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
 \item \Cref{sec:corrections-qcpm} corrects the simulator-norm estimate of
 QCPM v2, because a global supremum on a fixed truncation box is not
 controlled by concentration near the current center, and its separate clock
 error, because the time-varying kinetic coefficient requires a corrected
 clock.  This corrects that derivation; it is not an impossibility result
 for Hamiltonian optimization.
 \item \Cref{sec:spectra-quantum} corrects an overbroad conclusion about
 factor access in our earlier analysis: a square-root polynomial cost, or a
 two-stage solve with explicit overlap, does not imply a generic
 \(\Thetatilde(\sqrt\kappa)\) QLS solver with factor access, as the
 Orsucci--Dunjko instance shows.
 \item \Cref{sec:spectra-two-cluster} replaces a coarse three-scale
 description of the degree-two SDP witness by its four-window spectrum; this
 changes the intermediate spectral description, not the exponent of the
 condition number.
 \item \Cref{sec:effective-spectral-dimension} anchors the effective spectral
 dimension at the smallest eigenvalue that the right-hand side sees,
 repairing an earlier formulation by spectral shells; the phase laws of
 \cref{thm:spectral-dimension-laws} need this.
 \item
 \Cref{sec:state-conversion-model,sec:state-conversion-small-success,sec:state-conversion-open}
 replace the collision shortcut by a decoder that verifies sampled labels,
 restrict the classical law that is linear in the success probability to its
 actual domain, leave the very-small-success range open, and attribute the
 small-success search bound with \(k\) marked items to Dohotaru--H\o yer
 rather than to Zalka's theorem for one marked item alone.
 \item \Cref{sec:positive-face-repair} corrects the earlier claim that two
 convergent leakage sums alone bound the projective variation: a
 counterexample with an oscillating face displacement has convergent sums,
 satisfies an additional $O(\mu_t)$ tail bound, and has linearly growing
 realized variation.  Finite realized variation therefore remains a separate
 hypothesis.
\end{enumerate}
The list includes only changes that affect theorem statements or their
interpretation; other rejected shortcuts and their valid replacements are in
\cref{sec:corrections-dead-ends}.
