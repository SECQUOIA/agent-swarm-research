\begin{abstract}
When $H$ is the Hessian and $b$ the gradient of a barrier function,
$b^*H^{-1}b$ is the squared Newton decrement. The cost of estimating this
number can differ from that of preparing or writing out the Newton
direction. We study the query complexity of estimating it to relative
error $\epsilon$ when
$\kappa^{-1}I\preceq H\preceq I$, given sparse access to $H$, with at
most $d$ nonzeros per row, and sampling-and-query access to $b$. A
classical estimator that explores neighborhoods of sampled coordinates
uses
$\kappa\epsilon^{-2}(d+1)^{O(\sqrt\kappa\log(2/\epsilon))}$ queries at
constant confidence. Separate sparse families show that the
statistical factor $\kappa\epsilon^{-2}$ and the exponential factor are
each necessary, even with full sampling access to the matrix. An explicit
composition gives single instances that require both factors at once in
some parameter ranges, but it does not force the product of the two full
factors. In the
quantum model where $H/\alpha$ is a block of a unitary, a lower bound for
$3\times3$ matrices matches, up to logarithmic factors, the known
variable-time upper bound $\widetilde O(\alpha\kappa/\epsilon)$. We
construct sparse LPs and SOCPs whose optimal values, optimizer
coordinates, or Newton decrements are classically hard to estimate,
and every query to the full sampling-and-query interface of the
formulation can be simulated with $O(1)$ queries to the hidden input. We also show that this cost adds up
when the requested outputs (several path scalars or one endpoint
coordinate) encode independent instances, and we use examples and reuse
bounds to explain why one-shot hardness cannot simply be multiplied by
an iteration count. For Newton matrices $N$ formed by a well-conditioned sparse
matrix plus a sampled low-rank correction, including Lorentz and
generalized-power cone systems under stated assumptions, we give
classical algorithms that estimate $b^*N^{-1}b$ and sample from an
approximate solution of $Nx=b$. All results are query and arithmetic
bounds; they do not establish a quantum speedup
for interior-point methods.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
An interior-point method may need only one number before it decides how
to move: the squared Newton decrement $b^*H^{-1}b$, where $H$ and $b$
are the Hessian and gradient of the barrier subproblem at the current
point. It measures local centrality and Newton progress, but by itself
it does not certify global optimality. A linear-system solver may instead
return the Newton direction as a vector, as samples from its squared
coordinates, or as a normalized quantum state. A normalized state loses
the scale that a scalar estimate needs, and writing out a whole vector
can cost much more than estimating one number derived from it. This
matters when assessing quantum speedups of interior-point methods, which
must also read the data, choose steps, and report an answer.

We study the complexity of this scalar and when it carries over to
outputs of an optimization problem. The central problem is to estimate
\[
 q(H,b)=b^*H^{-1}b,\qquad \kappa^{-1}I\preceq H\preceq I,
\]
to relative error $\epsilon$. Classical algorithms get sparse row
access to $H$, with at most $d$ nonzeros per row, and sampling-and-query
access $\SQ(b)$: coordinate values, the norm $\norm b$, and samples of
an index $i$ with probability proportional to $|b_i|^2$. Our classical
lower bounds hold even with full matrix $\SQ$ access, which adds the row
and column norms of $H$ and the associated samples. Quantum algorithms
get either coherent access to the entries of $H$ or block access, a
unitary whose top-left block is $H/\alpha$. These two models differ even
for diagonal $3\times3$ matrices: our block-access lower bound uses such
matrices, and one exact entry query solves the same instances.

\subsection{Results and contribution}
Table~\ref{tab:rates} summarizes the bounds for a single scalar.

\paragraph{Upper and lower bounds for the scalar.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:classical-scalar} estimates $q$ with
$O(\kappa\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\zeta)(d+1)^{O(\sqrt\kappa\log(2/\epsilon))})$
queries and arithmetic operations at failure probability $\zeta$. It
combines known ingredients: a Chebyshev residual polynomial, local sparse
evaluation, and importance sampling. Because the form is positive, the
Kantorovich inequality bounds the relative second moment of the estimator
by $O(\kappa)$, so the statistical factor is $\kappa\epsilon^{-2}$, not
quadratic in $\kappa$. Theorem~\ref{thm:clock-lower} carries the
matrix-function lower bound of Montanaro--Shao over to positive definite
forms of row sparsity $s\geq5$: estimating two such forms with public
right-hand sides (known without queries) requires
$((s-1)/2)^{\Omega(\sqrt\kappa\log(1/\epsilon))}$ classical queries, up
to a polynomial denominator; the family is realized at the analytic center of a sparse box LP.
Theorem~\ref{thm:statistical-lower} gives a two-sparse family of
dimension $2M$ with the sharp lower bound
$\Omega(\min\{M,\kappa\epsilon^{-2}\})$, or
$\Omega(\kappa\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\zeta))$ at failure probability
$\zeta$ when $M$ is large enough.

Theorem~\ref{thm:distributional-product} shows when both kinds of cost
fall on one form: suppose a promise problem $g$ with randomized query
complexity $R(g)$ is encoded, under the access and promise conditions of the theorem, as
a form equal to a public positive baseline plus $C\Phi$, with
signal-to-baseline ratio $\chi$ ($C$ divided by the baseline) at most $5/3$. Then, for $0<\epsilon\leq\chi/200$, estimating the
average of such forms over many independent blocks to relative error
$\epsilon$ needs $\Omega((\chi/\epsilon)^2R(g))$ classical full-$\SQ$
queries. Relative error is measured against the whole value, so we
compute the baseline exactly for an explicit witness clock
(Proposition~\ref{prop:witness-diagonal}), a sparse matrix that applies
circuit gates along a path of time steps. This gives, for example, a
single-form lower bound $\epsilon^{-2}s^{\Omega(\sqrt\kappa)}$, up to
polynomial denominators, and a bound with a factor $\kappa^2$ in a
restricted high-accuracy regime (Theorem~\ref{thm:path-product}), but
not the product of the two full lower bounds. We also prove limits for
specific constructions. In the cyclic construction, the contrast (the
ratio of the two possible values of the form) is at most $(1+K/L)^2$,
where $K^2$ bounds the condition number, so contrast of the order of the condition number requires a bounded
clock length $L$. The two-cluster construction gives only constant
contrast. In a Schur-complement construction, a large form value
raises the condition number.

For plain block access with normalization $\alpha$,
Theorem~\ref{thm:coherent} matches the known variable-time negative-power
norm algorithm with an $\Omega(\alpha\kappa/\epsilon)$ lower bound on
block queries; its explicit unitary completion shows why this bound does
not transfer to exact entry queries. For coherent sparse access, the
proved bounds are $\Omega(\sqrt\kappa/\epsilon)$ and
$\widetilde O(d\kappa/\epsilon)$.

\paragraph{Optimization outputs and repeated queries.}
Section~\ref{sec:realizations} constructs sparse LPs and SOCPs whose
optimizer coordinate, optimal value, or Newton decrement encodes the
amplitude $\Phi$ of a Forrelation circuit. The underlying cyclic clock
matrix runs the circuit forward, holds, and undoes it. This matrix and
its Newton Hessian have public row and column norms, so full $\SQ$ access
to the formulation can be simulated; its inverse applied to the start
vector has an exactly known norm, and the clock parameters trade off
conditioning, sparsity, and accuracy. For the optimizer coordinate, classical full-$\SQ$
algorithms need $\Omega(2^{n/2}/n)$ queries, where $n$ is the number of
work qubits of the Forrelation circuit, while a quantum algorithm
with the corresponding coherent input oracle needs $O(1)$ queries to the
hidden Forrelation sign tables
(Theorem~\ref{thm:scalar-coordinate}). Scaling an objective tilt by the exact
public norm of an inverse-transpose vector, rather than by an upper bound
on it, enlarges the hidden signal in the optimal value
(Theorem~\ref{thm:tilted-value}). For an LP on a one-dimensional affine
slice, whose barrier has parameter one, the classical upper and lower
bounds for the optimal value have the same exponential dependence in the
accuracy regime of Theorem~\ref{thm:affine-lp}; the reduced objective is not assumed
given, since eliminating the equality constraints requires hidden
information.

Section~\ref{sec:temporal} encodes $B$ independent instances in the
changes of one coordinate between checkpoints on an LP central path, in
the predictor decrements of an ordinary SOCP, and, through their parity,
in one bounded optimizer coordinate. Because these outputs contain
information about all $B$ instances, returning them requires
$\Omega(BL_g)$ classical full-$\SQ$ queries, where $L_g=R(g)$
(Theorems~\ref{thm:lp-trajectory}, \ref{thm:socp-trajectory},
and~\ref{thm:threshold-xor}). Conversely, in Section~\ref{sec:reuse} a
sparse KKT system has the same hard normalized solution along the whole
central path, although reaching a small objective gap takes many bounded
local steps; learning the hidden input once suffices. Reusing the span of stored
solutions, and solving the system anew only when the smallest current
relative residual over that span fails a test, requires at most $r$ such
refresh solves if the solution directions span a space of
dimension $r$ and computations are exact (Proposition~\ref{prop:exact-reuse}),
and at most $2r-1$ if they lie close enough to one and the stated error
bounds hold (Theorem~\ref{thm:robust-reuse}); residual tests and
explicit outputs are counted separately.

\paragraph{Structured Newton systems.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:structured-sq} handles
$N=S+CXLX^TC^T$, where $S=CC^T$ is a well-conditioned sparse full-rank
base and $CXLX^TC^T$ is a low-rank correction whose symmetric core $L$
may be singular or indefinite.
Each column of $X$ is a known sparse contraction of a unit source vector
with $\SQ$ access, and no nearly zero column of $CX$ is normalized. The
algorithm builds one fixed approximate solution with a reusable setup,
and each draw returns a sample or a failure flag at bounded cost;
Corollary~\ref{cor:structured-scalar} gives relative estimation of the
inverse form. Lorentz and generalized-power barriers have this structure
under explicit bounds on eccentricity and base conditioning, when certain
scalars of each cone block are supplied; for the power cone, we prove a
query lower bound for computing one of them, a weighted geometric mean,
from vector access alone. For a full classical Newton direction, we give
separate solve bounds using eccentricity profiles (how many cone blocks
have large eccentricity) and latent sparsity (a supplied tree
decomposition of small width).

To our knowledge, the following are new: the full-$\SQ$ lower bounds for
positive forms, the single-form composition and baseline calculations,
the parameterized scalar realizations in optimization problems and along
paths with the examples that limit reuse, and the theorem for a sparse
full-rank base with low-rank corrections under source-vector access. We
claim no priority for inverse-form estimation, sparse polynomial
dequantization, Forrelation matrix constructions, hardness of scalar
optimization outputs, low-rank cone algebra, or subspace recycling.

\subsection{Relationship to prior work}
Li--Sra--Jegelka develop Gaussian quadrature for matrix inverse forms
\citep{LiSraJegelka2016}, and Bizas--Mitrouli--Turek give further analytic
and heuristic estimates \citep{BizasMitrouliTurek2025}; these methods
count matrix--vector products. Our classical model instead explores
neighborhoods of sampled coordinates, trading dependence on dimension for
exponential dependence on the polynomial degree.

Gharibian--Le Gall already give sparse polynomial overlap estimation from
sampling access \citep[Theorem 4.1]{GharibianLeGall2022}.
Montanaro--Shao relate the query complexity of matrix functions to
approximation degree and construct the weighted Forrelation clocks used
here \citep[Theorem 1.8]{MontanaroShao2023}. Cifuentes et al.\ classify
matrix-function tasks under several access and measurement models
\citep{CifuentesEtAl2024}, and Edenhofer--Hasegawa--Le Gall apply sparse
polynomial evaluation to spectral sums, including reciprocal traces
\citep[Theorem 3.1 and Section 3.3]{EdenhoferEtAl2026}. Our target is a positive inverse form
with an arbitrary vector and relative error, which requires controlling
the baseline and the variance; the polynomial method and its origin in
approximation theory come from this prior work. Le Gall's robustness results already cover
approximate length-squared sampling for sparse and low-rank
dequantization \citep{LeGall2025}; our finite-error analysis, for
approximate samples and estimator values, is only a sufficient condition
for our estimator, not a general robustness framework.

Block encodings and polynomial transformations are established quantum
tools \citep{GilyenEtAl2019}, and our block-access upper
bound specializes the norm estimator of Chakraborty--Gily\'en--Jeffery
\citep[Theorem 33 in the full version]{ChakrabortyGilyenJeffery2019}.
Alase et al.\ study scalar observables of linear-system solutions
\citep{AlaseEtAl2022}; their tight bound counts queries to a block of
the observable for additive error in an expectation value, while ours
counts queries to the system matrix for relative estimation of
$b^*H^{-1}b$. Orsucci--Dunjko study preparation of solution states for
positive definite systems, and stronger access structures that can
improve the dependence on conditioning \citep{OrsucciDunjko2021};
Section~\ref{sec:coherent} compares these different access models with
ours.

Gr\o nlund--Larsen prove an exponential separation between quantum
algorithms and $\SQ$ access for outputting linear-system solutions
\citep{GronlundLarsen2025}, and Apers--Gribling already prove full-$\SQ$
hardness for the optimal value of an ordinary LP
\citep[Lemma 8.5]{ApersGribling2026}. Our optimization constructions instead
add explicit control of condition number, sparsity, and accuracy, a
normalization of the hard scalar, realizations as actual Newton
quantities, and an account of the cost of acquiring the input. Zhao et al.\ study normalized solution
observables and Forrelation embeddings under restrictions on streaming
space and data samples \citep[Task F.1 and Sections E.5--F]{ZhaoEtAl2026};
here the input is static and classical storage is unrestricted. General
direct-sum, composition, and XOR results are also prior work
\citep{BlaisBrody2019,BenDavidBlais2020,ChakrabortyEtAl2023,BrodyEtAl2023};
we realize them in the stated optimization outputs and prove that every
access operation can be simulated.

Low-rank $\SQ$ matrix arithmetic and solution sampling are established
\citep{ChiaEtAl2022}; our structured theorem instead has a sparse
full-rank base with a low-rank correction, and does not require normalized
transformed columns as input. Hessian identities for cones and their
sparse implementations are likewise established
\citep{AlizadehGoldfarb2003,GoldfarbScheinberg2005,
RoyXiao2022,ChenGoulart2025,KapelevichAndersenVielma2024};
we prove the spectral and sampling conditions needed to use it with
local queries. Krylov recycling and greedy reduced
bases \citep{ParksEtAl2006,BinevEtAl2011}, and sparse and low-treewidth
elimination \citep{Vanderbei1995,FurerHoppenTrevisan2025}, are the
classical background for our results on reuse and on full output.

\subsection{Scope and organization}
Section~\ref{sec:models} states all oracle and ideal-arithmetic
assumptions. Sections~\ref{sec:classical}--\ref{sec:composition} give
classical algorithms, lower-bound families, and composition;
Section~\ref{sec:coherent} treats coherent access;
Sections~\ref{sec:realizations}--\ref{sec:reuse} treat optimization
outputs, composition over a path, and reuse; and
Section~\ref{sec:structured} treats structured cone systems.

The results are query and arithmetic bounds; they do not establish a quantum speedup for general interior-point
methods, a uniform bound on bit complexity, or that sampling oracles,
which change with the iterate, can be maintained at no cost. The general
classical upper and lower bounds do not match in their joint dependence
on all parameters, nor do the bounds for coherent sparse access. None of the optimization or structured results depends on
closing these gaps.
