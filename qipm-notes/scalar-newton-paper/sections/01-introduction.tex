\begin{abstract}
The positive inverse quadratic form $b^*H^{-1}b$ is the squared Newton
decrement when $H$ and $b$ are a Hessian and gradient. Its query complexity
need not coincide with the cost of preparing or writing a Newton direction.
We study relative estimation under sparse and sampling-and-query access,
and compare it with coherent block access. For
$\kappa^{-1}I\preceq H\preceq I$ and row sparsity $d$, a classical local
estimator uses
$\kappa\epsilon^{-2}(d+1)^{O(\sqrt\kappa\log(2/\epsilon))}$ queries at
constant confidence. Full-sampling lower families separately force the
statistical factor and the exponential scale. Explicit clock diagonals and
statistical composition give simultaneous lower bounds in stated regimes,
without establishing the product of both full factors. Under plain block
access of normalization $\alpha$, a three-dimensional lower bound matches, up to logarithmic factors,
the known variable-time upper bound $\widetilde O(\alpha\kappa/\epsilon)$.
We realize hard scalars as LP and SOCP values, optimizer coordinates, and
Newton decrements, preserving the promised input interfaces. We also give
scalar trajectory and endpoint constructions, together with examples and
residual-certified reuse bounds explaining why one-shot hardness cannot
simply be multiplied by an iteration count. Finally, a sparse-base solver
with sampled low-rank corrections yields scalar estimates and solution
samples for structured Lorentz and generalized-power Newton systems under
explicit spectral and data-access assumptions. All bounds distinguish
query cost, arithmetic, input acquisition, and the requested output.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
A Newton method may need only one number before it decides how to move:
the squared decrement $b^*H^{-1}b$. It measures local centrality and
Newton progress under the specified objective and barrier; by itself it
is not a general certificate of global optimality. A linear-system routine
may return a vector, a sample from its squared coordinates, or a normalized
quantum state. These outputs carry different information. Normalization
can discard the scale needed by a scalar estimate, while writing a whole
vector can cost much more than estimating one of its observables.
The distinction matters when assessing quantum improvements to
interior-point methods, whose work includes acquiring iterate data,
solving Newton systems, choosing steps, and reporting a final answer.

This paper studies the scalar itself and then asks when its complexity
survives an optimization formulation. Our central problem is relative
estimation of
\[
 q(H,b)=b^*H^{-1}b,\qquad \kappa^{-1}I\preceq H\preceq I.
\]
Classical algorithms receive local sparse matrix access and
sampling-and-query access to $b$: coordinate values, its norm, and samples
proportional to squared coordinate magnitudes. Lower bounds remain valid
when the matrix also supplies exact row and column norms and the associated
sampling operations. Quantum comparisons separately specify coherent
entry access or a normalized matrix block inside a unitary. The distinction
between these two quantum interfaces is necessary even for diagonal
matrices of dimension three.

\subsection{Results and contribution}
The results have three connected parts. First, we identify the relative
positive-form bounds justified by these interfaces. Second, we construct
optimization outputs that retain hardness and examine what information
can be reused across a path. Third, we give structured classical solves
that delimit the scope of the hard constructions. Table~\ref{tab:rates}
summarizes the central one-scalar rates; later sections state the
optimization and structured-system contracts separately.

\paragraph{Relative positive forms and simultaneous lower bounds.}
A Chebyshev residual polynomial, local sparse evaluation, and coordinate
importance sampling give Theorem~\ref{thm:classical-scalar}. Positivity
allows the Kantorovich inequality to bound the estimator's relative second
moment by $O(\kappa)$, yielding the statistical factor
$\kappa\epsilon^{-2}$ rather than a bound quadratic in $\kappa$.
These are established ingredients; the result makes their relative-error
and access implications explicit.
Theorem~\ref{thm:clock-lower} transfers the matrix-function hardness of
Montanaro--Shao to real positive definite forms, verifies every full-SQ
operation, and realizes the result at an LP analytic center.
Theorem~\ref{thm:statistical-lower} supplies an independent two-sparse
family with sharp statistical and confidence dependence, including the
finite-population cutoff.

Separate lower families do not justify multiplying their costs.
Section~\ref{sec:composition} proves a distributional composition theorem
for a single positive form and calculates the inverse diagonals of an
explicit witness clock. The diagonal determines the baseline against
which relative error measures a hidden signal. This yields, for example,
a simultaneous $\epsilon^{-2}s^{\Omega(\sqrt\kappa)}$ lower bound up to
polynomial denominators, and a different $\kappa^2$ product in a restricted
high-accuracy regime. We also prove contrast limitations for specified
cyclic, two-cluster, and Schur-complement constructions. These calculations
identify what the constructions establish and which stronger products
they do not establish.

For plain coherent block access, Theorem~\ref{thm:coherent} combines the
known variable-time inverse-power norm algorithm with a matching
$\Omega(\alpha\kappa/\epsilon)$ block-query lower bound. Its explicit
completion also explains why the lower bound does not transfer to exact
entry queries. For general coherent sparse access, the proved lower and
upper bounds remain $\Omega(\sqrt\kappa/\epsilon)$ and
$\widetilde O(d\kappa/\epsilon)$, with state preparation charged separately.

\paragraph{Optimization outputs and repeated queries.}
Section~\ref{sec:realizations} constructs LPs and SOCPs with hard optimizer
coordinates, ordinary values, or Newton decrements. An exact cyclic
inverse supplies public norms and a tunable conditioning--sparsity--accuracy
relation. A public inverse-transpose norm gives a larger value signal;
an affine-slice LP attains a matching classical upper and lower exponential
dependence under its stated value-accuracy contract. The barrier parameter
of that slice is one, but eliminating its equalities requires acquiring
the hidden information. No free reduced objective is assumed.

Section~\ref{sec:temporal} arranges independent inputs in successive scalar
increments and ordinary SOCP predictor decrements. It also compiles
promised overlaps into one bounded optimizer coordinate. The resulting
direct-sum and endpoint bounds charge the information requested by those
outputs. Section~\ref{sec:reuse} gives the complementary boundary: a sparse
KKT system can have the same hard normalized solution at every central
multiplier, even while reaching a small objective gap requires many bounded
local movements. One acquisition can then serve every checkpoint.
Residual-certified span reuse gives exact and approximate refresh bounds,
with current residual tests and explicit-output costs charged separately.

\paragraph{Structured Newton systems.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:structured-sq} handles
$N=S+CXLX^TC^T$, where $S=CC^T$ is a well-conditioned sparse full-rank
base and $L$ is a small, possibly singular or indefinite correction.
Its inputs are samples from source vectors for the columns of $X$.
The algorithm never needs to normalize a nearly zero transformed column.
A sampled small system, local polynomial evaluation, and raw rejection
proposals produce one fixed approximate solution, with a reusable setup
and bounded-cost flagged draws. Corollary~\ref{cor:structured-scalar}
gives relative inverse-form estimation. Lorentz and generalized-power
barriers satisfy the required structure under explicit eccentricity,
sparse-base conditioning, and scalar-access promises. The need to acquire
the power-cone geometric mean is a real restriction, for which we prove a
query lower bound. Eccentricity-profile and supplied-treewidth solves give
separate comparisons when a full classical direction is required.

To the best of our knowledge, the additional contributions are the
full-SQ positive-form lower bounds, the explicit single-form composition
and baseline calculations,
the parameterized scalar optimization and temporal realizations with their
reuse boundaries, and the sparse-full-rank correction theorem under the
specified source-vector interface. This is a claim about these precise
statements. It is not a priority claim for inverse-form estimation,
sparse polynomial dequantization, Forrelation matrix constructions,
scalar optimization hardness, low-rank cone algebra, or subspace recycling.

\subsection{Relationship to prior work}
Inverse quadratic forms are standard numerical quantities.
Li--Sra--Jegelka develop Gaussian quadrature for matrix inverse forms
\citep{LiSraJegelka2016}, and Bizas--Mitrouli--Turek give further analytic
and heuristic estimates \citep{BizasMitrouliTurek2025}. Those methods
charge matrix--vector products. Our classical model instead explores
neighborhoods of sampled coordinates, trading dimension dependence for
exponential dependence on polynomial degree.

Gharibian--Le Gall already give sparse polynomial overlap estimation from
sampling access \citep[Theorem 4.1]{GharibianLeGall2022}.
Montanaro--Shao relate matrix-function query complexity to approximation
degree and construct the weighted Forrelation clocks used here
\citep[Theorem 1.8]{MontanaroShao2023}. Cifuentes et al. classify
matrix-function tasks under several access and measurement models
\citep{CifuentesEtAl2024}. Edenhofer--Hasegawa--Le Gall apply sparse
polynomial evaluation to spectral sums, including reciprocal traces
\citep[Theorem 3.1 and Section 3.3]{EdenhoferEtAl2026}.
Our target is an arbitrary-vector positive inverse form with relative
error, whose baseline and variance must be controlled. The polynomial
principle and its approximation-theoretic origin are shared with this
prior work. Le Gall's robustness results already cover approximate
length-squared sampling for sparse and low-rank dequantization
\citep{LeGall2025}; our finite-error transfer is a sufficient condition
for the particular estimator, not a general new robustness framework.

For quantum algorithms, block encodings and polynomial transformations
are established tools \citep{GilyenEtAl2019}, and our block-access upper
bound specializes Chakraborty--Gily\'en--Jeffery's norm estimator
\citep[Theorem 33 in the full version]{ChakrabortyGilyenJeffery2019}. Alase et al. study
scalar observables of linear-system solutions
\citep{AlaseEtAl2022}. Their tight observable-query bound concerns the
queried observable block and additive expectation error; our sharp block
bound charges the system matrix for relative estimation of $b^*H^{-1}b$.
Orsucci--Dunjko study positive definite solution-state preparation and
stronger access structures that can improve conditioning dependence
\citep{OrsucciDunjko2021}. A normalized solution state, a shifted-matrix
oracle, and the plain block interface used here are distinct contracts.
Section~\ref{sec:coherent} details these comparisons.

Gr\o nlund--Larsen establish exponential quantum versus SQ separation for
linear-system solution output \citep{GronlundLarsen2025}.
Apers--Gribling already prove full-SQ hardness for an ordinary LP optimal
value \citep[Lemma 8.5]{ApersGribling2026}. Our optimization constructions
therefore address parameter control, scalar normalization, genuine Newton
realizations, and input acquisition rather than the first occurrence of
scalar or optimization hardness. An adjacent result of Zhao et al.
studies normalized solution observables and Forrelation embeddings under
streaming-space and data-sample restrictions \citep[Task F.1 and
Sections E.5--F]{ZhaoEtAl2026}. Here the input is static and classical
storage is unrestricted; those space restrictions are not used.
General direct-sum, composition, and XOR ingredients are also prior
\citep{BlaisBrody2019,BenDavidBlais2020,ChakrabortyEtAl2023,BrodyEtAl2023}.
Our contribution in that part is their realization in the specified
optimization outputs and the proof of all interface simulations.

Low-rank SQ matrix arithmetic and solution sampling are established
\citep{ChiaEtAl2022}. The structured theorem here has a sparse full-rank
base and a small correction, and avoids supplying normalized transformed
columns as inputs. Cone Hessian identities and sparse implementations are
likewise established \citep{AlizadehGoldfarb2003,GoldfarbScheinberg2005,
RoyXiao2022,ChenGoulart2025,KapelevichAndersenVielma2024}.
We prove the spectral and sampling conditions needed to use that algebra
in a local-query theorem. Krylov recycling and greedy reduced bases
\citep{ParksEtAl2006,BinevEtAl2011}, as well as sparse and low-treewidth
elimination \citep{Vanderbei1995,FurerHoppenTrevisan2025}, supply the
classical context for our reuse and full-output comparisons.

\subsection{Scope and organization}
All oracle and ideal-arithmetic assumptions are stated in
Section~\ref{sec:models}. Sections~\ref{sec:classical}--\ref{sec:composition}
give classical algorithms, lower families, and composition.
Section~\ref{sec:coherent} treats coherent access.
Sections~\ref{sec:realizations}--\ref{sec:reuse} connect scalar outputs to
optimization and distinguish temporal composition from reuse.
Section~\ref{sec:structured} gives the structured cone-system comparisons.

The results are query and arithmetic statements under specified inputs.
They do not certify a generic quantum interior-point speedup, a uniform
bit-complexity bound, or free maintenance of iterate-dependent sampling
oracles. The general joint classical bound and the general coherent sparse
bound are not fully matched. These are boundaries of the classifications
proved here; none of the optimization or structured results assumes their
resolution.
