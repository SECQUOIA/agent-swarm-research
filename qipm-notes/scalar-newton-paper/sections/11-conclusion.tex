\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
A scalar Newton quantity has its own complexity. Positive relative
inverse forms admit local classical estimation with a linear conditioning
factor in the variance, while sparse full-SQ families force both statistical
and approximation-degree costs. Explicit composition shows when those
costs occur on the same scalar and why a signal's baseline cannot be
ignored. Under plain block access, the known variable-time algorithm is
optimal up to logarithms, but exact sparse entries obey a different
comparison.

The optimization constructions make these distinctions concrete. Hardness
can remain in an ordinary value, an optimizer coordinate, or a small
Newton decrement with fully simulated formulation access. It can also
accumulate in a sequence of requested scalars or a single endpoint.
The same examples show the limits of such conclusions: an unperturbed
value may be public, a normalized state may discard a hard sign or scale,
and a hard Newton ray may be reused throughout a long trajectory.
Consequently, any whole-optimization comparison must account for input
acquisition, the particular outputs requested, and information retained
between solves.

Structured systems provide a complementary conclusion. A sparse full-rank
base with a few controlled corrections permits scalar estimation and
sampling without constructing a dense inverse or normalizing small
correction columns. Cone dimension alone then does not determine query
complexity. For explicit directions, eccentricity profiles and latent
sparsity give different classical solve bounds. Their assumptions must
hold at the iterates actually used, and their residual guarantees must
match the outer method. Together, the results provide concrete bounds and
counterexamples with which to make such comparisons, while leaving the
unmatched general parameter regimes stated in the introduction open.
