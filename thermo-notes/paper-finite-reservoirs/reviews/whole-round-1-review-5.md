## Findings

### R5.1 — Minor, required: state the nonnegative curvature scale in the smooth-necessity proposition

Location: `sections/boundary-and-smooth.tex:25–29`, Proposition `prop:smooth-necessity`.

The proposition assumes `-h_N'' >= k κ_N` for `k>0` but does not explicitly state `κ_N >= 0`. Its proof concludes a vanishing product from a one-sided chord bound, which requires that sign. Taken on its own with arbitrary real `κ_N`, the proposition is false: take the equal mixture of `Normal(-N,N)` and `Normal(N,N)`, choose `h_N=0` on the whole line, and put `κ_N=-1`. The two positive phase components meet the stated Gaussian assumptions, `Q_N=P_N`, and `-h_N''=0 >= -k`, yet `κ_N N^(3/2)` does not tend to zero. These energy laws can be realized as canonical references by choosing their corresponding reference measures, so the framework does not exclude the example.

Add `κ_N >= 0` to the statement, preferably next to `k>0`. I classify this as minor because the surrounding positive-capacity interpretation and the earlier upper-curvature assumptions already communicate the intended sign; the correction changes none of the physical applications or intended proof. It is nevertheless a real omission in the standalone proposition, rather than merely a typographical preference.

### R5.2 — Minor, optional: connect the reservoir scale to the subsystem's coexistence heat capacity

Location: `sections/introduction.tex:52–62`, or the discussion following Table 1.

For a chemical-engineering reader, the distinction between the within-phase fluctuation scale and the complete canonical subsystem heat capacity deserves one explicit sentence. In the verified microscopic two-phase examples, the law-of-total-variance identity gives

`C_S,can/k_B = β² Var(E_N) ~ β² w_- w_+ Δ_N²`. Thus the complete canonical coexistence heat capacity is of order `N²`, while within-phase heat capacities are of order `N`. The optimized bath threshold of `N^(3/2)` is not obtained by simply demanding a bath larger than that total coexistence heat capacity. The endpoint calibration and the product of gap and width explain the result.

A short statement restricted to the microscopic examples, or to two-phase laws with controlled second moments, would make the physical resource comparison easier to interpret. Do not infer this moment identity's asymptotic coefficient from the weak-support theorem alone, which deliberately has no moment assumptions. This is an explanatory improvement, not a missing hypothesis in the main results.

### R5.3 — Minor, optional: retain primary attribution for the capillarity modeling ingredients

Location: `sections/thresholds.tex`, the discussion of the interior density envelope; `sections/capillarity-diagnostics.tex:33–41` and `109–119`; `refs.bib`.

The text correctly states the envelope and square-torus rate as model assumptions, and the minimizer calculation is proved. It would still help to credit the established droplet/fluctuation competition and circular-droplet/strip picture beside those assumptions. The relevant development note, `research/finite-bath-physical-extension.md`, already identifies primary sources; these contextual references disappeared from the manuscript bibliography.

For example, [Biskup, Chayes, and Kotecký, On the formation/dissolution of equilibrium droplets](https://arxiv.org/abs/math-ph/0207012) treats the competition between bulk fluctuations and droplet surface cost in its stated setting. [Kim, Keyes, and Straub, Communication: Iteration-free, weighted histogram analysis method in terms of intensive variables](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3166335/) discusses droplet and strip configurations in toroidal Potts calculations. Cite the relevant physical motivation without presenting either source as proving the manuscript's full density envelope or its microscopic square-torus rate. The existing explicit model qualification should remain.
