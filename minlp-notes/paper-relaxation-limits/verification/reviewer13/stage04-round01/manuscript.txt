      Convex relaxation gaps and spatial certificates in nonlinear
                            optimization



                                                Abstract
         Separate convexification of nonlinear terms can lose information about their shared variables.
     We develop a probability formulation of this loss and explain its relation to spatial optimality
     certificates. For bilinear functions with arbitrary coefficient signs, an elementary cut argument
     bounds the ratio of the McCormick gap to the scalar graph-hull gap by four times the square root
     of the largest induced edge density. A random-sign construction gives the matching order on each
     individual interaction graph. Sharper maximum-degree and bipartite bounds, exactness criteria,
     and the distinction between exact termwise convexification and recursive product relaxations
     identify the scope of these comparisons. The density order is also a consequence of earlier
     Schur-multiplier theory; the explicit transfer and proof are included to make that attribution
     precise.


1    Introduction
Convex relaxations support global optimization by providing bounds on nonlinear functions over a
region. Their strength depends on how the model is divided into pieces. Even when each piece is
replaced by its exact convex hull, the resulting bounds can be weaker than the hull of their sum:
separate pieces may use incompatible representations of their common variables. Spatial subdivision
introduces a second question, namely how many regions are needed before a specified bounding rule
certifies a desired tolerance. These questions concern different mathematical objects. A pointwise
envelope gap comparison is not an approximation ratio for a constrained optimization problem, and
it does not by itself bound a branch-and-bound tree.
    McCormick’s construction provides the basic recursive mechanism for factorable functions [18].
For multilinear functions, the convex hull of the scalar graph has a finite representation using box
vertices. Luedtke, Namazifar, and Linderoth [17] compare this hull with termwise and recursive
relaxations, and establish several exactness and positive-coefficient bounds. Boland et al. [4] show
that signed bilinear gaps can grow with the square root of dimension and relate the comparison to
cuts in induced interaction graphs.
    We use the vertex representation to state the comparisons consistently. For positive multilinear
functions on nonnegative boxes, a common probability distribution attains all upper term envelopes,
so the entire gap question reduces to a single simultaneous rounding problem. For signed bilinear
functions, an exact cut-range characterization instead permits graph density and degree bounds.
The elementary density proof makes the constants explicit, while the accompanying Schur-multiplier
transfer explains which asymptotic content was already implicit in established theory [7]. The
framework also specifies the bounding oracle and the certified-region count needed for spatial lower
bounds.

2.3    What a spatial certificate counts
Let F be a nonempty compact feasible set, let f be continuous, and put f ∗ = minx∈F f (x). A
region oracle assigns a valid lower bound LB(S) to feasible points in each region S. A certificate at
target T∗ is a finite cover S1 , . . . , SN of F with LB(Sj ) ≥ T∗ for all j. Together with a feasible value
U and an absolute tolerance ϵ ≥ 0, the target U − ϵ certifies absolute gap at most ϵ. For U > 0 and
a relative tolerance 0 ≤ θ < 1, the target (1 − θ)U certifies (U − f ∗ )/U ≤ θ. A size lower bound
may grant the best incumbent U = f ∗ , requiring f ∗ > 0 in this relative convention. Under these
tolerance ranges, either target is nondecreasing in U , so worse incumbents require weakly higher
targets.
    A complete spatial split tree supplies a cover by its final regions. A lower bound for arbitrary
covers is thus independent of branching order and node selection within the specified region and
oracle model. If a procedure discards feasible regions by propagation or additional cuts, those
deductions must also be included in the certificate model. A lower bound for one oracle does not
automatically survive stronger coupled cuts, different branch coordinates, or analytic solution of
separate components.

7     Incidence structure and marginal restrictions
The incidence graph of a specified factorization has variable vertices i, factor vertices e, and one
edge ie for each i ∈ e. It is a simple bipartite graph even when two factors have identical supports.
This is different from the primal variable graph, which joins two variables occurring together in
a factor. All widths below concern the incidence graph. Its treewidth is the minimum, over tree
decompositions, of the largest bag size minus one; a tree decomposition covers every edge and
has connected bags for each vertex. Its degeneracy is the largest minimum degree of a nonempty
subgraph. Its orientation parameter is the minimum possible maximum outdegree, with no acyclicity
requirement. Its variable frequency ν is the largest number of factors containing one variable. A
feedback variable set is a set of variable vertices whose deletion leaves a forest; write fF for its size.
Affine factors are discarded before these parameters are measured, unless a factorization is explicitly
retained.

Theorem 9.1 (One-sided cycle coloring). In a finite simple bipartite graph G = (V, F ; I) of treewidth
at most two, one can color the factor side F with two colors so that every cycle whose factor vertices
all have one color contains an even number of factor vertices. Consequently every zero-one matrix
with this bipartite support has a partition of its rows into two totally unimodular matrices.

    The all-cycle assertion is stronger than excluding induced odd-factor cycles (balancedness). The
stronger statement will supply total unimodularity for arbitrary integer cardinality slabs.

Proof. We prove a two-terminal series-parallel invariant. A network starts with an edge; series com-
position identifies one terminal of each of two otherwise disjoint networks, and parallel composition
identifies both terminal pairs. Intermediate networks are simple and bipartite subgraphs of the
eventual graph. A coloring is good if its monochromatic-factor cycles have even factor count. A
terminal path is c-monochromatic when all its factor vertices, including factor terminals, have color
c. Its parity counts those factor vertices modulo two.
    For each network there is a parity ϵ ∈ {0, 1} with all the following witnesses available. They are
possibly different colorings. For V, V terminals and either color c, an active good coloring has at least
one c-monochromatic terminal path, all such paths have parity ϵ, and no path is monochromatic in
the other color. If ϵ = 0, a blocking good coloring with neither type of monochromatic terminal
path is also available. For mixed terminals, with either prescribed color c of the factor terminal, an
active good coloring has paths of color c, all of parity ϵ; a blocking witness with that prescribed
terminal color is available whenever there is no direct terminal edge. For F, F terminals, with either
prescribed common color c, the same active witness exists; if ϵ = 1 a blocking witness with those
colors also exists. In addition, for either assignment of distinct terminal colors, a good coloring
exists. Such colors automatically block monochromatic terminal paths. Finally a network with a
direct terminal edge is mixed and uses ϵ = 1.
    The base edge satisfies the mixed invariant with parity one. For a series composition let δ be
one if the shared vertex is a factor, and zero otherwise. The output parity is ϵ = ϵ1 ⊕ ϵ2 ⊕ δ. Every


                                                   35
path concatenates component terminal paths; subtracting the shared factor once accounts for δ.
Every cycle stays in a component. Active witnesses concatenate by using the same desired color
and matching the shared factor’s color when present. The required additional witnesses have the
following complete case analysis.

  1. For outer types V, V and a shared variable, use opposite active colors in the two V, V
     components. No monochromatic path can cross both.

  2. For outer types V, V and a shared factor, blocking is required only if the component parities
     differ. The even mixed component has no direct edge and can block with the prescribed
     shared-factor color; use an active witness of that color in the other component.

  3. For mixed outer types and a shared variable, use the active color opposite to the outer factor’s

     This blocks.

  4. For mixed outer types and a shared factor, give the shared factor the color opposite to the
     outer factor. The F, F component uses its distinct-terminal witness, and the mixed component

  5. For outer types F, F of common color and a shared variable, blocking is required only for
     output parity one, so the two mixed component parities differ. Block the even component,
     which has no direct edge, and activate the other, respecting both outer colors.

  6. For outer types F, F of common color and a shared factor, color the shared factor oppositely.
     Both F, F components have their required distinct-terminal good witnesses and block.

  7. For outer F, F terminals of distinct colors and a shared variable, activate each mixed component
     in its outer terminal’s color. If instead the shared vertex is a factor, give it either color and

These cases include reversed mixed types and exhaust all triples of terminal types. A nontrivial
series composition has no direct outer edge, so its mixed blocking requirement has also been checked.
     In a parallel composition every new simple cycle consists of one terminal path from each
component. If the paths are monochromatic in one color, its factor parity is their parity XOR plus
τ , the number of factor terminals modulo two. Thus τ = 0 for equal terminal types and τ = 1 for
mixed types. We check the invariant as follows. For V, V terminals with equal component parities,
activate both in the same color. If both are even, both may also block. For different parities,
activate the odd component and block the even component. Output parity is ϵ1 ∨ ϵ2 ; whenever
both components contribute paths their equal parities make new cycles even. For F, F terminals of
common prescribed color, activate both when parities agree; if both are odd, both may also block.
If parities differ, activate the even component and block the odd one. Output parity is ϵ1 ∧ ϵ2 .
With distinct prescribed terminal colors, use their good witnesses in both components; a crossing
monochromatic cycle would contain both differently colored terminals and is impossible. For mixed
terminals with no direct edge, both components can block in the prescribed factor color. Activate
either one and block the other for an active witness, choosing its parity as output, or block both
for the blocking witness. For mixed terminals with a direct edge, simplicity implies exactly one
component contains it. Activate its parity-one witness and block the edge-free component. No
blocking output is required. These mixed choices create no crossing monochromatic cycle. The
induction is complete.

Example 9.3 (An exact width ratio of three). Use six fair variables in pairs AB, BC, CA, and put

                      E(u, v) = 1 − u − v + 2uv,      N (u, v) = u + v − 2uv.

These are the multiaffine equality and inequality indicators, both in [0, 1] on the square. Define

             gA = E(AB)E(CA),          gB = N (AB)E(BC),         gC = N (BC)N (CA).

Each local upper envelope is one at the center: mix a satisfying assignment and its complement.
Each local lower envelope is zero, by the analogous mixture of a falsifying assignment. No two
factors can equal one on a binary assignment, because adjacent factors demand opposite parities on
their common pair. Thus the sum’s upper envelope is at most one, attained by the all-zero/all-one
mixture, which satisfies gA . The pair assignment AB = 01, BC = 01, CA = 00 and its complement
make all factors zero, proving the sum’s lower envelope zero. Therefore T = 3 and H = 1 for these
three entire factors.

variable has frequency two. The factors are nonnegative on the full cube, but their monomial
coefficients have mixed signs, so neither the positive-monomial nor the convex-cardinality factor
theorem applies.

Proposition 9.4 (Independent-set payoff family). For a simple graph G with nonnegative vertex

9.2    Limits of the coloring method
A distinct open question at incidence treewidth two asks whether the factors admit two classes
whose incidence subgraphs are each chordal bipartite, meaning they contain no induced cycle of
length at least six. The proved all-cycle parity property permits induced eight-cycles and does
not establish this target. The recorded experiment found such a partition for all 5,000 generated
supports; this is finite evidence only.
    The all-cycle coloring invariant cannot extend with a bounded number of colors to width three.
In K3,m , m ≥ 3, with its three-vertex side interpreted as variables, every three factors lie on a
six-cycle. Each permitted color class therefore contains at most two factors, requiring ⌈m/2⌉ colors,
although the graph has treewidth three. Its all-ones matrix is already TU: determinants of order at
least two vanish. The six-cycles have chords and do not obstruct balancedness. This separates the
stronger coloring mechanism from a TU-row-partition target.
    Finite searches found three balanced classes in 3,000 generated width-at-most-three supports
(212 required more than two), and three TU classes in 300 further supports. Their accepted widths
were certified by particular elimination orders; rejected greedy orders did not certify larger width.
The TU tests enumerated column signings by the Ghouila-Houri criterion and used exact coloring
backtracking. These searches are finite evidence, not proofs of universal partitions. The exact Wk
for k ≥ 3, k-class balanced or TU partitions, and sharp planar constants remain open here. A
planar bipartite incidence graph does admit an orientation of maximum outdegree at most two:
Hall’s theorem assigns every edge to one of two slots at an endpoint, since every edge set uses
at most twice as many edges as incident vertices. Orient from the assigned endpoint. Thus the
general orientation theorem gives a finite planar bound, without settling its sharp value. Auxiliary
reductions and the simpler forest-coloring obstruction are recorded in Appendix D.

A     Exhaustive verification of the finite signing constants
This appendix gives the finite computational proof used in Proposition 3.6. Label vertices 0, . . . , n−1.
For vertex signs ti , switching replaces aij by a′ij = aij ti tj . The identity Qa′ (s) = Qa (t · s) shows
that switching preserves both extrema of Q, and hence RV . Taking t0 = 1 and tj = a0j makes every
coefficient incident to zero positive. This normalized representative is unique within each switching
class: a switching that keeps those coefficients positive has tj = t0 for all j, so changes no edge.
                                      n−1
Thus it suffices to enumerate the 2( 2 ) choices of negative edges among vertices 1, . . . , n − 1. There
are respectively 2, 8, 64, 1024, 32768 representatives for n = 3, . . . , 7.
    Encode a cut by the subset on side one, always leaving vertex zero on side zero. Complementation
changes no cut, so the 2n−1 such subsets include every cut. If a cut has c crossing edges, of which h
are negative, its weight is exactly c − 2h. The following complete Python 3.10+ program computes
the first row of Proposition 3.6 using unbounded integers. The operation bit_count() counts
set bits in an integer; each free-edge mask records which edges cross the cut. No floating-point
arithmetic or solver is used.

from itertools import combinations
def min_range(n):
    edges = list(combinations(range(n), 2))
    free = [(i, j) for i, j in edges if i != 0]
    cuts = []
    for side in range(1 << (n - 1)):
        side <<= 1 # Vertex zero stays on side zero.
        cross = lambda i, j: ((side >> i) ^ (side >> j)) & 1
        size = sum(cross(i, j) for i, j in edges)
        mask = sum(cross(i, j) << k
                   for k, (i, j) in enumerate(free))
        cuts.append((size, mask))
    best = len(edges) + 1
    for negative in range(1 << len(free)):
        weights = [size - 2 * (negative & mask).bit_count()
                   for size, mask in cuts]
        best = min(best, max(weights) - min(weights))
    return best
result = [min_range(n) for n in range(2, 8)]
assert result == [1, 2, 4, 4, 5, 8]

E.3    Optimality within a fixed two-law mixture
Proposition E.3 (A limitation of fixed independent/fair-orientation mixtures). Fix ρ > 1 and an
orientation weight w ∈ [0, 1]. For mixtures of I and independently fair O, the best uniform per-
physical-monomial captured fraction over all dimensions is at most min{w/2, (1 − w)/ρ}. Optimizing
the weight gives exactly 1/(ρ + 2), attained at w = 2/(ρ + 2).

Proof. For a bilinear product at normalized means (ϵ, 1 − ϵ), 0 < ϵ < 1/2, deleting affine parts
gives DI /T = ϵ and DO /T = 1/2. Letting ϵ ↓ 0 bounds the mixture fraction by w/2. For the
other obstruction use means ϵ and k copies of 1 − ϵ/k, where k ≥ 1. Divide physical values by
ρk+1 , and write α = 1/ρ, η = 1 − α, γ = (1 + α)/2. The sum of success means is k, giving V = α.
Common-threshold and fair-orientation integration, split at ϵ/k and ϵ, and direct independent
multiplication give

                                      C = α + ϵ[η − α(1 − αk )/k],
                                      P = α[1 + (ρ − 1)ϵ][1 − ηϵ/k]k ,
                                      O = α + ϵ[η − 2γ(1 − γ k )/k].

For clarity, in the orientation law the two end intervals of length ϵ/k have normalized expected
product γ k+1 , the two next intervals of total length 2(ϵ − ϵ/k) have value γ, and the middle interval
of length 1 − 2ϵ has value α; these yield the stated O. For each fixed k, divide deficiencies by ϵ and
let ϵ ↓ 0. Then

                                     T /ϵ = η − α(1 − αk )/k,
                                    DI /ϵ −→ αη − α(1 − αk )/k,
                                   DO /ϵ = [2γ(1 − γ k ) − α(1 − αk )]/k.

The denominator coefficient is at least η 2 > 0 because 1 − αk ≤ kη. Now let k → ∞ to obtain
DI /T → 1/ρ and DO /T → 0, bounding the mixture by (1 − w)/ρ. The maximum of the minimum
of these two bounds is 1/(ρ + 2) at their intersection. Lemma 10.1 with fair orientations supplies
the matching guarantee.
