      Convex relaxation gaps and spatial certificates in nonlinear
                            optimization



                                                Abstract
         Separate convexification of nonlinear terms can lose information about their shared variables.
     We develop a probability formulation of this loss and explain its relation to spatial optimality
     certificates. For bilinear functions with arbitrary coefficient signs, an elementary cut argument
     bounds the ratio of the McCormick gap to the scalar graph-hull gap by four times the square root
     of the largest induced edge density. A random-sign construction gives the matching order on each
     individual interaction graph. Sharper maximum-degree and bipartite bounds, exactness criteria,
     and the distinction between exact termwise convexification and recursive product relaxations
     identify the scope of these comparisons. The density order is also a consequence of earlier
     Schur-multiplier theory; the explicit transfer and proof are included to make that attribution
     precise.


1    Introduction
Convex relaxations support global optimization by providing bounds on nonlinear functions over a
region. Their strength depends on how the model is divided into pieces. Even when each piece is
replaced by its exact convex hull, the resulting bounds can be weaker than the hull of their sum:
separate pieces may use incompatible representations of their common variables. Spatial subdivision
introduces a second question, namely how many regions are needed before a specified bounding rule
certifies a desired tolerance. These questions concern different mathematical objects. A pointwise
envelope gap comparison is not an approximation ratio for a constrained optimization problem, and
it does not by itself bound a branch-and-bound tree.
    McCormick’s construction provides the basic recursive mechanism for factorable functions [4].
For multilinear functions, the convex hull of the scalar graph has a finite representation using box
vertices. Luedtke, Namazifar, and Linderoth [3] compare this hull with termwise and recursive
relaxations, and establish several exactness and positive-coefficient bounds. Boland et al. [1] show
that signed bilinear gaps can grow with the square root of dimension and relate the comparison to
cuts in induced interaction graphs.
    We use the vertex representation to state the comparisons consistently. For positive multilinear
functions, a common probability distribution attains all upper term envelopes, so the entire gap
question reduces to a single simultaneous rounding problem. For signed bilinear functions, an exact
cut-range characterization instead permits graph density and degree bounds. The elementary density
proof makes the constants explicit, while the accompanying Schur-multiplier transfer explains which
asymptotic content was already implicit in established theory [2]. The framework also specifies the
bounding oracle and the certified-region count needed for spatial lower bounds.

2.3    What a spatial certificate counts
Let F be a nonempty compact feasible set, let f be continuous, and put f ∗ = minx∈F f (x). A
region oracle assigns a valid lower bound LB(S) to feasible points in each region S. A certificate
at target T∗ is a finite cover S1 , . . . , SN of F with LB(Sj ) ≥ T∗ for all j. Together with a feasible
value U , the target U − ϵ certifies absolute gap at most ϵ. For U > 0, the target (1 − θ)U certifies
(U − f ∗ )/U ≤ θ. A size lower bound may grant the best incumbent U = f ∗ : worse incumbents
require weakly higher targets.
    A complete spatial split tree supplies a cover by its final regions. A lower bound for arbitrary
covers is thus independent of branching order and node selection within the specified region and
oracle model. If a procedure discards feasible regions by propagation or additional cuts, those
deductions must also be included in the certificate model. A lower bound for one oracle does not
automatically survive stronger coupled cuts, different branch coordinates, or analytic solution of
separate components.
