      Rounding switching controls under a hard switch budget:
           sharp minimax bounds and exact algorithms



                                                  Abstract
           Combinatorial integral approximation replaces a relaxed mixture of control modes
       by one active mode at a time while controlling cumulative error. A hard bound on the
       number of switches changes the approximation problem: even on arbitrarily fine time
       grids, the worst-case error can remain proportional to the horizon. We study this error
       as a function of the number of modes, the switch budget, and the time grid. We establish
       exact uniform-input formulas and the full continuous one-switch minimax value, with
       matching extremizers and constructive proofs for measurable relaxed controls. These
       results give explicit counterexamples to a published switching-budget conjecture. The
       continuous and grid problems are formulated separately, so that bounds for individual
       inputs and worst-case bounds have unambiguous meanings.


1     Problem and basic properties
A relaxed switching control allocates one unit of activity among finitely many modes.
Rounding selects one mode at a time. The cumulative allocation error is the objective of
combinatorial integral approximation (CIA), the rounding subproblem in a decomposition
of mixed-integer optimal control. A restriction on the number of mode changes is a hard
constraint on that subproblem. Sager and Zeile [1] study this formulation, develop maximum
dwell rounding, and give sharp bounds and a conjecture for its worst-case error. Our focus
is the extremal rounding problem itself. In particular, a bound on cumulative allocation
error is not an assertion that the same schedule optimizes an underlying nonlinear control
objective.

2     Uniform inputs and the exact one-switch minimax
We first solve a benchmark family for every budget with fewer activation blocks than modes.
This family gives a dimension-dependent obstruction to small error. We then prove that
its obstruction, together with an omitted-mode obstruction, determines the full continuous
one-switch minimax.
