          Certified support cuts for shared nonlinear expressions
                           and quadratic blocks


                                                 Abstract
         Global solvers for nonconvex mixed-integer nonlinear programs relax nonlinear expressions
     one at a time and lose their dependence through shared variables. Support cuts of the hull
     of their joint graph keep it. We study when such cuts add strength, how to compute them
     exactly for quadratic blocks, and how to keep them valid. Gluing exact pair hulls on shared
     moments is known to lose strength; we quantify the loss. For a family of quadratic directions on
     a three-variable path, the glued relaxation misses either nothing or δ 2 /2, depending on whether
     two point sets at distance δ interleave; an interface that shares κ moments gives no positive
     bound exactly when the local zero sets alternate at least κ + 1 times; and from four variables on,
     joint blocks can be strictly stronger than dense semidefinite relaxations. For quadratic stars with
     k leaves, m center–leaf rows and m0 center rows we give an exact rational support algorithm with
     O(m0 + (m + k) log(m + k)) operations. Eliminating the model rows gives cuts in the original
     variables, valid as stored if their bound is certified for the exact binary64 direction, rounding is
     corrected and the stored row is checked. Every recorded cut passed replay, whereas sample-based
     constants would have cut off feasible solutions. On MINLPLib models the cuts did not help
     SCIP. On constructed path and star families the cuts closed nearly all of SCIP’s root gap, and a
     configuration fixed in advance solved all 20 fresh path instances, against at most 10 for SCIP
     and 5 for Gurobi.

Keywords: simultaneous convexification; support cuts; nonconvex quadratic programming; certified
cuts; safe rounding; mixed-integer nonlinear programming

1    Introduction
Global solvers for nonconvex mixed-integer nonlinear programs (MINLPs) bound the optimal value
by relaxing every nonlinear expression. The standard construction decomposes each expression
into elementary operations, introduces an auxiliary variable for each, and relaxes each operation
on its own [90, 117, 119]. This construction is general, but it relaxes expressions as if they were
unrelated. In many models they are not: the same variables appear in several nonlinear constraints,
and linear constraints restrict how these variables can move together. Pooling and blending models,
heat and mass exchange networks and gas networks are typical examples. A relaxation that treats
such expressions separately can be much weaker than one that treats them jointly.
    The strongest convex relaxation of several functions f1 , . . . , fp of the same variables x on
a common domain D is the convex hull of their joint graph {(x, f1 (x), . . . , fp (x)) : x ∈ D}.
Its valid linear inequalities are support cuts: for a direction (a, b), the inequality aT x + bT w ≥
minx∈D {aT x+bT F (x)} is valid, where wj stands for fj (x), and all such inequalities together describe
the hull. This simultaneous convexification has been studied for vectors of functions [118], [83,
Proposition 1], including vectors of univariate functions [67], for bilinear and composite functions
[36, 64, 130], and in applications [83]. Turning it into a solver component raises four questions.

                                                      1
 (1) Which blocks are worth relaxing jointly? In particular, is it enough to relax every pair of
     interacting variables exactly, or do larger blocks carry information that pairs miss?
 (2) Exactness. The support value is the minimum of a nonconvex function. For which blocks can
     it be computed exactly, so that the cuts are as strong as possible?
 (3) Integration and validity. Support cuts live in the space of (x, w), and introducing the variables
     w changes the model that the solver presolves, propagates and branches on. Can the cuts be
     stated in the original variables? And since directions are found numerically and the solver
     stores binary64 rows, which part of the computation must be exact for the stored row to be
     valid?
 (4) Usefulness. Modern solvers already detect quadratic, bilinear and convex structure and have
     their own cut families for it [13]. Do joint support cuts improve them?
   We answer these questions for quadratic blocks in depth and for shared nonlinear expressions
more generally. The contributions are the following.

  • Limits of composing pair hulls (Section 3). On a path of three variables, exact pair hulls
    identified on the first two moments of the shared variable miss a gap of 1/128 that a single
    support cut in the existing coordinates closes. For a family of such paths the gap is positive
    exactly when two two-point sets interleave, and it then equals δ 2 /2, where δ is their distance.
    An interface that identifies κ moments of the shared variable gives no positive bound exactly
    when the local zero sets alternate at least κ + 1 times, a reading of the classical description of
    Radon partitions on the moment curve, and for every κ there is a quadratic instance with
    a relative gap of 100%. On a three-variable path, a dense semidefinite relaxation with the
    McCormick inequalities of all products is exact, by a theorem of Burer et al. [27]; there the
    advantage of the joint block over its pairs is one of representation, which support cuts obtain
    in the coordinates that the model already has. From four variables on, the dense relaxation
    is no longer exact for every objective: on a four-variable path and on our full-gap instances,
    joint support cuts are strictly stronger, on the latter with a relative gap of nearly 100% for the
    dense relaxation. Through Lagrangian decomposition we also relate the gain from merging
    pairs into a star to the glued relaxation.
  • Exact support for constrained stars (Section 4). For quadratic stars whose affine rows couple
    the center with one leaf at a time, we give an exact rational algorithm with O(m0 + (m +
    k) log(m + k)) operations for k leaves, m center–leaf rows and m0 rows in the center alone; on
    instances with m = Θ(k) this is optimal for algebraic computation trees. The box-constrained
    case is a special case of the forest algorithm of Del Pia and Khajavirad [37]; to our knowledge,
    no exact algorithm for the case with center–leaf rows has been published. For polytopes of
    fixed dimension we state face enumeration together with the degenerate cases that a certificate
    must cover.
  • Certified cuts in the original variables (Sections 5 and 6). Minimizing nonnegative combinations
    of nonlinear rows over their common block domain and eliminating the rows gives cuts in the
    original variables, with no auxiliary variables. Together these cuts describe the projection of
    the joint-graph relaxation, and we give conditions under which this projection is the convex
    hull of the set that the rows define, and examples where it is not. Validity of a stored cut rests
    on four checks: a certified lower bound over the whole block domain for the exact binary64
    direction, exact elimination, bound-corrected rounding, and a comparison with the row that
    the solver stores; the direction itself may be found by any numerical method.

                                                  2
    • Implementation and evaluation (Sections 7 and 8). We implemented the cuts as a SCIP
      separator whose model import is checked to reproduce the exact binary64 source expressions
      and their domains, and whose every cut can be replayed. We evaluate it with prospective
      protocols on MINLPLib models and on constructed families with path and star structure,
      against SCIP’s default, against SCIP’s disabled-by-default nonconvex separators, and, on the
      constructed families, against Gurobi; an ablation measures what the certificate prevents.

    The other ingredients, namely the support description, Bernstein and ball-arithmetic bounds,
safe rounding, Lagrangian duality and oracle separation, are classical; we use them and state them
precisely where a certificate depends on the details (Sections 5 and 6, Appendices E and F).
    Section 8 reports five campaigns, each run with solver code, limits and protocol fixed in advance.
All 274,489 cut records of the last three campaigns passed a fresh-process replay, which re-executes
the exact support routines against the source model and the row stored by SCIP. Constants taken
from the separator’s samples would have been wrong for 38% of the cuts on MINLPLib models and
for almost all sampled path-family cuts, and their rows would have cut off feasible solutions; local
minimization reduced the share of wrong constants to a few percent but still left rows that cut off
known optima, and even a numerical global solver once reported optimality for a wrong constant.
On MINLPLib models the cuts did not change which models SCIP solved in campaigns 2 to 4 and
left SCIP’s own solving time unchanged; the Python separator made the runs slower, and SCIP’s
disabled-by-default separators improved the root bound more often. On 20 larger models that SCIP
does not solve within 60 seconds, the separator with default limits stopped during block discovery
on 12, and with raised limits its cuts improved one root bound and worsened five. On constructed
instances with the path structure of Section 3, SCIP’s root bound is weak because of a presolve step;
with that step switched off, SCIP reaches the level of glued pair relaxations and no further. A post
hoc diagnostic and a later prospective control showed that most of the remaining closure requires
enough cuts per block, and that trying the exact support of each whole row first supplies the rest
when the coupling row does not bind. On 20 fresh instances, this configuration, fixed before the
instances were generated, closed SCIP’s root gap and solved all 20 within 300 seconds, against at
most 10 for SCIP in three settings and 5 for Gurobi; with a binding coupling row the cuts solved all
20 as well, but so did SCIP without its presolve step, faster. On a family of constrained stars with
up to 16 leaves, star blocks certified by the star oracle made the root bound exact, which neither
smaller blocks nor SCIP achieved; Gurobi, which found good solutions faster, solved more of these
instances.
    We do not claim a new convexification principle, a general speedup, or a certificate for a complete
solve; the certificates cover the cuts and the model they were derived from. Section 2 fixes notation
and reviews related work, Sections 3–6 develop the theory, Section 7 describes the implementation,
Section 8 reports the experiments, and Section 9 discusses consequences and open problems. The
appendices contain proofs and examples, the relation of the cuts to the hull of the feasible set,
whole-domain bounds for non-quadratic blocks, complete separation, the first two campaigns, and
the test sets with detailed results.


2     Setting and related work
A block consists of variables x ∈ Rn , a nonempty compact domain D ⊂ Rn and continuous features
F = (f1 , . . . , fp ) : D → Rp . In a model, the features are expressions of the same variables, and D is
the set on which they must be relaxed, typically a box intersected with the linear rows that involve

only these variables. The joint graph of F on D and its convex hull are

Proposition 2.1 (Support description). For every (a, b) and every β ≤ hD (a, b), the inequality
aT x+bT w ≥ β is valid for HD (F ). Moreover HD (F ) is the intersection of the half-spaces aT x+bT w ≥
hD (a, b) over all (a, b).

Proof. The inequality holds on GD (F ) by definition and is preserved by convex combinations.
GD (F ) is compact, so HD (F ) is compact and convex, and a point outside it can be strictly separated
by a linear functional; written as a lower bound, the separating inequality is one of the stated
half-spaces.

    We call aT x + bT w ≥ β a support cut, and a support inequality when β = hD (a, b). Support
inequalities are as strong as possible for the block: no valid inequality in these coordinates is missing
from the family. They also combine information that separate relaxations discard. If each fj is
relaxed on its own, the result is the intersection of the hulls of the graphs of (x, fj ), which can be
much larger than HD (F ) when the fj share variables (Example 5.4). For n = p = 1, the family
recovers the convex and concave envelopes of a single function. If integer variables occur in a block,
we drop their integrality and keep their bounds; support cuts remain valid for the mixed-integer
graph, but they need not describe its convex hull.

2.1   Related work
Factorable and simultaneous relaxations. Global solvers relax nonconvex models by de-
composing expressions into elementary operations and relaxing each with an auxiliary variable
[8, 13, 90, 117, 119]. The resulting relaxations ignore the dependence among expressions that
share variables, and they ignore linear constraints that link those variables [130]. Simultaneous
convexification addresses the first point. Tawarmalani [118] develops inclusion certificates for the
convex hull of several functions of the same variables; Ballerstein [5], as stated by Liers et al. [83,
Proposition 1], characterizes the hull of a vector of functions by linear combinations, and Liers
et al. [83] separate over it in a gas network application. He and Tawarmalani [64, 65, 66] build
composite relaxations with vectors of outer functions, Zhu et al. [130] treat simultaneous factorable
graphs with coupling constraints, and Davarnia et al. [36] convexify vectors of bilinear functions
simultaneously over polytopes; He et al. [67] describe simultaneous hulls of vectors of univariate
functions through moment hulls. For multilinear and bilinear terms, the gap between termwise and
joint relaxations is well studied [7, 19, 86, 110]. Several bilinear and quadratic terms are relaxed
jointly by the multiterm relaxations of Bao et al. [6] and by the edge-concave relaxations of Misener
and Floudas [91]. Reduced-space relaxations avoid auxiliary variables altogether [20, 98, 123]. Our
support cuts are an instance of simultaneous convexification; what we add concerns which blocks
are worth relaxing jointly, which support problems can be solved exactly, how the cuts enter the
original model, and how they are certified.




                                                     4
Lagrangian and aggregation cuts. Cuts in the original variables obtained by minimizing a
weighted sum of constraint functions over a block are Lagrangian cuts. They appear in block
decomposition methods for MINLP [73, 106, 128], in range reduction [55, 119] and in rigorous
filtering [42]. The relaxation they describe, convexification in the joint space of variables and
constraint values, is the primal form of the Lagrangian dual [46, 48, 53, 82], and the set version of
this statement for Lagrangian cuts is given by Chen and Luedtke [29]. Aggregations of quadratic
constraints that describe convex hulls under additional hypotheses [18, 40], surrogate relaxations
that keep an aggregated constraint nonconvex [94], and the joint-range inequalities of Xu and
Pokutta [129], which convexify the joint range of two quadratic functions and lift the result to sparse
cuts, are different objects; Section 5.3 relates them to our cuts.

Exact low-dimensional and structured quadratic hulls. Anstreicher and Burer [3] show that
the convex hull of {(x, xxT )} is described by the semidefinite constraints and the constraints of the
reformulation-linearization technique (RLT) [113, 115] over a simplex of dimension at most three
and over a box in dimension two, and that polytopes of dimension at most three admit an extended
formulation built from a triangulation; for the box in dimension three these constraints are not
enough [2, 3, 26]. Explicit envelopes through polyhedral subdivisions are given by Tawarmalani et al.
[121]. Convex envelopes of bivariate functions over general polygons, with supporting hyperplanes,
are computed by Locatelli and Schoen [84]. Minimizing a quadratic over a polytope of fixed
dimension by enumerating faces is classical [97, Section 2.9], quadratic programming is in NP [124],
and box-constrained quadratic programming on forests is strongly polynomial [37]. Explicit hulls for
star-shaped sign patterns are given by Dey and Khajavirad [39] and Khajavirad [76], and parametric
dynamic programs over trees solve convex quadratic problems with indicators [15]. The gluing of
local relaxations along a sparsity pattern is discussed in Section 3.4.

Rigorous relaxations and safe cuts. Safe bounds and cuts for linear and mixed-integer programs
correct floating-point results with variable bounds and directed rounding [34, 45, 102]; exact MIP
solvers and VIPR certificates check complete solves against the original instance [31, 35, 44],
SCIP 10 offers an exact mode for MILP [68], and Halbig et al. [62] compute optimality certificates
for convex MINLPs. For nonlinear problems, rigorous linear relaxations and safe linear estimators
are established [21, 41, 74, 105], as are verified Bernstein bounds [50, 51, 95] and ball arithmetic
[70]. That rounding in the problem formulation itself must be controlled was pointed out by
Neumaier [101, Section 20] and Schichl and Neumaier [112]. Liers et al. [83] mention safe rounding
of coefficients without details. Cut generators are usually tested for validity against known feasible
solutions [89]; replay of exact certificates is a stronger test for the cuts it covers. SCIP relaxes its
own nonlinear cuts with variable bounds before storing them, in floating point, to protect them
against the solver’s coefficient rounding [12, Section 4.2.10]. To our knowledge, no published MINLP
implementation certifies the exported rows of joint support cuts against the source model and the
row stored by the solver.

Separation and oracles. Weak separation and weak optimization are polynomially equivalent
for convex bodies given by oracles [58, 59]. Gilbert’s nearest-point method gives an oracle procedure
that returns either a nearby point of the set or a separating hyperplane [25, 54], and the local-cut
and Fenchel-cut schemes separate by column generation over an optimization oracle, in exact
arithmetic when needed [22, 33]; in direction space this column generation is Kelley’s cutting-plane
method [30, 75]. Oracle-based cuts for polynomial optimization are studied by Bienstock et al. [17].
Appendix F states the corresponding finite procedure for joint graphs and its certificate.


                                                   5
Native SCIP. SCIP’s nonlinear constraint handler detects quadratic, bilinear, convex, concave
and other structures, uses projections of linear rows for bilinear terms [93], and separates RLT cuts
[14] and semidefinite minor cuts [13]. If a variable with finite bounds and no objective coefficient
occurs in a single polynomial constraint that is concave (convex) in it, with an infinite left-hand
(right-hand) side, some optimal solution has the variable at one of its bounds; SCIP’s presolve then
makes the variable binary when its bounds are [0, 1], or adds a bound disjunction [12, Section 4.2.7],
an idea that goes back to Hansen et al. [63]. Several cut families that target the same joint quadratic
structure as our cuts are implemented but disabled by default because they have not been found
to pay off in general [13, 68]: edge-concave cuts on aggregated quadratic terms [91], intersection
cuts for quadratic constraints from maximal quadratic-free sets [32, 96], interminor cuts, which are
intersection cuts for vanishing 2 × 2 minors of the matrix of products [12, Section 4.11], related to
the outer-product-free sets of Bienstock et al. [17], and RLT cuts for hidden products [14]. A new
separator therefore has to be evaluated against SCIP’s default and against these separators, not
against termwise McCormick relaxations; Section 8 does both.


3     When exact pair hulls do not compose
A solver that relaxes a quadratic expression term by term or pair by pair works with the variables
and products that occur in the model. Even if every pair were relaxed exactly, gluing the pair hulls
on their shared variables gives in general a weaker set than the hull of the joint graph, because finitely
many shared moments do not determine the distribution of a shared variable [47, 80, 103]. In the
MINLP literature, Tawarmalani [118, Example 3.8] shows that separate envelopes are weaker than
the simultaneous hull because they may represent the same point by different convex combinations.
This section quantifies the phenomenon for the smallest quadratic case, a path of three variables
with exact pair hulls, and determines exactly when gluing on κ shared moments yields no positive
bound. The results explain when a merged block is stronger than its pairs, and they delimit that
advantage.

3.3    Matching more moments
The same mechanism defeats any interface that identifies a fixed finite number of moments of the
shared variable. Let XL and XR be compact sets of points (x, y) and (y, z), with y scalar, and let
HL and HR be the convex hulls of continuous feature maps on them whose first κ coordinates are
y, y 2 , . . . , y κ ; the other local features are arbitrary. Let Rκ be the set of pairs of points of HL and
HR that agree in these κ coordinates. Let ΦL ≥ 0 on XL and ΦR ≥ 0 on XR be affine functions
of the local features, with zero sets ZL and ZR , and let A and C be the projections of ZL and ZR
onto y. For disjoint compact A, C ⊂ R, the alternation number alt(A, C) is the largest r for which
there are points t0 < · · · < tr of A ∪ C with consecutive points in different sets, and alt(A, C) = 0 if
A ∪ C has at most one point.

(x, y, z) with (x, y) ∈ XL and (y, z) ∈ XR . The linearization of ΦL + ΦR vanishes at some point of
Rκ if and only if alt(A, C) ≥ κ + 1. In particular, gluing on κ moments always gives a positive

3.5    When gluing is exact, and the gain from merging
Gluing is exact when the shared coordinates determine the shared distribution. Under a running-
intersection decomposition, local measures with equal full marginals on the separators glue to a
global measure [80, 126], and Tawarmalani [118, Corollary 3.10] glues two functions that share a
variable when their inclusion certificates have equal marginals of that variable. If the separator
takes finitely many known values t1 , . . . , tN , its moments of orders 0, . . . , N − 1 determine its
distribution through a Vandermonde system; for a binary separator the mean suffices. This is why
the McCormick inequalities describe the convex hull of a pure bilinear function on a forest over the
unit box [107, Proposition 8]: the extreme points are binary, the McCormick inequalities of an edge
are the nonnegativity of the four joint probabilities of its endpoints, and adjacent edges share a
binary variable whose distribution is fixed by its mean. Square terms break this argument, because
they let a fractional mean be realized by continuous distributions, as in (3).
    The gain from merging pair directions into one star direction can be computed exactly, and it is
related to the glued relaxation by a re-splitting of the center terms. Part (ii) of the next proposition
is the Lagrangian-decomposition duality [53, 60] applied to the pair hulls, with the shared moments
(my , sy ) as copied variables; in the language of sparse moment relaxations, it says that the glued
bound is the best split of the objective with an interface polynomial of degree two in y [56], [104,
Theorem 3.1]. What we add is that exact pair and star minima evaluate both sides.

4     Exact support for quadratic blocks
If every feature fj is a quadratic polynomial, then every support problem (2) asks for the minimum
of a single quadratic function over the block domain. This section gives two exact rational algorithms
for that problem. The first works for any bounded polytope of small dimension; the second works
for quadratic stars of any size whose constraints couple the center with one leaf at a time. Both
return an attained minimizer and a rational minimum, so they serve as exact support oracles and
give support inequalities that are tight.

Proposition 4.3 (Hardness in unrestricted dimension). Computing minx∈[0,1]n q(x) for rational

paths [37, Theorem 2].

Relation to forest algorithms. Del Pia and Khajavirad [37] solve box-constrained quadratic
programs on forests with O(n2 ) arithmetic operations. After an affine scaling of each variable
to [0, 1], a box-constrained star is such a forest, and their dynamic program rooted at the center
specializes to the box-constrained case of Theorem 4.4; in that case our contribution is only the
O(k log k) count, which replaces their O(k 2 ) merge at the root. Rows that couple the center with
a leaf are outside their model. A shear xi 7→ xi − ρy turns a leaf domain that is a parallelogram
with two vertical sides back into a box and keeps the star structure, but triangles, trapezoids with
nonparallel sides and polygons with more sides cannot be reduced in this way. Such rows also break
the structural property on which their algorithm rests: the conditional value of a leaf is then no
longer a minimum of affine functions of y over a fixed set, and it can have convex kinks. For example,
for x ∈ [0, 1] with rows x ≥ y − 12 and x ≥ 12 − y, the minimum of x is |y − 12 |. The second-order
cone descriptions of star hulls by Dey and Khajavirad [39] assume sign patterns of the square terms
and box domains, and the LP approximations of Bienstock and Muñoz [16] allow constraints but
are approximate. Our algorithm conditions on the center, as nonserial dynamic programming does
[11], and solves a one-parameter quadratic problem for each leaf, a special case of multiparametric
quadratic programming [9]; Bhathena et al. [15] use parametric dynamic programs of this kind on
trees for convex quadratic problems with indicators. To our knowledge, no exact algorithm for
nonconvex stars with center–leaf rows has been published.

convex leaf meets a bound or a concave leaf changes its preferred endpoint, and it then recomputes
every leaf rule on every piece. This costs O((m + k)3 ) operations, stores Θ(k) rules per piece, and can
produce more pieces than Theorem 4.4 requires. It certified the star cuts of our first implementation,
whose stars had at most four leaves (Appendix G), and those of the star blocks with up to 16 leaves

oracle certified every quadratic cut. Section 8.6 also compares the two star algorithms on random
stars with up to 104 leaves.


5     Cuts in the original variables
Proposition 2.1 produces inequalities in lifted coordinates (x, w). Using them requires variables
wj with wj = fj (x) in the model. If the solver’s representation does not already contain exactly
these variables, adding them changes the model: new equalities, new variables for branching and
propagation, and possibly a different presolve. Our first implementation used such a reformulation
and needed a control mode to separate its effect from that of the cuts (Appendix G). This section
shows how to avoid the auxiliary variables. The nonlinear functions are minimized jointly over the
block domain, and the rows of the model then eliminate them. The underlying facts are classical
Lagrangian duality; we state them in the form that the implementation and its certificates need.

5.1   Aggregating nonlinear rows
Let v ∈ RN be the vector of all model variables, including an objective variable if there is one, and
let x = Sv be a block of n of them, selected by a 0/1 matrix S. Suppose the model contains rows

                                           g(Sv) + Av ≤ b                                          (11)

with g : D → Rm and affine parts Av that may involve any variables, including block variables; an
equality is written as two opposite rows. Here D is a compact set that contains the block part Sv
of every point to which the cuts must apply, for example the block box intersected with the affine
rows that involve only block variables.

Proposition 5.1 (Aggregated support cut). Let a ∈ Rn , λ ∈ Rm

                                β ≤ φ(a, λ) := inf aT u + λT g(u) .                                (12)

Proof. At such a point, λ ≥ 0 gives λT g(Sv) ≤ λT (b − Av). Substitute this into aT Sv + λT g(Sv) ≥

    This is weak Lagrangian duality [23, Sections 5.1.3 and 5.2.2]. It needs neither convexity
nor optimal multipliers. The same inequality is the Lagrangian cut of a block in the extended
block-separable reformulation of Nowak [106, Section 7.1], after the block’s auxiliary variables
are eliminated with the rows (11); related cuts and range reductions appear in Tawarmalani and
Sahinidis [119], Karuppiah and Grossmann [73] and Domes and Neumaier [42], and cuts in the
original space obtained by projecting lifted quadratic relaxations are studied by Saxena et al. [111].
Its strength comes from minimizing the nonlinear functions jointly over their common domain before


                                                  16
they are eliminated. Nonnegative combinations of linear rows that are already in the relaxation
cannot cut off any point of it.
        An objective is handled through its epigraph. For minimization of g0 (x) + ℓ0 (v) + c0 with
objective variable t, the row g0 (x) + ℓ0 (v) − t ≤ −c0 is one of the rows (11); for maximization the
hypograph row −g0 (x) − ℓ0 (v) + t ≤ c0 is used. The two multipliers of the rows of an equality may
be combined into one free multiplier. Validity uses only Sv ∈ D and the selected rows. Integrality,
the other constraints and the objective play no role, so the cut is valid for the mixed-integer feasible
set and for its closed convex hull. If D is built from bounds valid only at a node of the search tree,
or from an incumbent cutoff, the cut is valid only there; our implementation uses only original
bounds and rows, so its cuts are global.
        The choice of D matters: affine rows that involve only block variables belong in D, not only in
the LP. On the triangle {(x, y) : x, y ≥ 0, x + y ≤ 1} every point satisfies x2 + 2xy + y 2 ≤ x + y and
xy ≤ 14 , because s = x + y ∈ [0, 1] gives s2 ≤ s and 4xy ≤ s2 . In the coordinates (mx , my , sx , sy , pxy )
these are linear inequalities valid for the hull of the quadratic graph over the triangle. The point
( 12 , 12 , 21 , 12 , 21 ), the average of the graph points at (0, 0) and (1, 1) of the box [0, 1]2 , lies in the hull
over the box and satisfies mx + my ≤ 1, but violates both inequalities. Intersecting the hull over the
box with the row is therefore strictly weaker than convexifying over the row-constrained domain, as
is known for factorable relaxations [2, 128, 130]. Here the first inequality is the sum of the RLT
products (1 − x − y)x ≥ 0 and (1 − x − y)y ≥ 0, so the example shows the weakness of the box
hull, not an advantage over RLT or semidefinite relaxations; for a single bilinear term, SCIP’s
projection-based envelopes [93] exploit linear rows in the same spirit.

5.2    What the cuts can express
Let K = conv{(u, g(u)) : u ∈ D} and Q = {0} × Rm      ≥0 . The affine map T (v) = (Sv, b − Av) places a
model point in the lifted space of the block. The next theorem identifies the set described by all
cuts (13). It is the set version of the classical fact that the Lagrangian dual bound of a nonconvex
problem equals the optimal value of its convexification in the joint space of variables and constraint
values [46, 48, 53, 82]; the separation argument is the one that Chen and Luedtke [29, Theorem 3] use
for Lagrangian cuts in two-stage integer programs. We include the short proof because our setting
has nonlinear rows and affine parts on arbitrary variables, and because no constraint qualification is
needed.

Theorem 5.2 (Closure of the aggregated cuts). Let D ⊂ Rn be nonempty and compact and let g be
continuous on D. A point v satisfies every inequality (13) with β = φ(a, λ), over all a ∈ Rn and
λ ∈ Rm≥0 , if and only if

                  v ∈ C := T −1 (K + Q) = v : ∃ y with (Sv, y) ∈ K and y ≤ b − Av .                              (14)
                                               


Proof. The set K is compact and convex, and Q is a closed convex cone, so U = K + Q is closed
and convex. A linear functional (a, λ) has finite infimum on U only if λ ≥ 0, and then the infimum
is φ(a, λ). Evaluating the valid inequality aT u + λT y ≥ φ(a, λ) of U at T (v) gives exactly (13), so
every point of C satisfies all cuts. Conversely, if T (v) ∈
                                                          / U , a linear functional strictly separates T (v)
from U ; its infimum on U is finite, so λ ≥ 0, and the corresponding cut is violated at v.

    If auxiliary variables w = g(x) were added, the joint-graph relaxation of the block would be
(x, w) ∈ K, w + Av ≤ b, and its projection onto v is C. The direct cuts can therefore express
exactly what this lifted relaxation expresses about the original variables, without adding variables
or equalities to the model, and no more. Relaxations that keep the rows inside the block, such as


                                                         17
the convex-hull relaxations of block-decomposition methods [106, Section 3], [128], can be strictly
stronger (Example D.2). There is also a cost: with the same linearization points, outer approximation
of a lifted formulation can be tighter than outer approximation in the original space [120], and a
finite set of direct cuts may need more members than a lifted description. For one row, C is the
familiar envelope relaxation. Write vex g and cav g for the convex and concave envelopes of a scalar
function g over D, so that vex g(x) = min{y : (x, y) ∈ K}.

Corollary 5.3 (One row). If m = 1, then C = {v : Sv ∈ conv D, vex g(Sv) + Av ≤ b}.
For an equality g(Sv) + Av = b, written as two rows, C is the set of v with Sv ∈ conv D and
vex g(Sv) ≤ b − Av ≤ cav g(Sv).

Proof. The projection of K onto x is conv D, and for each x ∈ conv D the fiber {y : (x, y) ∈ K} is
the interval [vex g(x), cav g(x)]. A suitable y in (14) exists if and only if vex g(Sv) ≤ b − Av. For the
equality, the lifted point of the two rows is (y, −y) with y in the same fiber, and the two conditions
y ≤ b − Av and −y ≤ −(b − Av) force y = b − Av.

    Joint treatment can only help when rows share block variables or when D couples them: if D is
a product D1 × D2 and the rows split into two groups whose nonlinear functions depend only on x1
and only on x2 , then C is the intersection of the closures of the two groups, because the convex hull
of a product of sets is the product of their convex hulls. When the rows do share variables, the gain
can be strict.

Example 5.4 (Two rows sharing one variable). Let D = [−1, 1] and consider the rows x2 −v1 ≤ 0 and
−x3 + v2 ≤ 0. The convex envelope of x2 is x2 , and the convex envelope of −x3 on [−1, 1] takes the
value −1/4 at x = 0 (on [−1/2, 1] it is the line through (1, −1) that is tangent to −x3 at x = −1/2).
By Corollary 5.3, the intersection of the two one-row closures contains (x, v1 , v2 ) = (0, 0, 1/4). The
aggregated cut with a = 0 and λ = (1, 1) uses minu∈[−1,1] (u2 − u3 ) = 0 and reads v1 − v2 ≥ 0, which
this point violates. In terms of (14), the only probability measure on [−1, 1] with mean 0 and second
moment 0 is the point mass at 0, whose third moment 0 is smaller than the required 1/4.

   That joint relaxations dominate separate envelopes is well known [106, Lemma 3.4], [83, 118];
the example makes the effect concrete for the cut family used here.

5.3   Relation to the hull of the feasible set
The closure C is a relaxation of the set Σ = {v : Sv ∈ D, v satisfies (11)}, so conv Σ ⊆ C. Equality
holds when every row has its own free direction in the affine parts, as in the setting of Liers et al.
[83] (Proposition D.1 in Appendix D). Without such free directions the gap can be strict, and it
is then not a failure of the direction search. For D = [0, 1] and the equality row x2 = 1/4, whose
feasible set is {1/2}, Corollary 5.3 gives C = {x : x2 ≤ 1/4 ≤ x} = [1/4, 1/2]: the point x = 1/4 is
the mean of the measure with mass 3/4 at 0 and 1/4 at 1, which satisfies the row in expectation,
so even the complete family of cuts accepts it. This is a Lagrangian duality gap [53, 106]. It
is closed by convexifying aggregated sets instead of aggregated functions, as in the aggregation
closure of Dey et al. [40], or by placing the row inside D, which changes the support problem; our
implementation keeps nonlinear rows out of D so that the support problems stay in the classes of
Section 4. Appendix D places C in a hierarchy with the surrogate relaxations of Müller et al. [94]
and shows that both inclusions can be strict.

6     Certified cuts and their replay
A separation routine has two parts. One part chooses a direction; the other proves a lower bound for
that direction. Only the second affects validity. This section states what must be checked for a cut
in the original variables to be valid as the solver stores it, and how a record of the check is replayed.

6.1   The stored row
Floating-point LP solvers store binary64 coefficients. Every finite binary64 number is a dyadic
rational, so the row that the solver uses is a rational inequality, and validity can be stated for that
row. For a cut of the form (13), four things must hold.

(C1) The direction (a, λ) is taken as the exact rational value of its binary64 representation, and a
     lower bound L ≤ φ(a, λ) over the whole domain D is certified.

(C2) The cut is assembled exactly: c = S T a − AT λ and r = L − λT b, with repeated occurrences of
     a variable combined.

(C3) The rational row cT v ≥ r is rounded to binary64 with a correction that keeps it valid
     (Proposition 6.1).

(C4) The row that the solver stores is the exported row.

By Proposition 5.1, (C1) and (C2) give a valid rational row, and (C3) and (C4) carry its validity
to the stored row. The direction itself may come from an LP over samples, from a heuristic
or from a previous iteration; none of these needs to be correct. The principle is established: a
numerically chosen affine bound needs no verification of how it was found, only a rigorous final shift
[51, Section 5], [50]; cut parameters may be chosen in floating point and the derivation then repeated
rigorously [102, p. 294]; and safe linear estimators are defined in terms of the machine coefficients
themselves [21]. The hazard at the last step is known to SCIP’s developers. Outside its exact mode,
SCIP 10 rounds a row coefficient that is integral within its tolerance to that integer and drops
coefficients below its epsilon, without adjusting the sides (functions rowAddCoef, rowChgCoefPos
and rowMerge in src/scip/lp.c of 122). Its nonlinear constraint handler therefore relaxes its own
cuts with variable bounds before storing them, in floating point, and rounds without correction
when a needed bound is infinite [12, Section 4.2.10]; cuts added by another separator receive no
such treatment. Two points are specific to support cuts: the bound must be certified for the final
direction on the whole block domain, which is a global nonconvex minimization, and the row that
SCIP stores must be compared with the certified row.

Proposition 6.1 (Safe export). Let cT v ≥ r be valid, let ĉ be binary64 coefficients and ∆ = ĉ − c,
and let ℓj ≤ vj ≤ uj be valid bounds, where ℓj is finite whenever ∆j > 0 and uj is finite whenever
∆j < 0. Put E = j:∆j >0 ∆j ℓj + j:∆j <0 ∆j uj . Then ĉT v ≥ r̂ is valid for every r̂ ≤ r + E.
                  P               P


Proof. ĉT v = cT v + ∆T v ≥ r + E.

     This is the bound-corrected rounding of safe LP bounds and safe cuts [34, 45, 102], applied
to the eliminated row. A coefficient that is exactly representable needs no bound, even on an
unbounded variable such as the objective variable. The correction must be applied after every
transformation that changes the row, including merging, scaling and the removal of tiny coefficients;
r̂ is rounded downward and compared with r + E in exact arithmetic. If a needed bound is infinite
or the correction destroys the violation, the row is discarded; a fixed safety shift does not replace the

calculation (Section 8.2 gives an example). The bounds must be global implications of the model
(Section 7.1). Our exporter is more conservative than the proposition: it requires both bounds of a
variable to be finite whenever the coefficient of that variable changes. For support inequalities in
lifted coordinates (x, w), as in our first implementation, bounds on w need not be available, and
the direct form is used instead: if L ≤ minx∈D {âT x + b̂T F (x)} for the exact values of the stored
coefficients (â, b̂) and r̂ ≤ L, then âT x + b̂T w ≥ r̂ is valid for HD (F ) by Proposition 2.1.

6.2   Lower bounds on the whole domain
For quadratic blocks, (C1) is computed exactly by the oracles of Section 4. For polynomial remainders
(the nonlinear part of a row, Section 7.2) in one or two variables we use Bernstein enclosures on
a subdivision of the block box, computed in exact rational arithmetic [50, 52, 95]; for univariate
elementary expressions, outward-rounded ball arithmetic [70] on interval cells, strengthened by a
chord correction from a verified bound on the second derivative (Appendix E). A certificate depends
on two hypotheses. The cells must cover the box exactly, starting from the trusted box and not from
a list supplied by the generator, and a bound proved on a smaller domain is never reused on a larger
one. Domain requirements, such as a positive argument of log or a nonzero denominator, are proved
on every cell; they are collected from the raw source expression before any symbolic simplification
(Section 7.1), and every original feature is enclosed on the whole cell before features are combined,
so that cancellation between features cannot hide a requirement. The chord correction is used only
on cells where the expression is twice continuously differentiable and a finite bound on its second
derivative is verified. A bound computation returns one of four outcomes: a certified bound with its
partition; empty, when every cell is excluded by a domain row, so that D = ∅; unsupported, for an
operation outside the grammar; or incomplete, when a work limit is reached. Only the first produces
a cut. An incomplete computation says nothing about the point being separated; in particular, it is
not evidence that the point lies in the hull.

6.3   Records and replay
Each cut is accepted through the same sequence: certify the support bound for the binary64 direction
(C1); eliminate the nonlinear rows exactly and merge coefficients (C2); apply the correction of
Proposition 6.1 (C3); and read back the row that SCIP has created from the cut, with its columns,
coefficients, constant, sides and scope, before it is passed to SCIP’s separation storage, and compare
it with the certified row after mapping original to transformed variables (C4). Any difference,
such as a coefficient rounded by SCIP or a variable aggregated or removed by presolve, rejects the
cut. Accepted rows are passed to SCIP as global, removable cuts with forcecut set. SCIP’s later
handling of the row, such as converting a cut in a single variable into a bound change, is outside
this check.
    The record of a cut contains the serialized source model, the signed source rows and multipliers,
the proven bounds with their derivations, the support domain, the direction, the support certificate
(the complete candidate enumeration, the star partition or the subdivision with its bounds), the
eliminated row, the rounding correction and the stored SCIP row. Replay runs in a fresh process.
It verifies the hashes of the archived sources and model, re-executes the model builder (which
constructs, but does not solve, a SCIP model through PySCIPOpt) and the bound derivations,
rebuilds every signed source side from the archived model with its own affine splitter, repeats the
exact support calculation for the recorded binary64 direction on the rebuilt features and domain,
recomputes elimination and rounding, and compares the result with the stored row as recorded
by the separator; the comparison with SCIP’s row itself is made only when the cut is generated.

7     Implementation in SCIP
We implemented the cuts as a separator for SCIP 10.0.2 [68], written in Python on top of
PySCIPOpt 6.2.1 [87], with exact arithmetic in Python fractions and SymPy, ball arithmetic
in Arb through python-flint, and HiGHS for the direction LPs. The separator adds global cuts at
the root node. It does not change SCIP’s constraint handlers, presolve, propagation or branching,
and every native nonlinear constraint stays in the model. Figure 2 shows the data flow. This section
describes the parts on which validity depends and the choices that shape the computational results;
Appendix H lists the work limits.

7.1   A faithful model
A cut certificate refers to the model that the solver solves. Two ordinary steps of model loading,
assembling expressions and simplifying them, can change that model without notice. We guard
against both in every mode, including the native baseline, so that all modes solve the same model.
We also describe the bounds that certificates use.



                                                  21
Arithmetic. Every finite binary64 number in the source file is interpreted as its exact rational
value. Assembling a native expression can still change values: in (0.1x)2 the source coefficient is
the exact square of the binary64 number 0.1, whereas a coefficient computed in floating point is
that square rounded again, and a constraint constructor may move a constant to the other side
with a rounded subtraction. After assembly, the builder reads back the PySCIPOpt expression
object that it passes to SCIP and compares its exact value with the source expression by symbolic
subtraction and expansion, with no tolerance. If they differ, it rebuilds the expression as an
explicit expression graph with separate constant nodes and compares again; a remaining difference
rejects the model. Constants are kept inside the expression, so the constructor cannot round them
into the row sides, which are passed unchanged. This is translation validation [99, 108] of the
model import. It addresses the observation of Neumaier [101, Section 20] that modeling systems
introduce uncontrolled rounding, and the requirement of Schichl and Neumaier [112, Section 3.1]
that simplification of an expression graph introduce no roundoff. Its boundary is the expression
object; PySCIPOpt’s translation of it into SCIP’s internal expression, and SCIP’s later simplification,
presolve and floating-point LP, are outside it.

Domains. Simplification can silently enlarge a domain: 0 · log x, x/x, ( x)2 and (log x)0 are
defined only for x > 0, x ̸= 0, x ≥ 0 and x > 0, respectively. The builder visits every node of the
source expression before simplification and proves each domain requirement from bounds and from
affine rows; for example, x − y = 1 proves that log(x − y) is defined, with no bounds on x and y.

is rejected, because imposing b > 0 could remove points at which the power is defined for other

7.2    Blocks, directions and activation
Discovery runs at the first separator call, so a model solved during presolve pays nothing. A ranged
row ℓ ≤ g(v) ≤ u has two sides, g(v) ≤ u and −g(v) ≤ −ℓ. Discovery writes each finite side of
each original row, including the objective epigraph row, exactly as a nonlinear remainder plus an
affine part. A side is admissible if its remainder involves one to four variables with finite proven


                                                  22
bounds and is either a polynomial of total degree at most eight or a nonpolynomial expression in one
variable. Blocks are the variable sets of single sides and greedy unions of overlapping sides, with at
most four variables, six nonlinear sides and sixteen signed affine domain sides; at most 32 blocks are
kept, the largest first and ties broken by the indices of their variables. Unsupported remainders are
declined as a whole, and a remainder is never altered. Affine rows that involve only block variables,
together with the proven bounds, form the support domain D. The certification kernels apply to
the whole block: exact enumeration (Theorem 4.1) when the combined function is quadratic and the
number of row subsets is at most 2,000, the star oracle (the simpler implementation described at
the end of Section 4.2) when the enumeration budget is exceeded and the block is a star, Bernstein
bounds for polynomials in a block of one or two variables (at most 1,024 tensor coefficients), and
ball arithmetic in a block of one variable. With at most sixteen affine sides the enumeration budget
is never exceeded in dimension three or less; for four variables it allows at most seven signed affine
sides. A direction whose combined function falls outside these cases yields no cut.
     At an LP solution the separator tries, for each block and each of its source sides j, the remainder
direction a = 0, λ = ej . It bounds only the side’s nonlinear remainder; the side’s affine terms in
the block variables enter the cut only through the elimination. Two variants try other directions
first. The whole-row direction of side j has λ = ej and a equal to the side’s coefficients of the block
variables, so that its support value is the minimum of the whole side over D. The block direction
has λj = 1 for every source side of the block and a equal to the sum of their coefficients of the block
variables; its support value is the minimum of the sum of the block’s rows. After these directions
the separator solves up to three LPs that separate the current point, after coordinate scaling, from
the convex hull of sampled graph points plus the cone of the side coordinates; an LP direction is
used only if its scaled violation exceeds 10−5 . Samples are grid points of the box (33 points in one
variable, 7 × 7 in two, 3d in d = 3, 4), plus, for quadratic blocks, the exact vertices of the domain
polytope and their centroid when the enumeration budget allows. Each direction is tried at most
once per block and run. The binary64 values of a and λ are then certified. For a non-quadratic
block, the subdivision is refined only until the bound makes the row violated by the threshold at
the current point; if it does not, the call counts as a failed certification. When an exact support
computation returns a minimizer that the samples missed, the minimizer is added and the LP is
solved again.
     Two modes add cuts. Mode all tries the admitted blocks in their fixed order until a cap is
reached. Mode auto admits only quadratic blocks that contain a side not proved convex and that
have either an affine domain row that is not a bound or at least two distinct nonlinear rows; it
requires a larger violation and stops trying a block after two failed certifications. Both modes run in
at most three root callbacks, cap the number of cuts and support calls (default limits, Appendix H),
and give all separator work a cumulative allowance of min{1 s, 0.05 T } for a total budget T , checked
between operations; a single symbolic operation can overrun it. A cut is added only if its violation
divided by max{1, ∥ĉ∥1 } exceeds 10−5 in all and 5 · 10−4 in auto. The separator runs before SCIP’s
own separators in each root round, and its rows are added with forcecut, so they bypass SCIP’s
cut selection. Mode baseline uses the same model builder and adds nothing. The experiments also
use raised limits (128 blocks, 200 cuts) on MINLPLib models and, on the constructed families of
Section 8.5, limits proportional to the number n of copies: base limits (4n cuts, n per callback) and
wide limits (16n cuts, 4n per callback); all of these allow ten root callbacks, and the larger cut caps
of Section 8.5 forty (Table 7). For the star experiment of Section 8.6, discovery can also form star
blocks: a center variable together with up to sixteen variables that share two-variable sides with it,
all these sides, and the affine rows among these variables. Star blocks precede the other blocks; for
blocks of more than four variables the separator tries only the block direction and the whole-row
directions, without sampling, and the star oracle certifies them.

8     Computational study
The experiments address four questions: whether the stored cuts are valid in practice, and what
certification prevents; whether the cuts change SCIP’s results on benchmark models, including
models that SCIP finds hard; what the separator does and what it costs; and how much of the
root gap the cuts close on instances with the structure of Sections 3 and 4, compared with SCIP’s
own alternatives and with Gurobi. We report five campaigns. Each was run with solver code,
limits and protocol fixed before its first run; we call these results prospective, and we label analyses
designed after results were seen as post hoc. Campaigns 1 and 2 used earlier implementations and
are summarized in Appendix G. Campaign 3 tests the final implementation on MINLPLib models
(Parts 3A and 3B) and on a constructed family (Part 3C); a post hoc diagnostic of its results
is Part 3P. Campaign 4 was designed after campaign 3 to answer the questions that its results

8.1    Setup
All runs use SCIP 10.0.2 through PySCIPOpt 6.2.1 and Python 3.13, with one SCIP thread and one
BLAS thread per process, on a Linux host (WSL2) with 18 cores and 36 hardware threads that was
shared with unrelated jobs. The one-minute load average was 14 to 21 during the prospective runs
of campaign 3 (2 to 21 during Part 3P), 2 to 9 during campaign 4 (5th to 95th percentile; at most 15,
in Part 4C4) and 1 to 20 during campaign 5, apart from a spike to 126 during Part 5S (Section 8.6).
Within a part, at most six runs were active at a time, and the modes of one instance ran back
to back in rotated order; the Part 3P runs on the path family overlapped the last 28 minutes of
Part 3A, when up to ten runs were active. We compare times only within these paired runs of one
part. Part 4C2 has no baseline of its own and is compared with the campaign-3 runs of the same
instances; root bounds of identical runs repeated exactly across campaigns (all 100 baseline root
bounds rerun in Part 3P), whereas solved counts and times are not paired across campaigns, and
we say so where it matters. Timings are descriptive. Times are in-process wall times charged to
the time budget (model build, discovery, separation and solve), excluding interpreter start-up and
the primal check; SCIP time excluding the callback is SCIP’s solving time minus the time spent in
the separator callback. Full runs have a budget of 300 s; root runs have a node limit of one and a
budget of 60 s (Parts 3A, 3B, 4B2) or 120 s (path family, Parts 4D and 5S). In Parts 3A and 3B a
seed is SCIP’s random seed shift; constructed instances are identified by their generator seed and
run with SCIP’s seed 0.
     We write SCIP for SCIP with default settings, SCIP-nolocks for SCIP with its implicit-
discreteness presolve step switched off (constraints/nonlinear/checkvarlocks = d), and SCIP-
extra for SCIP with its disabled-by-default nonconvex separators switched on (Section 2.1); all three
use our model builder and add no certified cut. Gurobi 13.0.3 [61] solves the same model with
one thread, its nonconvex mode (NonConvex=2) and the same gap limit. The separator modes are
listed in Table 7. Models come from MINLPLib [28, 125] in OSiL format, the XML instance format
of Optimization Services (Appendix H), stratified by the library’s metadata into convex models,
nonconvex models without integer variables, and nonconvex models with integer variables.

8.3    MINLPLib models
On MINLPLib models the cuts were neutral for SCIP: they changed neither which models it solved
nor its own solving time. Part 3A repeats the 30 models of campaign 2 (ten per stratum, selected
by a hash ordering from 422 eligible models with at most 120 variables) with a 300-second budget,
seeds 0, 1 and 2, and root runs. Part 3B uses a sample selected by structure: from the 392 eligible
models not used in campaign 2, the 111 on which discovery finds a block that the auto rule admits,
ranked by a hash, of which we take the first 30, with seeds 0 and 1. The root runs of both parts
include mode all-diag, whose raised limits test whether the default limits, rather than the cuts,
bound their effect. SCIP solves most of these models in about a second, so they can show validity
and cost but hardly a benefit. Part 4D addresses this. Its pool consists of the 322 MINLPLib models
with 121 to 1,000 variables that are not convex by the library’s metadata, contain a quadratic or
polynomial function and were not used before. Of these, 85 have a block that auto admits, and 58
of those are not solved by SCIP within 60 seconds; Part 4D takes the first 20 of the 58 in a fixed
hash order (Appendix H). Table 3 summarizes the full runs; Tables 9, 10 and 11 in Appendix H
give the results per model.

8.4   What the separator does and what it costs
Table 8 in Appendix H follows the certified rows from the support calls to the cuts that reach SCIP.
Three observations explain most of the results of Section 8.3.
    First, most certified supports are not violated at the LP point: on the MINLPLib models, 65
to 86% of the certified rows fell below the violation threshold, because SCIP’s relaxation already
implied them up to the threshold. Certification failed in few support calls. In the campaign-3 reruns
that recorded the outcome, every failure was incomplete (a Bernstein or ball-arithmetic bound
reached its depth budget, or did not certify the required value) or unsupported (a quadratic block
whose number of row subsets exceeded the enumeration budget and which the fallback bounds do
not cover); none was an empty domain. In Part 4D, 307 of the 313 failures came from one model.
    Second, the stored-row check lost a substantial share of the violated rows on MINLPLib models,
mainly to presolve aggregation (Section 8.3), and a small share on the constructed families, where
SCIP dropped coefficients below its epsilon of 10−9 in at most 3% of the violated rows. Each such
loss would be a silent change of a certified row if the check were absent. A separator that declares
its variables non-aggregatable before presolve avoids most of the presolve losses; we did this only as
an experiment (Part 4B2), because it changes SCIP’s own relaxation. A separator that works in the
presolved space would instead have to treat SCIP’s reductions, which are made in floating point,
as part of the model that the certificate refers to. The losses to dropped and snapped coefficients
could be avoided by applying SCIP’s coefficient handling in the exporter, followed by the correction
of Proposition 6.1.
    Third, on the MINLPLib models the whole slowdown is the separator’s own time. SCIP’s solving
time without the callback was unchanged in every part: its shifted geometric mean differed from
SCIP’s by at most 4% in Parts 3A and 3B, and the median per-run ratio was between 0.96 and 1.01
in Parts 4B2 and 4D. The callback cost about half a second per run in Part 3B, more than SCIP’s
whole solving time on most of these models (median 0.16 s). In campaign 3, exact certification took
70% of the callback time on Part 3B, the direction search and discovery the rest; on the larger
models of Part 4D, discovery took 85% of the callback time in mode all. All of this is Python
code; a compiled implementation would reduce it, but on these models it would at best break even,
because the cuts did not reduce SCIP’s work. On the constructed families, in contrast, the cuts
reduced SCIP’s own time sharply (Section 8.5), and the callback was the dominant remaining cost.

The prospective separator and the attribution. The separator of Part 3C (remainder

8.6    Stars
The blocks of Sections 8.3–8.5 have at most four variables, so the star algorithm of Theorem 4.4 was
not needed there. We test it in two ways: offline at large scale, and inside the solver on a family
whose merged blocks are stars with up to 16 leaves.

The algorithm at scale (Part 4S). On random constrained stars with k ∈ {10, 100, 1000, 10000}
leaves (five instances each), 2k center–leaf rows, one to three rows in the center alone and small
rational coefficients of both signs, we compared an implementation of the sweep of the proof with
the simpler star oracle of our separator (Section 4.2). Where both ran (k ≤ 1000), the two minima
were equal as rational numbers, and on all 20 instances the sweep’s minimizer was exactly feasible
and attained the reported value. The sweep took a median of 1.3 ms, 12 ms, 0.17 s and 2.1 s of CPU
time for the four values of k, close to linear growth, whereas the simpler oracle took 6 ms, 0.43 s
and 29 s for k ≤ 1000, about 70 times more per tenfold increase in k; at k = 104 it was not run,
because its extrapolated time exceeded ten minutes per instance. The minima had numerators and
denominators of at most 94 and 78 bits, with no steady growth in k, in line with the size bounds of
Appendix C.

8.7   Summary and limitations
Every one of the 274,489 recorded cuts passed replay against the source model and the row stored
by SCIP. Constants taken from the separator’s samples would have cut off feasible points on seven
MINLPLib models and on 58 of the 60 sampled path-family instances, and constants improved by
local minimization still did so on two models and 19 instances; a numerical global solve was almost
always right, but not always. On MINLPLib models the cuts were neutral for SCIP: they changed
neither which models it solved in campaigns 2 to 4 nor its own solving time, the Python separator
made the runs slower, and SCIP’s disabled-by-default separators improved the root bound more
often than the certified cuts did. On the constructed families, exact block support closed the root
gap that SCIP leaves. With a non-binding coupling row, a separator configuration chosen after the
campaign-3 diagnostic and fixed before the fresh instances were generated solved all 20 of them,
against at most 10 for each SCIP setting and 5 for Gurobi. With a binding row, both direction
rules solved all instances, but so did SCIP without implicit discreteness, faster. On the star family,
star blocks certified by the star oracle made the root bound exact, which neither blocks of four
variables nor any SCIP setting achieved; the solved count was then limited by SCIP’s search for
good solutions, and Gurobi solved more instances. We therefore do not recommend enabling the
separator by default; its value is on models whose joint block structure SCIP’s relaxation misses,
and an activation rule that recognizes such models is not available.

Limitations. The MINLPLib parts use models with at most 1,000 variables, and their blocks have
at most four variables; star blocks occur only in the constructed star family. The implementation
is in Python, so the measured cost is an upper bound on that of a compiled separator. The host
was shared, with different loads in different campaigns and one load spike during Part 5S, so we
compare times only within paired runs of one part, and time differences of a few percent are not


                                                      34
meaningful [85]. Runs used one seed, except Parts 3A and 3B, and one thread. Gurobi was run
with one thread and default settings apart from its nonconvex mode. The constructed families favor
block cuts; the post hoc diagnostic of campaign 3 was designed after its results were seen, and its
finding was then tested prospectively on fresh instances.


9    Discussion and conclusions
Two points of the theory matter for solver design. First, pair hulls glued on finitely many moments
of a shared variable accept distributions that no joint distribution reproduces, and they give no
positive bound exactly when the local zero sets alternate often enough. On three-variable paths,
dense moment relaxations with the missing product close these gaps, so there the advantage of
joint blocks over their pairs is one of representation, which support cuts obtain in the model’s own
coordinates. For larger blocks this is no longer true: on a four-variable path, and with a relative gap
of nearly 100% on the full-gap instances of Proposition 3.4 with five and seven variables, an exact
support cut of the block is strictly stronger than the dense first-level relaxation with all products.
Second, for stars, the merged blocks of Section 3, the support problem remains exactly solvable in
nearly linear time when rows couple the center with one leaf at a time, and, as in safe-cut practice,
certification does not constrain how directions are found: only the final binary64 row, its bound
over the whole block domain and the row that the solver stores need to be checked, and all of this
can be replayed.
    The computations add two findings to the theory. First, certification matters in practice. The
sample minimum would have given wrong constants for 38% of the MINLPLib cuts and for almost
all sampled path-family cuts, and local minimization still left wrong constants that cut off feasible
solutions, including known optima. A numerical global solve was a much better substitute on these
small blocks, but on one model it reported optimality for a constant 1.8 · 10−4 above the true
minimum, which a fixed safety shift of 10−6 does not cover. Second, the stored-row check exposed
an obstacle that the theory does not see: presolve aggregates or fixes the variables to which a block
refers, so a separator stated in the original variables loses cuts unless it works in the presolved space
or prevents these reductions, and preventing them weakens SCIP’s own relaxation.
    On MINLPLib models the cuts did not help SCIP: they improved its root bound on few models
and changed no full solve, while SCIP’s disabled-by-default separators achieved as much or more.
On the constructed families, where the theory predicts a gap, the picture is different. With a
non-binding coupling row, native SCIP stopped at the level of glued pair relaxations in all three
settings we ran, and the certified cuts went beyond it when the separator could add enough cuts per
block and tried the exact support of each whole row first; this configuration solved every instance,
against at most half of them for each SCIP setting and for Gurobi. With a binding row, both
direction rules solved every instance, and the cuts that mattered came from the LP direction search;
but SCIP with its implicit-discreteness presolve step switched off solved every instance as well, and
faster, because its own relaxation already captured most of the gap. On the star family, star blocks
certified by the star oracle made the root bound exact, whereas blocks of four variables added almost
nothing to SCIP’s bound; the solved count was then limited by SCIP’s search for good solutions,
where Gurobi did better. Joint blocks larger than the separator’s default of four variables thus
matter, and the near-linear star algorithm makes them cheap to certify.
    Several questions remain open. Exact support for trees deeper than stars with edge-local rows
would extend the star algorithm to the structures of network models; Remark 4.5 shows that the
rational partition argument does not extend directly. Proposition 3.5(ii) reduces the gap of the
glued relaxation in any direction to a concave maximization over re-splittings, but which directions


                                                   35
to merge, and when merging pays off, is not answered by it. A direction search with the guarantees
of Appendix F and the speed of a solver callback is not available. Finally, an activation rule that
predicts when joint cuts pay off would be needed before any default use; our structural rule did not
achieve this.

A.3    Dense moment matrices for the path family
We use the notation of Theorem 3.2, assume that A and C interleave, and let v ∈ R be a point with
lin Φ(v) = 0. Let M be the moment matrix of (1, x, y, z) with the entries of v and an unspecified
entry pxz .
    The dense moment matrix alone does not exclude v. The specified entries of M form a chordal
pattern whose two maximal blocks, the moment matrices of (1, x, y) and (1, y, z), are positive
semidefinite because the two parts of v are moment vectors. Hence M has a positive semidefinite
completion [57], and v together with this completion satisfies the dense moment constraint and all
RLT inequalities of the edges and squares.

Relation to the ellipsoid method. In the oracle framework of Grötschel et al. [59], weak
separation is obtained from weak optimization for every convex body, without radius information.
Convex bodies are full-dimensional by definition there, however, and the weak separation problem
asks only for a hyperplane that is almost valid on the points deep inside the body. For a lower-
dimensional hull this requirement is vacuous, and an almost valid hyperplane is not a cut. The
contract above is different: the cut side is exact, only the membership side is approximate, and
lower-dimensional hulls are allowed; the same contract appears in Brierley et al. [25].

Outcomes and a reference implementation. The procedure returns cut with an exact rational
row, within tolerance with a certificate, or empty domain when exact vertex enumeration of P
finds no vertex. A bounded variant, which caps the number of oracle calls and net points, may in
addition return unresolved, which carries no geometric information. A cut needs a lower bound
over the whole domain, whereas a within-tolerance answer needs only feasible points and exact
arithmetic. A rational cut can be exported in binary64 with the correction of Proposition 6.1, using
enclosures of the features; rounding to nearest preserves the signs of the cone coefficients. Our
reference implementation solves the master LP numerically, makes the returned weights nonnegative
and renormalizes them in exact arithmetic, checks the resulting mixture exactly, and certifies every
proposed direction with the exact oracle, so an inaccurate LP can waste work but cannot produce a
wrong answer. Completeness is guaranteed by a grid fallback over CJ , with values −1 + 2i/N in the
free coordinates, i/N in the cone coordinates, i = 0, . . . , N , and N = ⌈2ρ/(ϵ − θ)⌉; every direction is
within ℓ∞ distance 1/N of this grid, so if no grid direction yields a cut, Lemma F.1 bounds maxCJ ψ
by θ + ρ/N ≤ ϵ, and the within-tolerance answer is then one exactly feasible point per grid direction.
An exact version with a rational simplex method for (16) is part of the verification scripts (see Code
and data availability); on the test instances it needed at most six oracle calls, while the bound of
Theorem F.2 ranged from 102 to 1014 . For aggregated cuts, a returned rational row maps to an
original-variable cut through Proposition 5.1; a within-tolerance answer bounds a distance in the
lifted coordinates (x, g), not a distance in the original variables to the set C of Theorem 5.2.


G     The first two campaigns
Both campaigns were run with code, limits and protocol fixed in advance. Their records were
audited independently from the raw run records (418 checks of the original reports, all passed), and
the statistics below were recomputed from the same records.


                                                   54
Campaign 1: lifted cuts with auxiliary variables. The first implementation added an
auxiliary variable w = f (x) for each selected expression of one or two variables and separated
support cuts in (x, w) (Section 6.1). Its oracles were the polytope oracle of Theorem 4.1 in dimension
two, the star oracle of Section 4.2, Bernstein bounds and ball arithmetic. Because the auxiliary
variables change the model, it was compared with a control mode that adds them without cuts. The
automatic mode also used an inexpensive screening certificate, a dual-norm bound on the violation
of any cut computed from a certified convex combination of graph points [88]; it allowed only 2
of 506 attempted skips and was dropped afterwards. The campaign used 24 MINLPLib models
selected by a fixed hash ordering (Appendix H), 13 constructed mechanism cases (small models
with shared expressions and linking rows, built so that joint support cuts close a gap that separate
envelopes leave), a historical suite of four models from earlier development, a six-second budget and
a two-second root budget.
     Of the 24 models, 20 passed the model import in every mode; three were rejected because a
domain restriction could not be proved on the declared box, and one failed with an unsupported
variable power. Baseline and control solved 19 of the 20, both cut modes 18; the lost model,
genpooling_lee2, was solved by the baseline in 5.6 s and by the control in 4.9 s, and reached the
6 s limit in both cut modes. On the 18 models solved in every mode, the shifted geometric mean
time was 0.26 s for the baseline, 0.41 s for the control, 0.57 s for all and 0.46 s for auto. On the
mechanism cases, the control and both cut modes solved all 13 and the baseline 12, so the additional
solve is explained by the reformulation, not by the cuts. On the historical model waterno2_06 the
final dual bound after 6 s was 26.59 for the baseline and 21.17 for the control, against 7.05 (all) and
2.41 (auto) for this minimization model. The 1,082 recorded cuts (450 from the two-dimensional
polytope oracle, 125 from the star oracle, 460 from Bernstein bounds and 47 from ball arithmetic)
all passed replay, with 12 of 12 corrupted records rejected, and all 270 returned incumbents passed
the original-model check. The auxiliary variables thus cost time on their own, and the cuts did not
recover it; this motivated the original-variable cuts of Section 5.

Campaign 2: cuts in the original variables. The second implementation is, up to the repairs
described below, the one of Section 7. Its campaign used 30 new MINLPLib models, ten per stratum,
selected by a fixed hash ordering from the 422 eligible models that had not been used before, with a
30-second budget, root runs with one node, 13 mechanism cases, ten diagnostic models from the
first campaign, and seed-one repeats on six models (282 runs). All 30 models were admitted, and
every mode solved the same 25. The five unsolved models received no cut in any mode. Cuts were
added on 7 models in mode all (38 cuts) and on 3 in mode auto (10 cuts), all of which the baseline
also solved; four of them were solved at the root in every mode. At the relative tolerance 10−4 no
final or root dual bound was better in a cut mode than in the baseline; four time-limited models had
worse final bounds in both cut modes, and graphpart_clique-40 had a worse root bound in both
cut modes. At a tolerance of 10−6 two models appeared better and up to seven worse, differences
of the same size as the variation between identical baseline reruns. On the 25 commonly solved
models the shifted geometric mean time rose from 0.55 s to 0.99 s (all) and 0.96 s (auto), and each
cut mode was more than 10% slower on 21 of the 25 (both on 19). On the 30 full runs of mode all,
discovery accounted for 24.4 of the 27.6 callback seconds; direction LPs took 0.7 s and certification
2.4 s. On the diagnostic model btest14 the final bound was −72.30 for the baseline and −115.60 in
both cut modes.
     The campaign exposed two defects of the frozen code. On two diagnostic models, symbolic
polynomial conversion over all model symbols crashed during discovery (four worker errors, with
unknown cut logs), and discovery did not check the time allowance between rows, so it exceeded

the allowance in 46 cut-mode runs, by up to 19 s. Both were repaired; the repair also added two
guards, rejecting rows whose side reaches SCIP’s infinity value (all 123 recorded rows of campaign 2
