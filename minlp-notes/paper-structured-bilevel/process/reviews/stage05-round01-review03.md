The ternary residual margin dominates the later-coordinate feedback and the combined auxiliary/clause perturbation. 7. **New padded identity-Hessian bilevel extension** — Section 5, lines 617–706; Appendix D, lines 106–170. The weighted slack products telescope to `F=L-z_n^2`, and nonnegativity forces one endpoint per coordinate at every zero. The slab's forward `1/8` error and reverse `3/8<1` integer argument are valid. Distinct terminal values have the advertised common denominator, giving a squared score separation. The convex-combination estimate controls arbitrary feasible points, not merely comparisons among vertices; the quadratic perturbation and price perturbation preserve a strict optimum on a nontrivial interval. Multiplying the entire objective by `1/tau` preserves responses and yields exactly the identity Hessian with polynomial-bit data.

   For fixed local coefficients, zero padding bits produce the required free-coordinate approximation. The exposure inequality was proved on the entire original path polytope, so it remains valid after adding padding bounds, even though those bounds may create additional vertices of the restricted polytope. Conversely, `F<=0` still forces an original vertex and its padding bits must be zero. Thus the added vertices do not create false yes instances. The certificate is a polynomial-size bit pattern with its explicit exposing leader. Fixed alphabets cover local inequalities and right-hand sides, not aggregate weights or scaled linear costs.

   The nonconvex upper quadratic row is identified explicitly. Removing it makes the slab-only problem feasible for every allowed integer target by continuity and the endpoint/free-bit weighted-sum bounds. The optimization variant attains its minimum on a compact feasible graph. No all-linear-upper feasibility hardness or strong hardness is claimed.

8. **Equal-gain strip projection** — Appendix D, lines 174–269. On each realizable gain-sign condition, normalization gives two-way difference constraints with nonnegative backtracking cycles. The all-pairs lower-versus-propagated-upper conditions are necessary and sufficient; the minimum-of-upper-cones formula recovers a witness. Retained values can be added as both lower and upper bounds without discarding original bounds. Zero gains correctly add bounds to the original head and split the forest. Reciprocal gains in arbitrarily oriented trees preserve the argument. Retaining endpoints of a fixed total number of deleted edges handles fixed total cycle rank; a per-block cycle-rank claim is correctly excluded. Products and denominator clearing have polynomial numerical degree and coefficient length in fixed parameter dimension. Recovery uses only comparisons and rational functions in the retained field. The conclusion distinguishes finite infima from attained minima and excludes objectives on unretained states.

9. **Curvature and arithmetic boundaries** — Section 5, lines 712–800. Both Boolean reductions concern existential, optimistic feasibility, with valid assignment certificates for the restricted families. They do not transfer to universal response semantics. In each new one-power example, the guarantee forces a positive leader no larger than `(5/8)^P`; any reduced rational therefore has denominator at least `(8/5)^P`. The stated strict anchor and convex reduced inequality are valid and do not remove this numeric-degree dependence. The separate sparse upper equation has degree `2^t` by Eisenstein and a short sparse input. The text preserves the distinction between these examples and the one-power affine-objective/no-upper-row subclass that these examples do not settle.

   The multiquadratic induction proves degree `2^N` for the prime-radical sum, while the cubic followers have the stated uniform positive curvature on their intervals. The singleton quadratic local sets are convex as sets, although their displayed quadratic equality is not a convex inequality representation. The manuscript does not confuse those two notions or assert a polynomial convex-QCQP encoding. Exact Square Root Sum comparison is used as an arithmetic boundary, not as an NP-hardness theorem. The distinction between common-field/minimal-polynomial output and a concise list of radicals is explicit.
