\section{Polynomial encoding with few nonlinear quadratic inequalities}
\label{sec:fixedcount}
Let $k$ bound the number of native inequalities nonlinear in the continuous
variables after any integer assignment is fixed. Affine constraints and
the quadratic objective are not counted in $k$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:fixedcount}
Under the assumptions of Section~\ref{sec:upper}, there is an integer
$\rho\ge1$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixedbits}
 \operatorname{bit}(\rho)\le N^{O(k+1)}
\end{equation}
that is minimizer-set exact at multiplier zero for either the infinity
norm or the one-norm. The exponent has an absolute constant, independent
of the numbers of continuous variables, affine rows and integer assignments.
\end{theorem}
The proof allows singular quadratic Hessians and degenerate affine
geometry. Its main step is an optimal-value bound without a constraint
qualification. Few-quadratic algebraic methods have direct precedents:
\citet[Theorem~1.2]{GrigorievPasechnik2005} give sampling and coefficient
bounds for quadratic maps, while
\citet[Sections~6.3--7.2 and~8.5]{KammingaRudolph2025} give reduced-variable
optimization formulas for a bounded solution set and an explicit application
to QCQP with a fixed total number of constraints under that boundedness
hypothesis. Our convex affine-face reduction below
allows arbitrary affine rows. The value bound is a consequence of
established algebraic tools; the result here is its exact-penalty
application and the explicit dependence on the nonlinear count.

\subsection{A nonzero optimal-value bound}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:nonzerovalue}
Minimize a rational convex quadratic objective over a nonempty set
described by a rational polyhedron with finite coordinate bounds and at
most $k$ convex quadratic inequalities. Let $L\ge2$ be its explicit
input length and $\theta$ its optimum. Then $\theta$ is a root of a
nonzero integer polynomial of degree and coefficient bitsize
$L^{O(k+1)}$. In particular, if $\theta\ne0$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nonzero}
 |\theta|\ge2^{-L^{O(k+1)}}.
\end{equation}
No Slater condition is required.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We first remove affine inequalities without changing the optimum, then
eliminate the primal variables from a regularized KKT system.

\emph{Affine-face reduction.}
Let $x^*$ be an optimum and let $H$ be the affine space defined by all
original equalities and all affine inequalities active at $x^*$.
The remaining affine rows have positive slack in a neighborhood of $x^*$
relative to $H$. Deleting these rows leaves $x^*$ globally optimal over
$H$ and the nonlinear inequalities: a point of lower objective value
would, by convexity, give lower values along its segment to $x^*$,
including in that neighborhood, contradicting original optimality.

Choose independent equations defining $H$. Rational elimination gives
$x=x_0+Vu$ with polynomial coefficient bitsize in $L$. The free coordinates
$u\in\R^d$ can be chosen among the coordinates of $x$. If $d=0$, the
objective value is rational of polynomial bit length and the assertion
follows. Otherwise let an integer $B\ge1$ of polynomial bit length bound
the original coordinates. Then $\norm{u^*}_2^2\le dB^2$.
Add the ball $\norm u_2^2\le dB^2+1$. It is strict at $u^*$, retains
the same optimum, and makes the new feasible set compact. We now have
an objective $q_0$ and $h\le k+1$ convex quadratic inequalities
$q_i\le0$, with no remaining affine constraints and coefficient bitsize
polynomial in $L$. Some retained polynomials may become affine or
constant after substitution, which does not affect the argument.

\emph{Regularization and elimination.}
For $0<\varepsilon<1$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:regularized}
 \theta(\varepsilon)=\min\{q_0(u)+\varepsilon\norm u_2^2:
                         q_i(u)\le\varepsilon\ (1\le i\le h)\}.
\end{equation}
The point $u^*$ strictly satisfies the relaxed inequalities. The relaxed
ball gives a common bound for all feasible points. The objective is
strictly convex, so an optimum and nonnegative KKT multipliers exist.
Write $q_i(u)=\tfrac12u^TQ_i u+a_i^Tu+c_i$ with $Q_i\succeq0$,
including $i=0$. Stationarity becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrixstationarity}
 Mu=-a_0-\sum_{i=1}^h\lambda_i a_i,\qquad
 M=Q_0+2\varepsilon I+\sum_{i=1}^h\lambda_iQ_i\succ0.
\end{equation}
Let $\Delta=\det M$ and
$p=-\operatorname{adj}(M)(a_0+\sum_i\lambda_i a_i)$.
These are polynomials of degree at most $d$ in $(\varepsilon,\lambda)$,
and $u=p/\Delta$. Define
\begin{align}
 G_i&=\tfrac12p^TQ_i p+\Delta a_i^Tp
                  +(c_i-\varepsilon)\Delta^2\quad(1\le i\le h),
                  \label{eq:Gconstraint}\\
 G_0&=\tfrac12p^TQ_0p+\Delta a_0^Tp
                  +(c_0-w)\Delta^2+\varepsilon p^Tp.
                  \label{eq:Gobjective}
\end{align}
For $0<\varepsilon<1$, the polynomial system
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ksystem}
 \mathcal K(\varepsilon,w,\lambda):\quad
 \Delta>0,\quad
 \lambda_i\ge0,\ G_i\le0,\ \lambda_iG_i=0\ (1\le i\le h),
 \quad G_0=0
\end{equation}
has a solution in $\lambda$ exactly when $w=\theta(\varepsilon)$.
Necessity follows from KKT. Conversely, $p/\Delta$ satisfies
stationarity, feasibility and complementarity, since
$G_i=\Delta^2(q_i(p/\Delta)-\varepsilon)$ and
$G_0=\Delta^2(q_0(p/\Delta)+\varepsilon\norm{p/\Delta}_2^2-w)$.
Convexity then certifies global optimality.

Every polynomial in \eqref{eq:Ksystem} has degree at most $2d+2$.
After a common denominator clears the quadratic coefficients, a
determinant coefficient sums at most $d!(h+2)^d$ products of at most
$d$ input coefficients. Its bitsize is therefore polynomial in $L$;
the adjugate and the $G_i$ have the same property. This bounds coefficient
heights even when the number of monomials is large. Clearing the final
positive denominators does not change the conditions.

\emph{The limit and its algebraic height.}
We have $\theta(\varepsilon)\to\theta$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$.
The original optimum supplies the upper limit. For the lower limit,
take a convergent subsequence of regularized minimizers, using the
common ball bound. Its limit satisfies all original inequalities, and
continuity gives objective at least $\theta$. No boundedness of the
regularized multipliers is needed.

Consequently the following formula, with free scalar $a$, defines
exactly $\{\theta\}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:limitformula}
 \forall\eta\ \left[\eta\le0\ \lor\
  \exists\varepsilon,w,\lambda\ \left(
   \begin{array}{l}
    0<\varepsilon<1,\quad\varepsilon<\eta,\\
    -\eta<w-a<\eta,\quad\mathcal K(\varepsilon,w,\lambda)
   \end{array}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
For $a=\theta$, convergence supplies a witness for every positive
$\eta$. Conversely, witnesses with $\eta\downarrow0$ give
$\varepsilon\downarrow0$ and $w\to a$, so $a=\theta$.
There are two quantifier blocks of sizes $1$ and $h+2$, one free variable,
degree $O(d+1)$ and polynomial input coefficient bitsize. The existential
block can pass the disjunct $\eta\le0$, whose variables it does not contain.

Effective block quantifier elimination
\citep[Theorem~14.16]{BasuPollackRoy2006}, also stated in
\citet[Theorem~2.27]{Basu2014}, yields an equivalent quantifier-free formula
with degrees and integer coefficient bitsizes $L^{O(k+1)}$.
Specifically, its degree bound is $D^{O(b_1)O(b_2)}$ and its coefficient
bitsize bound is $\tau D^{O(b_1)O(b_2)O(\ell)}$ for two blocks of sizes
$b_1,b_2$ and $\ell$ free variables. Here $b_1=\ell=1$,
$b_2=h+2\le k+3$, $D=O(d+1)$ and $\tau=L^{O(1)}$.
This uses the coefficient-height conclusion, not just the arithmetic
operation bound of quantifier elimination.

Remove identically zero polynomials from that formula. At least one
remaining polynomial vanishes at $\theta$; otherwise all its signs
would be constant in a neighborhood of $\theta$, whereas it defines a
singleton. This proves the algebraic degree and height claim. If
$\theta\ne0$, remove any factor that is a power of the indeterminate
from that polynomial. A Cauchy bound on the reciprocal polynomial gives
$|\theta|\ge1/(1+2^T)$ when $T$ bounds its coefficient bitsize.
Absorbing the additive constant in the exponent proves \eqref{eq:nonzero}.
\end{proof}
The argument does not identify the active affine face algorithmically,
nor does it need a genericity assumption. After the affine-face reduction,
squared slack variables would also convert the inequalities into a
bounded quadratic-map zero set. This explains the relation to the
few-constraint algebraic results cited above; the proof here instead uses
a direct convex KKT elimination and an explicit two-block limit formula.

\subsection{Residual separation and multiplier bounds}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:fixedcount}]
Retain the objective bound $|f|\le F_0$ from Section~\ref{sec:upper}.
For a nonempty equality-infeasible slice, minimize $t$ subject to its
native constraints and
\[
 -t\le r_j(x,z)\le t\quad(1\le j\le m),\qquad 0\le t\le R_0,
\]
where a rational $R_0$ bounds all residual magnitudes on the input box.
This is a nonempty compact convex quadratic problem with only $k$
nonlinear inequalities and positive optimum
$\delta_z=\min_{X_z}\norm r_\infty$.
Its explicit length is polynomial in $N$, uniformly in $z$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:nonzerovalue} therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixedseparation}
 \delta_z\ge2^{-N^{O(k+1)}}.
\end{equation}

For an equality-feasible slice with at least one nonlinear row, let $P_z$
be the polyhedron of native affine constraints and linking equalities.
Define the common nonlinear slack
\begin{equation}\label{eq:slatermargin}
 \sigma_z=\max\{t:x\in P_z,\ g_i(x,z)+t\le0
           \text{ for all nonlinear rows }i,\ 0\le t\le1\}.
\end{equation}
Refined Slater gives $\sigma_z>0$. Minimizing $-t$ in this compact
problem and applying Lemma~\ref{lem:nonzerovalue} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:marginbound}
 \sigma_z\ge2^{-N^{O(k+1)}}.
\end{equation}
Let $\bar x$ attain this margin and take a KKT certificate at a primal
slice optimum $x^*$. Write $\mu$ for its nonlinear multipliers.
Evaluate the globally minimized convex Lagrangian at $\bar x$.
The linking terms vanish and the native affine terms are nonpositive,
so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nonlinearmult}
 v_z\le f(\bar x,z)-\sigma_z\sum_i\mu_i,\qquad
 \sum_i\mu_i\le\frac{2F_0}{\sigma_z}.
\end{equation}

It remains to bound linking multipliers without bounding the affine
multipliers in this particular certificate. The vector
\[
 b_*=-\nabla f(x^*,z)-\sum_i\mu_i\nabla g_i(x^*,z)
\]
has coordinates bounded by
$2^{N^{O(1)}}(1+2F_0/\sigma_z)$, since all gradients are affine and
$x^*,z$ lie in their boxes. It belongs to the cone generated by active
native affine normals and both signs of the linking normals.
Choose a conic representation with linearly independent generators,
at most $n$ in number. Restrict their column matrix to a nonsingular
square row minor. Its entries are rational input coefficients after
integer substitution, so determinant bounds and Cramer's rule bound
its inverse entries by $2^{N^{O(1)}}$.
Applying that inverse to the corresponding coordinates of $b_*$ bounds
all selected conic coefficients by $2^{N^{O(k+1)}}$. This is a bound
for real coefficients; the original primal point and nonlinear
multipliers need not be rational. Combining the coefficients of
oppositely signed linking normals gives a new certificate with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixedmult}
 \norm{\lambda_z}_1\le2^{N^{O(k+1)}}.
\end{equation}
Only active affine rows were used and the nonlinear multipliers were
retained, so stationarity and complementarity still hold. If there are
no nonlinear rows, the same conic argument applies directly with
$b_*=-\nabla f(x^*,z)$; no slack problem is needed.

All auxiliary encodings have length polynomial in $N$, and all constants
are uniform over integer assignments. Choose an integer strictly larger
than the bound in \eqref{eq:fixedmult} and than $2F_0$ times the reciprocal
bound in \eqref{eq:fixedseparation}. Its bit length is $N^{O(k+1)}$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:slices} proves minimizer-set exactness for the infinity
norm, and hence for the one-norm as before.
\end{proof}
The growing number of nonlinear inequalities in the chain construction
is therefore necessary for superpolynomial penalty encoding within this
model. Arbitrarily many affine rows alone do not reproduce that effect.

\subsection{A conservative coefficient can be printed in polynomial time}
The uniformity of the encoding bounds has an algorithmic consequence
that does not require finding an optimum, a Slater point or an active face.
The linear-constraint case already has polynomial encoding
\citep[Theorem~11]{GuAhmedDey2020} and polynomial-time sufficient-penalty
output for MILPs \citep[Theorem~15]{LefebvreSchmidt2025}; the latter source
also notes the convex-MIQP extension. Here the bounds extend conservative
output to native quadratic constraints when either a positive count $k$
or the continuous dimension $n$ is fixed.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:printing}
For each fixed continuous dimension $n$, and separately for each fixed
bound $k$ on the nonlinear native count, there is a polynomial-time
algorithm that, on input promised to satisfy Section~\ref{sec:upper},
outputs an ordinary binary integer $\rho$ that is minimizer-set exact
at multiplier zero for both stated norms.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Choose a universal integer constant $C$ large enough that the bounds in
Theorems~\ref{thm:generalupper} and~\ref{thm:fixedcount} are at most
\[
 B_n=(N+1)2^{C(n+1)},\qquad B_k=N^{C(k+1)},
\]
respectively. In each case some sufficient integer has at most $B_n$
or $B_k$ bits and is therefore smaller than $2^{B_n}$ or $2^{B_k}$.
Increasing an exact zero-multiplier penalty preserves value exactness
and excludes every nonzero-residual minimizer. Thus the algorithm may
simply print a one followed by $B_n$ or $B_k$ zeros. For fixed $n$ or
fixed $k$, respectively, this output and its production time are
polynomial in $N$.
\end{proof}
The absolute constants exist by the cited effective bounds and may be
hardcoded; the corollary does not require them as part of the input.
The cited quantifier-elimination bounds give asymptotic constants, which
we have not evaluated numerically; we have not implemented this
conservative rule. The algorithm does not verify the input promises,
solve the original mixed-integer problem, or approximate the smallest
penalty. A polynomial bit bound still permits an exponentially large
numerical coefficient.
