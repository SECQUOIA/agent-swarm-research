\section{Introduction}
An indicator quadratic problem chooses which continuous coordinates may be
nonzero and then minimizes a convex quadratic objective on the selected
coordinates. Once that choice is fixed, a linear system solves the continuous
problem. The difficulty lies in selecting the indicators. Sparse interaction
graphs suggest dynamic programming, but eliminating a subtree creates a
function of its continuous boundary variables. A small separator controls the
dimension of this function, not the number of supports that can define it.

We give conditions under which all these value functions can be constructed
exactly in expected polynomial time. The matrix is positive definite and its
support graph has fixed treewidth. We perturb only the indicator penalties,
using independent uniform choices from a rational grid of linear size.
For each prescribed bounded separator box, the algorithm returns a
\emph{dictionary}: a list of full quadratic functions whose pointwise minimum
is the exact value function. It retains every support that attains this
minimum anywhere, even at a single tie point. It may also retain supports
that are never optimal. The construction does not partition the box into
optimality regions.

The main contribution is this complete message construction under a shared
penalty perturbation. It separates two questions that are often conflated:
whether a short representation exists and whether one can find it. First, a
short shattering argument bounds the expected number of supports near the
optimum at any fixed parameter. Second, a certified version of classical
partition enumeration finds a controlled superset of those supports using
only additive approximation. Finally, a deterministic parameter net captures
every support that is optimal somewhere. Computing each message directly
avoids a recursive product of child dictionaries. This is useful when
reusable exact conditional value functions are required, as opposed to one
optimal solution at one parameter.

Our complexity bound is polynomial in numerical bounds as well as input
length. In particular, it depends on an upper spectral bound, the inverse
lower spectral bound, a bound on the linear coefficients, the separator box
radius, and the inverse perturbation scale. It is not a bound polynomial in
the logarithms of all these quantities. The exact answer concerns the
perturbed instance. We also obtain a certified additive interval for the
unperturbed optimum.

Three complementary results explain why additional conditions matter.
Section~\ref{sec:hardness} proves exact NP-hardness at bandwidth two with
positive penalties and matrices arbitrarily close to the identity. It
separates large-coefficient constant-gap hardness from bounded-coefficient
exact hardness, and shows why bounded penalty noise cannot erase arbitrary
coefficient magnitudes. Section~\ref{sec:message-limits} constructs a scalar
message with exponentially many necessary formulas from a fixed coefficient
alphabet, and proves a matching power law for approximation by pruning its
original quadratics. Appendix~\ref{sec:representation} gives two limits for
universal representations on stars: a correlation-polytope face in the
inverse-principal polytope and exponential width for exact projected-row
decision diagrams, including row normalization. These results distinguish
the complexity of optimization, conditional value functions, and particular
universal representations.

The supporting appendices separate three output notions: all active support
labels, distinct necessary polynomial formulas, and connected regions of a
unique winning label. Appendix~\ref{sec:moments} proves every fixed moment
of active-label counts, including atomic noise, and combines those moments
with classical algebraic bounds. This gives polynomial expected connected
region counts in every fixed dimension and an exact subdivision labeled
with all minimizing supports on every cell, using a larger polynomial-size
noise grid than the main dictionary theorem. Appendix~\ref{sec:geometry}
gives sharper continuous scalar and planar estimates and a discrepancy
transfer for necessary formulas. Appendix~\ref{sec:recursive} proves direct
block and fixed-width recursions under diagonal dominance. These derived
results retain their distinct assumptions and outputs; the general geometric,
coarea, and CAD tools are established.

\subsection{Relation to prior work}
Bhathena, Fattahi, G\'omez, and K\"u\c{c}\"ukyavuz
\cite{BhathenaTrees} already solve the unperturbed problem on arbitrary trees
in quadratic arithmetic time and memory. Their parametric value functions
and conjugate calculations are direct antecedents. Our result does not
improve that tree-specific guarantee. Their subsequent work on structured
graphs \cite[Definition~5, Lemma~9, and Theorem~1]{BhathenaStructured}
constructs exact parametric dynamic programs with complexity controlled by
treewidth, volume growth, conditioning, and a deterministic bound on the
multiplicity of nearly optimal local supports. We instead control that
multiplicity in expectation under penalty perturbations. Fixed treewidth
suffices without a volume-growth or degree restriction, but we solve a
perturbed problem and give no comparable practical running-time claim.

Approximation on sparse graphs is also established. Bienstock and Mu\~noz
\cite{BienstockMunoz} develop bounded-treewidth formulations for polynomial
optimization. Bienstock and Chen \cite[Theorem~1.4]{BienstockChen} treat
structured indicator quadratic models, with a general guarantee allowing
controlled violation of certain constraints. G\'omez, Han, and Lozano
\cite{GomezBanded} develop decision-diagram approximations for banded
quadratics. Choi et al.\ \cite[Sections~7--8]{ChoiDiagrams} provide
exact structured diagrams and approximation results using decay, volume
growth, and boundary-size bounds for the support graph of a matrix or its
inverse. Our proof uses an elementary feasible grid approximation on a tree
decomposition; we do not claim a new approximation principle or a uniformly
stronger result than these methods.

The probabilistic and enumeration ingredients have close classical
antecedents. The sandwich inequality for shattered sets is established; we
use the form stated by Kozma and Moran \cite[Theorem~5]{KozmaMoran}.
Lawler's partition method \cite{Lawler} lists ranked solutions from an exact
optimization oracle. Our additive-oracle version gives a two-sided
near-optimality inclusion instead of an exact ranking.
R\"oglin and Teng \cite[Sections~6.1--6.2]{RoglinTeng} prove higher winner-gap
bounds and expected polynomial smoothed optimization from
pseudopolynomial algorithms. Dughmi and Roughgarden
\cite[Proposition~2.4]{DughmiRoughgarden} explicitly state a conversion from
an approximation scheme to exact smoothed optimization for binary linear
objectives. Thus neither the general approximation-to-exact idea nor
expected polynomial smoothed optimization is new here.

Beier, R\"oglin, R\"osner, and V\"ocking \cite[Theorem~1]{BeierPareto} bound
smoothed bicriteria Pareto counts with one arbitrary deterministic objective
and one linear objective whose independent coefficients have bounded densities
and finite first moments. Polynomial moments of smoothed Pareto counts are likewise known
\cite{RoglinTeng,BrunschRoglin}, and smoothed parametric enumeration has
precedent in shortest paths \cite[Section~6]{ApplegatePaths}. Those results
use different perturbation models and outputs. Our general theorem allows
arbitrary deterministic Lipschitz branches and adds one shared random
linear penalty to their binary labels. To the best of our knowledge, the
cited results do not provide the complete bounded-parameter dictionaries
with the restricted additive-oracle guarantee proved here. This precise
construction and its indicator-quadratic specialization are the principal
novelty claims. The companion contributions are the simultaneous restrictions
in the hardness and message examples and the two specific star obstructions.
To the best of our knowledge, the inspected sources do not contain those
restricted statements. Their proofs and comparisons appear with the results;
the underlying reduction, pruning, and conic-transfer principles are established.
