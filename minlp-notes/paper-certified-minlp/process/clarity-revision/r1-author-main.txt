    Checkable Lower Bounds for Convex Mixed-Integer Nonlinear
                           Optimization
              through Rational Outer Approximations
                                          Anonymous authors


                                                 Abstract
         An exact mixed-integer linear proof does not by itself certify a bound for a convex mixed-
     integer nonlinear program: the nonlinear cuts must be valid, and the proof must refer to the
     master justified for the stated model. We develop an implemented certificate interface that closes
     these gaps through rigorous rational affine underestimators, a common exact interpretation of the
     loaded expression tree, explicit master matching, and replay of saved rational discrete inferences.
     We prove that evidence satisfying this contract gives a bound on every feasible point of the
     interpreted nonlinear model, independently of numerical subproblem accuracy. Focused Lean
     proofs validate selected mathematical implications; the executable checker remains outside that
     formal coverage. A frozen uniform experiment yields accepted artifacts for 203 of 289 attempted
     models in separate replay. Exact audits also identify three new completed proofs that received
     external acceptance despite invalid supplied linear-combination or integer-disjunction steps. The
     interface makes nonlinear bound claims replayable without repeating their numerical search
     and makes failures attributable to explicit checking obligations, building on established convex
     certificates and rigorous-bounding principles.

Keywords: convex MINLP; outer approximation; lower-bound certificates; rational arithmetic;
verification.


1    Introduction
An exact mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) proof establishes a bound for its input model.
Certifying a convex mixed-integer nonlinear program (MINLP) requires two further links: the
nonlinear cuts must be valid for the stated nonlinear model, and the proved MILP must be the
master justified by those cuts and the original constraints. Without these links, exact discrete
arithmetic can certify the wrong relaxation. The research problem addressed here is to make this
complete connection explicit and replayable.
    For minimization, the output of interest is a rational number β and evidence establishing

                        β ≤ f (x)      for every feasible mixed-integer point x                            (1)

for a precisely interpreted nonlinear model. Outer approximation (OA) supplies the classical
optimization principle: affine underestimators of convex functions define a MILP relaxation whose
lower bounds transfer to the original problem [9, 17]. The certification difficulty lies in preserving
that principle through expression translation, derivative evaluation, coefficient rounding, master
construction, and proof checking. For example, rounding a tangent’s slope without correcting its
intercept can exclude a nonlinear feasible point; a proof for a different master cannot repair that
error.

                                                     1
    We develop and implement a certificate interface that joins these obligations. A common exact
interpretation of the loaded expression tree governs nonlinear checks and master construction.
Rigorous enclosures justify rational affine underestimators, an explicit correspondence check binds
the discrete proof to the reconstructed master, and complete rational replay establishes its bound.
The soundness argument constructs an objective-preserving image of every original feasible point in
that master. Numerical search supplies candidate points, slopes, and proofs; acceptance depends on
their checked properties rather than on numerical subproblem accuracy. The resulting artifact can
be replayed without repeating the search.

1.1   Contribution and scope
The contribution is the concrete integration of established convex-support and discrete-proof
principles into a checked nonlinear-to-linear certificate interface, together with evidence about its
reliability. We distinguish four parts of the work:

  1. Method. The interface uses one exact loaded-model interpretation, a rational cut contract for
     bounded, one-sided, and free coordinates, explicit master identity, and complete discrete-proof
     replay. We prove the composition sound: accepted evidence satisfying the stated mathematical
     contracts implies (1).

  2. Empirical findings. A frozen uniform experiment yields accepted artifacts for 203 of 289
     attempted models in separate replay. Exact audits identify invalid supplied proof inferences
     that received external acceptance and infeasible saved solver points. These local witnesses
     distinguish failures of particular justifications from conservative rejection or mere disagreement
     with a reference objective.

  3. Validation. A suite of 161 targeted tests exercises executable contracts and regression cases.
     Focused Lean proofs check coordinate correction, finite-sum composition, and selected transfer
     and primal-completion implications. The formal result assumes genuine support and enclosure
     inequalities; the executable expression, interval, master-matching, and proof-checking software
     remains part of the trusted base.

  4. Reusable artifacts. Portable checker code, frozen inputs and outcomes, complete proof bundles,
     exact failure extracts, and original-model witness audits support reproduction. A descriptive
     catalogue collects checked bounds for 222 distinct loaded models across the recorded protocols;
     it is a reusable collection, not a uniform-run success rate.

The method’s significance is that a nonlinear bound is tied to its stated model by explicit checking
obligations. The empirical contribution shows where those obligations change what can be justified,
and the validation and artifacts make the reasoning inspectable. We establish neither a new general
support-minimization principle nor a certificate-size or solver-performance advantage over prior
work.
    The implementation covers supported smooth convex expressions and exactly recognized
quadratic and structured functions. General nonconvex MINLP, nonlinear equalities, and un-
supported expression domains lie outside its contract. It certifies the exact interpretation of the
loaded expression model, whose relation to the source modeling file is specified in Section 2. A
lower bound becomes a certified optimality gap only when combined with an independently checked
feasible upper-bound witness for that same model. We obtain matching witnesses for a self-contained
quadratic example and clay0204m; proximity to a library reference value alone supplies no such
witness.

1.2   Relationship to prior work
Mixed-integer convex optimality certificates. Certificates for convex MINLP are established
prior work. Baes et al. [1] derive finite optimality certificates and mixed-integer duality using
subgradients and mixed-integer-free polyhedra. Under their respective assumptions, Theorems 1
and 3 bound the number of certificate points by 2|J| , where |J| is the number of integer variables.
This is a bound on the number of points, not on the bit length of their real-valued data. Basu et al.
[2] relate finite convex optimality certificates to Helly numbers for more general feasible sets.
    The closest computational comparison is Halbig et al. [13]. Their generalized certificates consist
of suspension points (points used to anchor the certificate planes) and hyperplanes. They give
construction and reduction algorithms, structural bounds on certificate size, and experiments on
MINLPLib and MIPLIB. Their verification procedure removes integrality and solves a continuous
convex problem to test each certificate plane (Algorithm 3); a linear mixed-integer optimization
problem tests integer-freeness of the certificate polyhedron.
    Our interface checks nonlinear cut inequalities by recomputing rigorous function and derivative
enclosures at supplied support points, then replays saved rational inferences for the discrete bound.
It gives an outer-approximation certificate with a different checking procedure from the geometric
certificates above. The certified bound may come from an interrupted master solve and need not
equal a known optimum. Its soundness implication assumes valid underestimators and a valid
master-bound proof, without a Slater condition or an attainment premise. This is a conditional
validity result, not a completeness theorem guaranteeing such evidence for every optimum.

Outer approximation and numerical safeguards. Coey et al. [7] derive outer-approximation
cuts from conic dual certificates and analyze scaling in the presence of MILP feasibility tolerances.
Our interface checks affine underestimation for explicitly interpreted nonlinear expressions and
exports an exact rational master proof. The approaches differ in their nonlinear representations and
checking obligations.
    Directed rounding and rational repair provide established safeguards for approximate numerical
information. Neumaier and Shcherbina [23] develop safe bounds and cuts for linear and mixed-integer
linear programming. Eifler and Gleixner [10] combine safe Gomory mixed-integer cuts with rational
proof verification. Borst et al. [4] treat certified propagation and dual proof analysis.
    Jansson [15] develops rigorous lower bounds for convex optimization, and Jansson [16] surveys
verified convex and conic computations and their use in reliable global and combinatorial optimization.
Messine and Trombettoni [20] minimize supporting affine functions over finite boxes to bound convex
relaxations. Our finite-box correction applies this established calculation to the residual obtained
after subtracting the proposed affine row. Elloumi et al. [11] describe QIBEX-R, which combines
interval branch-and-bound for quadratic mixed-integer problems with curvature correction and
rigorous relaxation bounds, drawing on this verified-bounding literature. These rigorous-bounding
methods provide precedents for the nonlinear layer; our contribution joins that layer to explicit
model/master binding and saved discrete-proof replay.

MILP proofs and checker correctness. Cheung et al. [6] introduce the VIPR format, whose
inference steps can be checked independently of the producing MILP solver. Exact MILP solving
and certificate production are available in the SCIP software family [14]. Recent work also constructs
VIPR proofs using a black-box ILP solver and rational reconstruction [25]. These methods supply
discrete evidence; they do not establish the validity of nonlinear cuts supplied as input.
    Proof-format semantics and correctness of a particular executable checker are separate issues.
Wood et al. [27] prove a logical encoding of VIPR validity correct in Why3 and implement an


                                                  3
SMT-based checker. Their theorem verifies the logical transformation; it does not automatically
verify the C++ translation program. They also identify trust-sensitive aspects of the existing checker.
The CakeML project also contains a VIPR formalization, pure checker, and verified program with file
and standard-input semantics theorems [5]. Our Python replay kernel is a separate, unmechanized
implementation that checks complete proofs in a restricted subset of VIPR. Its acceptance contract
includes binding to the input master and rejection of unsupported or malformed cases.

Formal nonlinear optimization and verified reductions. Solovyev and Hales [24] verify
nonlinear inequalities using Taylor interval approximations in HOL Light. Narkawicz and Muñoz
[22] verify a generic global-optimization branching algorithm in PVS. Magron et al. [19] combine
semialgebraic approximations and sums-of-squares witnesses with Coq to establish nonlinear lower
bounds. These methods provide important precedents for proof-producing numerical computation,
with a different representation of the nonlinear and discrete reasoning.
    CvxLean verifies optimization reductions in Lean [3]. Its reduction proofs explain why a
transformed problem represents the original one; the external numerical solve is a distinct obligation.
This distinction also governs our formalization: Section 5 states the proved implications and the
separate executable obligations needed to apply them to an artifact.
    Section 2 fixes the model and certificate contract. Section 3 proves soundness, Sections 4 and 5
distinguish executable checking from formal coverage, and Section 6 reports the experiments and
audits. Section 7 draws the practical conclusions; Appendix A describes the accompanying materials
and reproduction commands.

2.2   Expressions and model identity
A certificate refers to a particular mathematical expression model. A rational coefficient p/q,
with integers p, q and q > 0, denotes its exact real value. In the implementation, every finite
floating-point leaf of the loaded Pyomo tree denotes its exact stored binary value as a rational, and
every integer leaf denotes that integer. All subsequent symbolic additions and multiplications use
exact arithmetic. This rule also applies to bounds, fixed values, row shifts, exponents, and objective
constants. Addition, multiplication, and powers are interpreted compositionally; logarithms and
noninteger powers require their stated domains. Algebraic normalization is admissible only when it
preserves this interpretation on the certified domain. In particular, cancellation of a denominator
must not remove a domain restriction.
    There are three different objects in a software pipeline: the source modeling file, the model built
by its modeling environment, and the exact expression model checked by the certificate system. A
source decimal such as 0.1 can become a binary floating-point value during construction. Treating
that resulting value as an exact rational is a precise convention, but it does not recover the decimal
rational 1/10. Similarly, floating-point aggregation performed before extraction cannot be undone
by subsequently storing the aggregate as a rational. If the modeling environment rewrites x/3 using
a binary approximation to 1/3, that resulting coefficient defines the checked input; the original
rational division is not recovered. The Python model loader executes the input file and is trusted
input construction, not a sandbox for untrusted programs.
    The distinction affects more than tiny changes in the optimum. For example, if the binary values
of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.30000000000000004 are treated as exact coefficients, then
                    (0.1)x2 + (0.2)x2 − (0.30000000000000004)x2 = −2−55 x2 .                           (4)
Floating-point coefficient aggregation can instead produce zero. The first expression is concave and
the second affine. A convexity check of one expression cannot justify underestimator evaluation of
the other.
    The required invariant is therefore that curvature analysis, domain checks, differentiation or
subgradient justification, interval evaluation, and master construction all refer to the same exact
expression model. A reproducible artifact must identify that model and document its relation to
the source file. A hash identifies bytes; it does not prove semantic equivalence. When extraction
itself is not formally verified, the extraction code and its interpretation rules remain part of the
trusted software base. Agreement with a solver using another input format must be described as an
empirical comparison unless equivalence of the interpreted models has been established.

2.3   Boxes, support points, and domains
A checker may derive a tighter rational box B ⊆ B0 from the affine rows and integer restrictions,
provided it establishes F ⊆ B. This propagation need not reach a fixed point. Every accepted
tightening must preserve all feasible points; incomplete propagation affects strength, not validity.

    The implementation first validates original expression domains on B0 , before algebraic cancella-
tions, and does not infer missing domains from nonlinear constraints. For example, x/x cannot be
replaced by 1 on a box containing zero. This can reject an otherwise valid constrained problem.
Subsequent linear bound propagation may strengthen a cut by reducing B without changing the
input-domain convention.
    A cut for a nonlinear row hi is an affine function a⊤ x + b satisfying

                                      a⊤ x + b ≤ hi (x)      (x ∈ B).                                    (5)

Here a ∈ Qn and b ∈ Q. Condition (5) is sufficient for a⊤ x + b ≤ 0 to be valid for the row hi (x) ≤ 0
on B. It is stronger than necessary for validity on that feasible subset. In particular, cut validity is
not equivalent to global underestimation on the box.
   A point z ∈ B used to justify an underestimator need not satisfy the nonlinear rows or integrality
constraints. What is required is a valid support inequality

                                 hi (x) ≥ hi (z) + s⊤ (x − z)     (x ∈ B)                                (6)

for a vector s, and justified enclosures of the quantities used in the rational correction. Differentiability
of a convex function on an open convex neighborhood of B is one sufficient condition, with s = ∇hi (z).
More general support conditions can apply on boundary domains, but evaluating an undefined

derivative at a boundary is not justified by convexity alone. For example, − x is convex and finite
on [0, 1] but has no finite supporting slope at zero. The implementation must reject a proposed cut
when its evaluation or support conditions cannot be established. The mathematical formulation
permits subgradients; that permission is not a claim that the implementation supports arbitrary
nonsmooth functions.

2.4    What a complete check establishes
A rational master M retains justified bounds, affine rows, integrality, and valid nonlinear-row cuts,
together with either the original affine objective or epigraph cuts. Its obligation is to contain the
appropriate image of every feasible point of (2), with the same objective value. A proof of the
master’s bound then transfers to (1). The master objective includes the affine constant exactly
once. Variable correspondence, row coefficients and directions, bounds, integrality, and the objective
sense must match the proof input. A proof for a presolved model requires a separately justified
transformation; matching only its objective value is insufficient. No assumption of accurate nonlinear
subproblem solutions is part of this implication.
    The following distinctions govern the use of the term “verified” throughout the paper.

   1. Nonlinear-lemma checking establishes curvature, domains, bound propagation, and the validity
      of the rational cuts. It does not establish a master bound.

   2. Complete certificate checking additionally checks master identity and a proof of the reported
      master bound under an explicitly stated proof-checker contract.

   3. Proof-assistant verification establishes the specific theorems or executable checks processed by
      the proof assistant. Its coverage must be stated separately from that of the ordinary software
      checker.

Even complete certificate checking has a trusted computing base: the arithmetic and expression
implementation, parser, proof checker, and their execution environment unless individually verified.


                                                     6
Repeating the producer’s numerical search is unnecessary, but sharing code for mathematical rules
means the implementation is not independently developed in every component. The accurate
description is a separate replay from the numerical search, with shared trusted components identified
explicitly.
   A complete lower-bound check returns a finite bound only after all its obligations pass. Partial
nonlinear/master checking has a distinct result and yields no certified bound. Rejection can reflect
unsupported syntax, a failed sufficient condition, or a mathematically invalid proof step; those
outcomes have different diagnostic meanings and none is silently treated as acceptance.
   Finally, a feasible MILP-master solution is generally not a feasible nonlinear solution. A primal
witness must independently satisfy all original domains, bounds, integrality conditions, affine rows,
and nonlinear rows, and provide an outward objective enclosure. For minimization, such a witness
with upper bound U , combined with a checked lower bound β, proves 0 ≤ U − p∗ ≤ U − β when
the witness exists. Equality U = β proves attainment and exact optimality. Comparison with a
rounded reference objective supplies none of these feasibility premises.


3     Soundness of the certificate interface
This section proves the mathematical implications used by the checker. The results apply to accepted
evidence satisfying their hypotheses; they do not assert that every convex MINLP has evidence
accepted by the implementation. We separate the nonlinear support calculation, the discrete proof,
and their composition so that a defect in one interface cannot be hidden by a successful check of
another.

4    Implementation and its checking boundary
The implementation separates a numerical producer from replay. The producer uses nonlinear and
MILP solvers to seek useful evidence. Replay reconstructs the exact model, checks the cuts, matches
the rational master, and checks its discrete derivations. Reusing the producer’s search history is
unnecessary. Several mathematical utilities are shared, so replay is independent of the numerical
search but is not a separately developed implementation of every mathematical rule.

4.1   Artifact and execution contract
A certificate bundle consists of the supplied model, a nonlinear-lemma file, a rational LP master, and
a completed VIPR proof. Each nonlinear lemma names its normalized row and supplies a rational
point z, a sparse rational slope vector a, and an intercept b. The checker reconstructs the box and
expressions from the model; it does not trust a supplied box, derivative value, curvature label, or re-
ported solver bound. New bundles record the semantic identifier pyomo-expression-rationals-v1
and source hash. For compatibility with the saved historical files these fields may be absent; a
present identifier must match, and a present source hash is checked. In either case the explicit model
passed to replay defines the theorem being checked. The frozen campaign manifest additionally
identifies its exact bytes.
    Complete checking performs the following operations in order.
   1. Load and validate the model, normalize the objective and nonlinear rows, check domains and
      curvature, extract exact affine rows, and propagate rational bounds.
   2. Reconstruct each lemma’s row and symbolic derivatives, verify its point and variable names,
      and recompute a rigorous intercept bound. Require the supplied b to be no larger than that
      bound.
   3. Regenerate the LP master and require byte equality with the saved LP file. Compare the

   4. Check every supplied master solution and every discrete proof step. Require a finite minimiza-

certified numerical result. The lower-level VIPR kernel supports infeasibility and either objective
sense; the MINLP interface deliberately supports finite normalized lower-bound reports. Theorems
about infeasibility in Section 3 do not enlarge this API.
    The model loader executes a Python/Pyomo file and applies a documented dedenting repair to

not replaced by a guessed value.

4.3   Interval cut replay and numerical proposals

are built from that same expression. The interval evaluator implements addition, multiplication,
powers, exponential, logarithm, and absolute value. A rational point is converted by interval division

are evaluated with the applicable square-root or exponential/logarithm formula. If a formula or
derivative is unsupported, undefined, or nonfinite at the point, the cut is refused. In particular,
recognizing an absolute value or norm as convex does not provide a general nonsmooth derivative
rule.

4.4   Binding the discrete proof to the master
The LP writer includes rational affine rows, accepted cut rows, explicit finite bounds, integrality
declarations, and the normalized objective. Every free variable has explicit infinite bounds. Fixed
variables retain equal bounds, and a nonzero affine objective constant is carried by a new variable
objconst fixed to one. This prevents a silent loss of the constant during proof export. An epigraph
coordinate is real and free. Integer bounds tightened by propagation are justified by Lemma 3.1.
    The VIPR problem parser is shared with the proof kernel. Comparison requires the same
variable set under a bijection, the same integer-variable set, minimization sense, and identical
rational objective coefficients. A uniform single t_ prefix introduced by SCIP can be removed only
if this yields that bijection; genuine original names with that prefix are preserved when already
matched. Constraints and bounds are compared as multisets after dividing each nonzero row by
the absolute value of its first coefficient in sorted variable order. This permits positive rational row
scaling, retaining the relation direction. The current matcher is deliberately stricter than general
linear equivalence: it does not recognize arbitrary equivalent row transformations or negative scaling
even of equalities. Additional, missing, or altered rows are rejected. A certificate for a different
presolved model cannot pass merely because it has the same reported optimum.
    The saved LP must also equal the regenerated LP byte for byte. This adds an artifact consistency
check; the semantic VIPR comparison supplies the mathematical link to the proof. Original zero-
coefficient affine tautologies are omitted in both constructions, and contradictory constant rows are
rejected earlier. A hash or LP byte comparison alone cannot replace proof-input matching.

4.5   Proof grammar, arithmetic, and streaming
The kernel accepts complete proofs in a restricted subset of VIPR versions 1.0 and 1.1. It requires
the section order
                         VER, VAR, INT, OBJ, CON, RTP, SOL, DER
with the declared numbers of variables, indices, rows, solutions, and derivations. Input uses ASCII
integer or fraction tokens with positive denominators; decimal and exponent notation are outside this
parser’s contract. Variable names, integer indices, vector coefficient indices, and linear-combination
references must satisfy the corresponding uniqueness and range checks, including entries whose
value is zero. Omitted solution coordinates mean zero and are still checked against all original rows
and integrality conditions. The finite primal endpoint of a range report requires a feasible solution
attaining that endpoint or a better one.
    Each noncomment derivation occupies one line and carries a complete asm, sol, lin, rnd, or
uns reason implementing Proposition 3.5. Row labels are metadata; references identify rows by
indices. The OBJ coefficient abbreviation denotes the exact parsed objective. Arithmetic uses
rational numbers through python-flint. A combination with incompatible inequality directions or
a stronger-than-justified right-hand side is rejected even if its discrepancy is numerically small.



                                                  17
Assumptions arise only from actual assumption rows and are tracked through every nonzero inference
term.
     The checker requires every inference reference to precede its row, including references with zero
multipliers. A lifetime annotation is −1 or a nonnegative in-range index; references after a stated

the independently computed assumption set is empty. It cannot remove assumptions or validate a
coefficient. Repeated markers, incomplete reasons, unexpected extra tokens, missing derivations,
and noncomment suffix material beyond the declared count are rejected.

as the proving derivation. It need not be the final row. Every declared later derivation must still
pass, and the parser must consume the entire file. Consequently a valid proof prefix followed by
invalid arithmetic cannot be accepted. A later valid but weaker bound is harmless. The lower-level
kernel can also accept a range with no finite dual endpoint when its remaining obligations hold; the
MINLP interface requires a finite lower endpoint before reporting a result.
     Large proofs are processed in two passes. A first pass validates record shapes, references, and
lifetime annotations and computes actual last uses with two arrays of eight-byte integers, about 16
bytes per row. A second pass retains one such array and live sparse rows, freeing a row only after
its computed final reference. Rows annotated −1 are therefore also freed when safe. The parser
initially holds the original master rows for solution checking; this storage and the longest input line
must be included in memory accounting. The method avoids storing the whole proof text, but has
no constant-memory guarantee: many simultaneously live dense rows, long rational tokens, or a
large original master can still consume substantial memory.

4.6    Failures that motivated the checking boundary
Two small counterexamples show why an external success message is insufficient. The inspected
upstream viprchk checkout1 used an initially zero best objective for a solution inference even with
an empty solution section. A matching artifact for min x subject to x ≥ 1 could thereby receive
acceptance for a claimed lower bound of 106 , although x = 1 is feasible. The new kernel refuses a
solution cutoff without an exactly checked witness. Wood et al. also discuss solution-inference and
reference-management weaknesses in older checker behavior [27]; this work makes no priority claim
for identifying that general risk.
    The second reproduced defect rounded the continuous inequality x ≥ 1/2 to x ≥ 1. This
implication is false, even when every printed coefficient is rational. The corrected rule checks both
integer coefficients and integer variable declarations before applying a floor or ceiling. Likewise,
complementary branch inequalities form an exhaustive disjunction only for an integral linear form.
Merely seeing adjacent integer right-hand sides is insufficient.
    Other targeted regressions cover exact floating-leaf aggregation, lost domains after cancellation,
singular norm derivatives, omitted slopes on half-lines and free coordinates, objective constants and
maximization signs, source/master mismatches, invalid solution witnesses, cross-branch assumption
retention, duplicate entries, invalid lifetimes, forged global markers, and invalid suffixes after an
already sufficient bound. These tests establish distinct observable contracts; passing them is not a
formal proof of the parser or executable.
    The distinction between rejection and refutation is material. An insufficient safe-cut enclosure
can reject a globally valid cut. An unsupported curvature rule can reject a convex model. In contrast,
an exact arithmetic audit can establish that a particular recorded inference is invalid. Even the latter

4.7   Generation, frozen replay, and trust
The current producer uses numerical OA with Gurobi and IPOPT, followed by exact SCIP and
VIPR completion tools. It first tries its default exact-SCIP configuration and can retry with selected
separation, presolve, and propagation features disabled. Each attempt has its own search budget,
and proof completion and checking add time. A solver time limit is consequently not a total pipeline
wall-clock limit. Fraction canonicalization in newly generated completed proofs preserves their
rational values; solution sections and inference meanings are not rewritten to obtain acceptance.
Existing historical artifact directories are preserved.
     Replay requires only the checker dependencies: Pyomo, NumPy, SymPy, mpmath, and python-
flint, together with the included Python modules. Neither Gurobi, IPOPT, nor SCIP is required to
check an already complete bundle. The optional external viprchk must return a zero status and
one complete range message matching the exact internal result; it is additional corroboration. The
generation harness requests this corroboration, while standalone replay need not.
     The campaign wrapper hashes model, lemma, LP, and proof files before and after checking. It
records source and environment identifiers, input records, optional external-checker identity, explicit
missing/rejected/verified/error/timeout outcomes, and the original record index. Resume requires
matching artifact fingerprints and the inputs, source/environment identifiers, external checker, and
timeout pinned in the manifest; worker parallelism may change. A worker runs in its own process
group so its timeout also stops descendant checkers. Completed output records are flushed; only an
interrupted final JSON fragment may be recovered. These measures provide provenance and detect
ordinary changes; they do not establish semantic equivalence or protect against an adversary that
changes and restores a file during verification. Stable artifact contents and trusted model execution
remain explicit execution assumptions. Summaries report whether all expected record indices are
present exactly once and use exact rational objective-sense comparisons before converting values for
display. An incomplete cohort can still be summarized, with its completeness flag false.
     The trusted computing base comprises the loader and exact reconstruction, domain/range and
curvature rules, symbolic differentiation, interval arithmetic and endpoint conversion, rational
propagation, serialization and master comparison, proof parser and rational kernel, their libraries,
and the runtime. The numerical search and proof producer need not be trusted when their outputs
pass this contract. Shared trusted code can create common failures, so separate replay and adversarial
tests reduce dependence on the numerical solver without eliminating implementation trust. Focused
proof-assistant theorems in the following section address only their stated mathematical coverage;
they do not automatically certify this entire software stack or every benchmark artifact.

    The theorem safe_affine_of_shift_bounds first proves the finite-sum algebra over an arbitrary
finite coordinate type, including an empty type. Its premises are the support inequality for g, the
individual shift bounds, and the intercept inequality. It derives the affine underestimator by summing
the shift inequalities and expanding the residual p + q − a. The theorem rational_enclosure_cut
then instantiates those individual bounds using (14). Its certificate inputs a, q, z, c, b, v, d− , d+ are
rational, its support vector and function values are real, and its conclusion is (5) for every point of
the box. Rational sums and inequalities are cast exactly into the real-number argument. Neither
theorem assumes the desired affine underestimator as a hypothesis.
    There are two deliberate interfaces. First, a valid support inequality is a premise; deriving it
from convexity, domains, differentiability, or a subgradient is outside these proofs. Second, the
claimed enclosures must actually contain the real values. Lean verifies what follows from those
inequalities, rather than the mpmath computation that supplies them. This separation permits a
clear correspondence with Corollary 3.3 without claiming a formal verification of the executable
nonlinear checker.

5.2   Transfer, cutoffs, and primal witnesses
The predicate LowerBoundOn means β ≤ f (x) for every x in a specified feasible set. The generic
theorem objective_preserving_transfer takes an embedding into a master and equality of
objective values and proves the transferred bound. These premises are precisely the interface
obligations in Theorem 3.7; their establishment by the parser and master matcher is not mechanized.
    For the epigraph case, the formalization also constructs the embedding. The defined mas-
ter retains a base set containing F and a family of cuts valid whenever x ∈ F and h(x) ≤ t.
The theorem epigraph_graph_feasible proves that (x, h(x)) satisfies those constraints, and
epigraph_bound_transfer applies the master bound to that graph point. A separate lemma de-
rives epigraph-cut validity from underestimation of h(x)−t. The base-set inclusion collects the affine,
bound, and integrality obligations; it is an assumption rather than a verified propagation algorithm.



                                                    20
The signed-objective theorems establish the minimization and maximization interpretations. An
empty master implies an empty original feasible set under the same embedding.
    The theorem incumbent_cutoff_lifting formalizes the additional argument in Theorem 3.6:
a checked feasible incumbent belongs to its own weak objective cutoff, so any lower bound valid
inside that cutoff also holds outside it. This proves the lifting step without an optimum-attainment
premise. It assumes the bound on the restricted set; it does not formalize the VIPR inference
invariant or replay a derivation.
    Finally, primal_infimum_bounds proves the bound chain and objective-gap inequalities of
Corollary 3.8. A feasible witness makes the objective image nonempty, and the certified finite lower
bound makes it bounded below; both hypotheses are supplied before using the real infimum. The
separate theorem matching_primal_attains proves that a feasible witness with objective at most
the certified lower bound has objective exactly that bound and is optimal. Thus the formal result
does not mistake a master solution or a reference objective for a feasible nonlinear witness.

5.3   Build and trust boundary
The companion package pins Lean 4.33.1 and mathlib 4.33.1 [26], with exact transitive dependency
revisions in its manifest. Its verification script checks that every proof module is imported, builds
with warnings treated as failures, and audits every project-owned declaration, including private
and generated auxiliary declarations. The transitive axiom audit permits only Lean’s standard
propext, Classical.choice, and Quot.sound. It rejects unfinished proofs and additional axioms.
A subsequent leanchecker pass replays compiled project declarations through the installed Lean
kernel. This is a second checking pass with the same kernel, not a separately implemented proof
assistant; imported mathlib declarations remain the dependency base.
    The package’s coverage table maps each assertion above to its declaration and lists its premises.
The executable parser, model extraction, curvature recognizer, propagation, symbolic differentiation,
interval evaluation, serialization, master comparison, VIPR grammar and arithmetic implementation,
and primal-witness evaluator are outside the formalized coverage. The numerical experiments likewise
remain ordinary software checks. The contribution of this component is a machine-checked account
of selected mathematical interfaces, with that boundary explicit; it is not a claim to originate verified
nonlinear optimization or verified MILP checking.


6     Computational evidence and independent solution audits
The experiments answer three questions: which archived certificates survive the current checks, how
often the repaired pipeline produces a checkable bound under one uniform protocol, and what a
disagreement with a reported objective can actually establish. These are distinct questions. In
particular, a rejected certificate does not establish that its proposed bound is false, and a numerical
reference value is not an independently verified feasible objective.

The bound and the decimal reference string are converted to exact rationals before subtraction. We

6.4      What rejected proof steps reveal
The twelve historical failures that reach the discrete proof checker admit small exact arithmetic

7     Discussion and conclusions
The certificate interface makes the implication from a rational master bound to a convex MINLP
bound explicit and checkable. Its essential connection is objective-preserving feasible-set inclusion:
rigorously justified cuts retain every nonlinear feasible point, master identity binds those cuts to
the discrete proof, and complete rational replay establishes the transferred bound. This allows
numerical search to propose evidence without making its tolerances part of the final mathematical
assertion.
    A first lesson is that exactness must refer to one model throughout the calculation. Exact
arithmetic in the discrete proof is insufficient if curvature analysis, derivative evaluation, or master

                                                  26
construction interpret an expression differently. The loaded-tree contract fixes the coefficients and
domains to which the certificate refers. The bound therefore concerns that exact model; relating it
to an earlier modeling file is a further semantic obligation.
     A second lesson is that verification failures need mathematical classification. The audits identify
invalid supplied linear combinations and integer disjunctions, including steps that received external
acceptance. Tiny residuals and successful process status do not justify those steps. Other rejected
artifacts fail sufficient domain, curvature, or cut-enclosure tests and may still represent convex
models or valid cuts. A local invalid inference refutes its supplied justification, not necessarily its
final proposed bound. Retaining the evidence makes these distinctions reproducible.
     A third lesson is that primal feasibility supplies information that objective comparisons cannot.
Original-variable audits expose violations in two saved solver points, while independently checked
original-model witnesses complete exact optimality proofs in two cases. These conclusions require
bounds, integrality, rows, and objective evaluation for the same model. A feasible master point or a
close library reference value cannot substitute for that work.
     The uniform experiment demonstrates practical certificate production and replay for 203 of
289 attempted models under the stated protocol. Complete proofs surviving unsuccessful producer
returns further show the value of separating an artifact’s validity from a process outcome. The
long-rational failures show that exact values must survive text conversion and reporting as well
as inference arithmetic. These results establish capability and identify failure mechanisms; the
incomplete admission rules, large proof files, and shared-machine timings limit broader coverage and
efficiency conclusions. Two-pass replay reduces unnecessary storage without bounding the number
of simultaneously live rows, and the present measurements do not establish a speed advantage over
other certificate methods or solvers.
     The validation has complementary roles. Executable tests and exact local audits exercise concrete
checking behavior; Lean verifies selected mathematical implications from explicit premises. The
formal proofs do not verify the model loader, symbolic and interval software, master matcher, or
executable VIPR checker. The completed result is consequently a replayable certificate method for
the stated model class with an explicit trust boundary, supported by reproducible capability and
reliability evidence.
