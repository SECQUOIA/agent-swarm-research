Definition 1.1 (Hidden hyperplane convexity). A homogeneous quadratic map ϕ : Rd → Rm has
hidden hyperplane convexity (HHC) if ϕ(H) is convex for every linear hyperplane H ⊂ Rd .
    This is the definition of Blekherman, Dey, and Sun [7, Definition 2.1]; their work appeared as
[8]. Ordinary hidden convexity means only that ϕ(Rd ) is convex. The two properties differ. All
homogeneous quadratic images contain zero and are invariant under nonnegative scaling, so a convex
such image is a convex cone; it need not be closed. HHC implies ordinary hidden convexity when
d ≥ 3: any two points of Rd lie in some linear hyperplane, whose image contains the segment joining
their images.

    The formulation in Blekherman et al. [7, Conjecture 3.3] says that the aggregated function is
not a negative constant. Under nonemptiness this is equivalent to the displayed condition. Their
standing setting has n ≥ 3 and m ≥ 2; the formulation here also includes the smaller positive
dimensions and numbers of inequalities. Throughout, numbered result locators for that work refer
to the explicitly cited May 29, 2023 arXiv version; the earlier author manuscript has different
numbering for several background results.
    The reverse implication in (1.5) is elementary: a nonconstant convex quadratic aggregation
bounds S by a proper convex sublevel set. The unresolved case in the cited conjecture concerns the
opposite implication when K contains nonzero vectors but all of them belong to T . Under convexity
of (g1 , . . . , gm )(Rn ), Blekherman, Dey, and Sun already prove that K = {0} implies conv S = Rn
[7, Proposition 2.14]. For n ≥ 2, their separable case, with all Qi diagonal, also already gives the
certificate characterization [7, Theorem 2.12].
    It is essential to distinguish this certificate question from a complete description of the hull.
In the aggregation literature, a good aggregation is a nonnegative nonzero multiplier λ such that
conv S ⊆ {x : fλ (x) < 0} and Qλ has at most one negative eigenvalue. The function fλ need not be
globally convex. Blekherman, Dey, and Sun prove that these good aggregations describe conv S
under HHC, n ≥ 3, and ∅ ̸= S with proper hull [7, Theorem 2.9]. Thus an existence certificate and
a family of hull-defining aggregations address different questions.
    The two-constraint case belongs to established theory. Dines’ theorem gives convexity of the joint
image of two homogeneous quadratic forms [14], and therefore HHC for a pair of homogeneous forms
and their restrictions. Yildiran characterizes the hull of a nonempty set defined by two strict quadratic
inequalities using at most two aggregations [25]. For three quadratic inequalities in dimension
n ≥ 3 with a nonempty proper hull, Dey, Muñoz, and Serrano obtain an aggregation description
when the homogenized matrices Q1 , Q2 , Q3 admit a positive definite real linear combination [12].
Here “positive definite linear combination” allows real coefficients of either sign; it does not mean
a nonnegative aggregation. More recently, Blekherman and Dunbar use the spectral curve and
algebraic topology to obtain further three-quadratic hull descriptions [6]. Their available preprint
treats finiteness and related spectral conditions [5]. These results set the context for the certificate
question, while neither the classical two-form convexity theorem nor an elementary separation
lemma is itself a new contribution of the present work.

Remark 2.8 (What the limit proves). Normalizing the weights in the simplex alone can give a
negative constant aggregation in the limit. Normalizing the nonconstant coefficient pair prevents
this loss of information. Bound (2.5) removes the constant before normalization; no bound on ck /ρk
is assumed. The final weights need not be a limit of the original weights, or of their rescalings.
Their existence follows from closedness of the finitely generated cone P .
Remark 2.9 (Convexity along one normal). The hard implication needs convexity of f h (Hα,s ) at
arbitrarily large levels for only one α satisfying (2.2). AHC is a convenient condition that can be
stated without knowing a supporting normal. For fixed α, the hyperplanes Hα,s approach E as
s → +∞; convexity of f h (E) alone is not the hypothesis used in the proof. There is no restriction
beyond n, m ≥ 1. For one constraint, a negative quadratic direction gives a whole-space hull by
Lemma 2.4; the remaining cases follow from the easy implication or a negative constant. For two
constraints, Dines’ theorem makes HHC automatic, recovering the known certificate case discussed
in Section 1.

2.3   A quantitative form of the obstruction
For completeness, we give a second way to exclude degeneration to constant certificates. It supplies
a uniform bound on the sweep level under the hypothesis that all convex certificates are trivial. The
cone estimate itself is a standard compactness argument.
Lemma 2.10 (Uniform cone separation). Let P, D be closed cones containing zero in a finite-
dimensional normed space, with P ∩ D = {0}. Then some κ > 0 satisfies
                                    dist(p, D) ≥ κ∥p∥         (p ∈ P ).
Neither cone is required to be convex.
Proof. If P = {0}, any positive κ works. Otherwise the continuous function p 7→ dist(p, D) has a

follows from 0 ∈ D.

3     Consequences for closed systems and semidefinite relaxations
The certificate theorem determines whether a hull is the whole space. Its consequences below
concern that dichotomy; they do not identify every supporting inequality of a proper hull.

3.1    Closed inequalities and the three feasibility regimes
Write T = {x : fi (x) ≤ 0 for all i}.

Corollary 3.1. Suppose S ̸= ∅ and f h has asymptotic hyperplane convexity. Then

                        conv T ̸= Rn    ⇐⇒     conv S ̸= Rn    ⇐⇒     K ̸⊆ T .

Proof. The inclusion S ⊆ T gives one implication. A nontrivial convex certificate bounds T by the
closed convex set {fλ ≤ 0}. That set is proper because the nonconstant convex quadratic fλ is
unbounded above. The remaining implication is Theorem 2.2.

    Strict feasibility is essential here; Example 4.5 gives a counterexample even with HHC and a
closed-system hull with interior. No equality between conv T and cl conv S is asserted.

6.4   Relation to previous infinite examples and finite bounds
Infinite necessity itself predates the HHC framework. Dey et al. [12, Proposition 2.8 and Section 7.4]
give a closed two-variable example with defining functions

                  g1 = x2 − 1,      g2 = y 2 − 1,        g3 = −(x − 1)2 − (y − 1)2 + 1.

It fails HHC: on the homogeneous hyperplane y = 0, the image is (x2 − t2 , −t2 , −x2 + 2xt − t2 ). The
images of (x, t) = (1, 0) and (0, 1) have midpoint (0, −1/2, −1), which would require x2 = t2 = 1/2
and xt = 0. Moreover, their displayed multipliers (a2 , (1 − a)2 , a2 − a + 1), 0 < a < 1, have
leading matrix diag(a − 1, −a) and therefore at least two negative homogeneous eigenvalues. That
family is outside the good-aggregation class used here. Another infinite necessity result, Dey et al.
[13, Theorem 2], aggregates boxed bilinear equalities with signed multipliers and convexifies each
aggregated set before intersecting. It concerns a different aggregation operation.
    The relevant distinction is therefore precise: the construction in (6.1) has HHC and still requires a
continuum of good rays for its strict hull, with the stronger original-space obstruction in Theorem 6.4.
To the best of our knowledge, this gives an explicit resolution of the HHC finiteness question in
Blekherman et al. [7, Conjecture 3.1]. We do not claim that infinite aggregation in general, or a
continuously varying family of active inequalities, first arises here.
    Semidefinite convexification is also established prior theory. Quadratic matrix programming was
studied by Beck [2]. The convex-hull result of Wang and Kılınç-Karzan [24, Section 4.1] applies
to (6.1) for r ≥ 3: take zero objective, three constraints, replication count r, and the positive definite
sum A1 + A2 = I2r to meet their definiteness assumption. Their paper appeared as [23]. This
comparison concerns the closed feasible-set hull, obtained from the zero-objective epigraph, rather
than an automatic replacement of nonstrict inequalities by strict ones. Brun et al. [11, Section 3.2.1,
Corollary 1] also derive a related inner-product hypograph hull over two balls. Fixing the level after
convexification does not by itself prove the fixed-level hull here. The explicit proof above covers
r = 2 and both choices of strictness; its finite lift is not claimed as a new SDP convexification
principle.

7.4   Comparison with approximation theory
The exponent two is familiar in convex approximation. Rote [20] proves optimal O(N −2 ) error
bounds for piecewise linear upper and lower approximations of univariate convex functions on
compact intervals with finite endpoint slopes, using function values and supporting slopes, and
treats planar convex figures as well. This is particularly close context for sampling a one-dimensional
parameter family. His tangent-and-chord approximations are different from the prescribed quadratic
sublevel sets considered here. The classical polyhedral bounds of Bronshteyn and Ivanov [10], and
the modern area-sensitive bounds and comparison in Arya et al. [1, Section 1 and Theorems 1–2],
concern approximation by vertices, linear halfspaces, or piecewise affine functions. They provide the
appropriate context for quadratic local error from sampling a one-dimensional family; they do not
directly give Theorem 7.1 for this prescribed cone of quadratic cuts in 2r dimensions.
    The specific addition is a two-sided bound for the HHC example, including a lower bound
against arbitrary interior good multipliers, constants uniform in the replication dimension, and an
exact rational construction. It quantifies the size of a finite collection of good aggregations as an
alternative to the exact finite SDP lift in Theorem 6.3. No new general approximation exponent,
optimal constant, numerical conditioning guarantee, solver improvement, or runtime lower bound is
asserted. Proposition 7.4 also prevents interpreting a uniform representation bound as a bound for
one objective.

A     An elementary application to local infeasibility certificates
Convex aggregation can also turn a local affine proof into a globally valid convex inequality. We give
the exact certificate and a small example to delimit this use. These are applications of established
aggregation and convexification principles, not an algorithmic novelty or a performance claim.
In related conflict analysis, Berthold and Witzig [4] use local affine proofs and aggregate convex
nonlinear relaxation rows. Dong [15] explicitly allows aggregation of original quadratic inequalities
or quadratic rows reconstructed from a lift before convexification. The construction below selects
nonnegative weights for the original quadratic rows while retaining an affine box margin.

A.3    Limits of this application
If the quadratic rows are purely bilinear, their quadratic matrices have zero diagonal. Any positive
semidefinite aggregate then vanishes: each 2 × 2 principal minor gives 0 ≤ −A2jk , hence Ajk = 0 for
every j, k. Signed combinations of purely bilinear equalities have the same obstruction. Cancellation
can produce an affine aggregate but cannot produce nonzero positive semidefinite curvature.
    Finally every positive semidefinite aggregate is implied by the common Shor lift, by (3.4); its
tangents are implied as well. The example demonstrates strength beyond the termwise relaxation,
not beyond the full Shor relaxation. The existence theorem does not guarantee a positive-margin
diagonally dominant solution on a prescribed local support. No solver implementation or empirical
performance conclusion is needed for the validity statements above.
    The accompanying script supplement/check_examples.py uses rational arithmetic to check
the displayed polynomial identities, margins, lifted witnesses, and selected matrix calculations.
These checks supplement the proofs; finite test instances do not prove the universal geometric
statements.
