Definition 1.1 (Hidden hyperplane convexity). A homogeneous quadratic map ϕ : Rd → Rm has
hidden hyperplane convexity (HHC) if ϕ(H) is convex for every linear hyperplane H ⊂ Rd .

    This is the definition of Blekherman, Dey, and Sun [3, Definition 2.1]; their work appeared as
[4]. Ordinary hidden convexity means only that ϕ(Rd ) is convex. The two properties differ. All
homogeneous quadratic images contain zero and are invariant under nonnegative scaling, so a convex
such image is a convex cone; it need not be closed. HHC implies ordinary hidden convexity when
d ≥ 3: any two points of Rd lie in some linear hyperplane, whose image contains the segment joining
their images.

Question 1.2 (Blekherman–Dey–Sun, Conjecture 3.3). Suppose S ̸= ∅ and f h has HHC. Is

                    conv S ̸= Rn        ⇐⇒         ∃λ ∈ Rm
                                                         + : Aλ ⪰ 0,            (Aλ , bλ ) ̸= (0, 0)?                   (1.5)

    The formulation in Blekherman et al. [3, Conjecture 3.3] says that the aggregated function is
not a negative constant. Under nonemptiness this is equivalent to the displayed condition. Their
standing setting has n ≥ 3 and m ≥ 2; the formulation here also includes the smaller positive
dimensions and numbers of inequalities. Throughout, numbered result locators for that work refer
to the explicitly cited May 29, 2023 arXiv version; the earlier author manuscript has different
numbering for several background results.
    The reverse implication in (1.5) is elementary: a nonconstant convex quadratic aggregation
bounds S by a proper convex sublevel set. The unresolved case in the cited conjecture concerns the
opposite implication when K contains nonzero vectors but all of them belong to T . Under convexity
of (g1 , . . . , gm )(Rn ), Blekherman, Dey, and Sun already prove that K = {0} implies conv S = Rn
[3, Proposition 2.14]. For n ≥ 2, their separable case, with all Qi diagonal, also already gives the
certificate characterization [3, Theorem 2.12].

                                                                2
    It is essential to distinguish this certificate question from a complete description of the hull.
In the aggregation literature, a good aggregation is a nonnegative nonzero multiplier λ such that
conv S ⊆ {x : fλ (x) < 0} and Qλ has at most one negative eigenvalue. The function fλ need not be
globally convex. Blekherman, Dey, and Sun prove that these good aggregations describe conv S
under HHC, n ≥ 3, and ∅ ̸= S with proper hull [3, Theorem 2.9]. Thus an existence certificate and
a family of hull-defining aggregations address different questions.
    The two-constraint case belongs to established theory. Dines’ theorem gives convexity of the joint
image of two homogeneous quadratic forms [6], and therefore HHC for a pair of homogeneous forms
and their restrictions. Yildiran characterizes the hull of a nonempty set defined by two strict quadratic
inequalities using at most two aggregations [7]. For three quadratic inequalities in dimension n ≥ 3
with a nonempty proper hull, Dey, Muñoz, and Serrano obtain an aggregation description when the
homogenized matrices Q1 , Q2 , Q3 admit a positive definite real linear combination [5]. Here “positive
definite linear combination” allows real coefficients of either sign; it does not mean a nonnegative
aggregation. More recently, Blekherman and Dunbar use the spectral curve and algebraic topology
to obtain further three-quadratic hull descriptions [2]. Their available preprint treats finiteness
and related spectral conditions [1]. These results set the context for the certificate question, while
neither the classical two-form convexity theorem nor an elementary separation lemma is itself a new
contribution of the present work.

Remark 2.8 (What the limit proves). Normalizing the weights in the simplex alone can give a
negative constant aggregation in the limit. Normalizing the nonconstant coefficient pair prevents
this loss of information. Bound (2.5) removes the constant before normalization; no bound on ck /ρk

Remark 2.9 (Convexity along one normal). The hard implication needs convexity of f h (Hα,s ) at
arbitrarily large levels for only one α satisfying (2.2). AHC is a convenient condition that can be
stated without knowing a supporting normal. For fixed α, the hyperplanes Hα,s approach E as
s → +∞; convexity of f h (E) alone is not the hypothesis used in the proof. There is no restriction
beyond n, m ≥ 1. For one constraint, a negative quadratic direction gives a whole-space hull by
Lemma 2.4; the remaining cases follow from the easy implication or a negative constant. For two
constraints, Dines’ theorem makes HHC automatic, recovering the known certificate case discussed
in Section 1.

2.3   A quantitative form of the obstruction
For completeness, we give a second way to exclude degeneration to constant certificates. It supplies
a uniform bound on the sweep level under the hypothesis that all convex certificates are trivial. The
cone estimate itself is a standard compactness argument.

Lemma 2.10 (Uniform cone separation). Let P, D be closed cones containing zero in a finite-
dimensional normed space, with P ∩ D = {0}. Then some κ > 0 satisfies

                                    dist(p, D) ≥ κ∥p∥      (p ∈ P ).

Neither cone is required to be convex.

Proof. If P = {0}, any positive κ works. Otherwise the continuous function p 7→ dist(p, D) has a
positive minimum κ on the compact set P ∩ {∥p∥ = 1}: a zero distance would put a unit point of
P in the closed set D. Scaling both cones proves the bound for p ̸= 0, and the assertion at zero
follows from 0 ∈ D.

    Apply the lemma to P in (2.4) and D = Sn+ × Rn . The condition P ∩ D = {0} is exactly the
assertion that every convex certificate is trivial. If A− is the positive semidefinite negative part of a
symmetric matrix A, then

3    Formal verification of the certificate theorem
A Lean development verifies Theorem 2.2 and its HHC specialization, Corollary 2.3, for finite
families of real symmetric matrices, real vectors, and real constants. Its strict feasible set, ordi-
nary convex hull, and nontrivial certificate agree with those defined here. The main declaration
is proper_hull_iff_certificate in the namespace QuadraticAggregation.System; the HHC
specialization is proper_hull_iff_certificate_of_hhc. The assumptions are nonemptiness and
AHC, or nonemptiness and HHC, respectively. No positive definite combination is assumed. The
certificate-to-proper-hull implication is proved separately without either assumption.
    The formal definitions express HHC through kernels of nonzero linear functionals, which are
precisely the linear hyperplanes in this finite-dimensional setting. AHC is stated using arbitrarily
large good levels, as in Definition 2.1; its equivalence with the increasing sequence formulation is
also proved. Matrix positive semidefiniteness is linked to nonnegativity of the quadratic form, using
the symmetry of the original matrices and their aggregations.
    The proof follows the constant-elimination and coefficient-normalization argument in Section 2.
In particular, it constructs separation weights from the convex hyperplane images and obtains the
nonzero limit in the actual finitely generated coefficient cone. These are proved steps, not additional
hypotheses of the headline theorem. The implementation uses the entrywise maximum matrix norm
and the product maximum norm for coefficient pairs. These are equivalent to the Frobenius and
Euclidean norms in finite dimension; the compactness proof works in either choice.
    The verified supporting results include Lemmas 2.4, 2.5, 2.6, and 2.7, and the uniform cone-
separation lemma, Lemma 2.10. The last lemma is checked independently, although the main formal
proof does not use it. The spectral distance calculation and quantitative alternative in Section 2.3
are not formalized as separate results.
    This core package consists of eleven modules in supplement/lean/Formal/QuadraticAggregation/,
accompanied by a declaration coverage map and reproducible checks. With the pinned Lean 4.33.1
toolchain, targeted compilation without warnings, a transitive axiom audit of all 178 declarations

preparation of this manuscript, with source fingerprints unchanged throughout the run. The only per-

Lean kernel and imported dependencies; it does not constitute verification by an independently

Verified boundary examples. The first example uses

                   f1 = x1 x2 − 1,      f2 = 1 − x1 x2 ,      f3 = x21 − x22   on R3 .

Its strict system is empty, its closed set is nonempty, and its closed feasible hull lies in |x1 | ≤ 1.
Every PSD aggregation has zero quadratic and linear parts. Nevertheless HHC holds. Thus strict
feasibility cannot be removed from the closed-system consequence.
    The second example uses f1 = x21 − 4 and f2 = −x21 + 2x1 + 3 on R3 . Its strict feasible set is the
convex strip −2 < x1 < −1, and HHC holds. Every nonzero nonnegative globally convex aggregation
is strictly negative at zero. Zero also belongs to the Shor projection: X = diag(7/2, 0, 0) gives a
PSD block and both lifted residuals equal −1/2. Hence that projection is proper but strictly larger
than the hull. The examples include proofs of HHC: a formal algebraic proof of Dines’ two-form
theorem establishes HHC for pairs of forms, and a linear-image argument handles the repeated
negative form.

5    Formal verification of the infinite-aggregation example
The third Lean package concerns the explicit family

               Sr = {(u, v) ∈ Rr × Rr : ∥u∥2 < 1, ∥v∥2 < 1, uT v > 1/2},            r ≥ 2.

Squared lengths and inner products in the formalization are Euclidean coordinate sums. The
package treats the actual quadratic map and ordinary convex hull, including the four-variable case
r = 2.

Geometry and good multipliers. The set Sr is nonempty and bounded, and its ordinary convex
hull is proper. Its homogeneous map is

                          (u, v, t) 7−→ (∥u∥2 − t2 , ∥v∥2 − t2 , t2 /2 − uT v).

The formal HHC assertion requires convexity of this image on the kernel of every nonzero real linear
functional. It is proved for all r ≥ 2; HHC is not supplied as an additional premise for this example.
