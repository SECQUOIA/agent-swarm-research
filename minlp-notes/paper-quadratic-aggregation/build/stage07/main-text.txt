        Quadratic Aggregation: Certificates, Finite Descriptions,
                         and Approximation



                                                  Abstract
          Nonnegative aggregation of quadratic inequalities can certify that a feasible set has a proper
      convex hull, describe that hull exactly, or approximate it with finitely many inequalities. We
      separate these three questions. First, a nonempty strict quadratic system with hidden hyperplane
      convexity has a proper convex hull if and only if it admits a nonconstant globally convex
      aggregation. The proof works under a weaker asymptotic hyperplane condition and allows
      aggregations whose quadratic and linear parts cancel. Second, the Gram map (X, t) 7→ (XX T , t2 )
      has hidden hyperplane convexity exactly when the number of columns of X is at least its
      number of rows. This yields a three-inequality example in four variables whose open hull requires
      uncountably many strict quadratic inequalities, even allowing inequalities unrelated to the
      original system. Its closed hull admits no finite conjunction of nonstrict quadratic inequalities in
      the original variables, but has a finite semidefinite lift. We give exact hull formulas, a two-point
      decomposition, and dimension-independent Θ(N −2 ) Hausdorff bounds for approximation by
      N good aggregations. Third, three homogenized quadratics with a positive definite real linear
      combination need at most four good aggregations for any nonempty proper strict hull; four
      is sharp in dimensions at least three. The proof transfers the known four-bound for regular
      nonstrict systems to arbitrary strict systems. Together these results resolve three conjectures of
      Blekherman, Dey, and Sun. A portable Lean supplement verifies the certificate theorem and
      specified consequences, the infinite-aggregation example, its exact hulls, and the approximation
      rate.


1    Introduction
Quadratic inequalities are a basic source of nonconvexity in optimization. Combining their rows
before relaxing them can reveal convex information that is absent from any individual row. The
resulting aggregation is simple to state: multiply each inequality by a nonnegative weight and
add. The difficult questions concern the geometry of the aggregated quadratic and whether the
resulting inequalities recover the convex hull. An existence certificate, an exact hull description,
and a uniformly accurate finite approximation impose different requirements on this operation.
Understanding their differences is useful both for structural convexification results and for interpreting
relaxation certificates.
    The classical starting point is convexity of the joint image of two homogeneous quadratic forms,
proved by Dines [14]. Yildiran’s two-inequality theory gives hull descriptions with at most two
aggregations [26]. For three forms, positive definite real linear combinations support hidden-convexity
results [19] and aggregation descriptions [12]. A positive definite real linear combination permits
signed coefficients; a valid aggregation of inequalities uses nonnegative coefficients. Keeping these
two roles separate is essential.
    Blekherman, Dey, and Sun introduced hidden hyperplane convexity (HHC): the image of the
homogenized quadratic map is convex on every linear hyperplane [7, 8]. Under nonemptiness,


                                                       1
properness, and their dimension assumptions, the hull is the intersection of all good aggregations.
Such an aggregation contains the ordinary hull in its strict sublevel set and has at most one negative
homogeneous eigenvalue. It need not be a globally convex function. Their framework leaves three
specific questions: whether HHC can still require infinitely many good aggregations, whether six can
be necessary for three forms satisfying homogeneous positive definite linear combination (PDLC),
and whether properness always has a nonconstant globally convex aggregation certificate. These are
Conjectures 3.1, 3.2, and 3.3, respectively, in the May 29, 2023 version of [7]. We use that version
for all numbered locators; [8] is the journal publication.
    Our first result answers the certificate question affirmatively (Theorem 3.2). For a nonempty
strict system, HHC can be weakened to convexity on arbitrarily distant sweeping hyperplanes for
each normal. Properness is equivalent to a nonnegative aggregation whose quadratic part is positive
semidefinite and whose quadratic and linear parts do not both vanish. The point requiring care
is a degenerating sequence of separation multipliers: a nonzero multiplier may produce only a
negative constant. We eliminate the constant using a strictly feasible point and normalize in the
finite-dimensional cone of quadratic and linear coefficients. A nonzero positive semidefinite limit
then remains an actual nonnegative aggregation. This treats the cancellation case left beyond
the previously established zero-cone criterion. Consequences include an exact test using 2n + 1
semidefinite objectives and equivalence of the whole-space hull and whole-space Shor projection
under the stated hypotheses. Explicit examples delimit both the hyperplane condition and the need
for closure when relating the Shor projection to convex aggregations.
    Our second result answers the HHC finiteness question affirmatively and quantifies it. The
Gram map (X, t) 7→ (XX T , t2 ), with X ∈ Rk×r , has HHC exactly when r ≥ k (Theorem 6.1). Its
hyperplane images are expressed through the classical squared-fidelity function; we use established
matrix variational formulas rather than claim a new fidelity inequality. For r ≥ 2, consider

                      Sr = {(u, v) ∈ Rr × Rr : ∥u∥ < 1, ∥v∥ < 1, uT v > 1/2}.

The Gram criterion proves HHC. Nevertheless, every exact strict good aggregation description of
conv Sr must contain a prescribed continuum of rays (Theorem 7.2). More strongly, no countable
conjunction of arbitrary strict quadratics describes this open hull; no finite conjunction of arbitrary
nonstrict quadratics describes its closure (Theorem 7.4). These are statements in the original
variables. Both hulls have explicit finite semidefinite lifts, and a direct two-point decomposition
proves the strict hull formula already in four variables (Theorem 7.3).
    For the same example, the best Hausdorff error using at most N good aggregations is Θ(N −2 ),
with explicit constants independent of r (Theorem 8.1). The lower bound allows all good multipliers,
including interior ones. The upper bound has an exact rational version with coefficients of logarithmic
binary size. The exponent two is familiar from approximation of univariate convex functions [21]; the
result here concerns a prescribed cone of quadratic cuts in an arbitrarily large ambient dimension. A
single aggregation chosen for one fixed linear objective can nevertheless attain its exact optimal value
(Proposition 8.4). That consequence of convex duality explains why the uniform approximation
lower bound is not an optimization-iteration lower bound.
    Our third result answers the proposed six-necessary PDLC example negatively. For three
homogeneous matrices admitting a positive definite real linear combination, four good aggregations
suffice for every nonempty proper strict hull in every dimension n ≥ 1 (Theorem 9.1). We use
the four-bound established by Blekherman and Dunbar for regular nonstrict systems [5, 6], obtain
such systems by compact inward perturbations, and control the negative components when taking
limits. The original matrices may be dependent; the original weak system need not be regular or
bounded. The four-necessary example is due to Blekherman, Dey, and Sun, and supplies sharpness


                                                   2
for n ≥ 3. Dunbar’s dissertation already states a stronger regular nonstrict four-bound without a
no-points-at-infinity assumption [16]. Our contribution is the complete transfer to the unrestricted
strict setting, not the first four-bound or the first statement dispensing with that infinity assumption.
    These distinctions also delimit our novelty claims. Infinite aggregation was known before HHC
[12], and semidefinite convexification of quadratic matrix programs has established antecedents
[2, 3, 24]. To the best of our knowledge, the results above provide the stated resolutions of the
three HHC/PDLC conjectures, the sharp Gram-map HHC criterion, and the stated cardinality and
quantitative refinements for this explicit example. The detailed comparisons in the relevant sections
identify the precise hypotheses and operations that differ from prior work. We make no general
claim that aggregation, infinite necessity, semidefinite lifting, or the inverse-square approximation
exponent originates here.
    Sections 2–5 develop the certificate theorem, its consequences, and counterexamples. Sections 6–8
establish the Gram criterion, the infinite example, and its approximation rate. Section 9 proves the
sharp four-bound. Section 10 states the exact formal-verification scope. Appendix A gives a proved
local-certificate selection construction; Appendix B treats many rows in a three-dimensional matrix
span, including a counterexample to an otherwise tempting extension of the two-aggregation case.

Definition 2.1 (Hidden hyperplane convexity). A homogeneous quadratic map ϕ : Rd → Rm has
hidden hyperplane convexity (HHC) if ϕ(H) is convex for every linear hyperplane H ⊂ Rd .
    This is the definition of Blekherman, Dey, and Sun [7, Definition 2.1]; their work appeared as
[8]. Ordinary hidden convexity means only that ϕ(Rd ) is convex. The two properties differ. All
homogeneous quadratic images contain zero and are invariant under nonnegative scaling, so a convex
such image is a convex cone; it need not be closed. HHC implies ordinary hidden convexity when
d ≥ 3: any two points of Rd lie in some linear hyperplane, whose image contains the segment joining
their images.

    The formulation in Blekherman et al. [7, Conjecture 3.3] says that the aggregated function is
not a negative constant. Under nonemptiness this is equivalent to the displayed condition. Their
standing setting has n ≥ 3 and m ≥ 2; the formulation here also includes the smaller positive
dimensions and numbers of inequalities. Throughout, numbered result locators for that work refer
to the explicitly cited May 29, 2023 arXiv version; the earlier author manuscript has different
numbering for several background results.
    The reverse implication in (2.5) is elementary: a nonconstant convex quadratic aggregation
bounds S by a proper convex sublevel set. The unresolved case in the cited conjecture concerns the
opposite implication when K contains nonzero vectors but all of them belong to T . Under convexity
of (g1 , . . . , gm )(Rn ), Blekherman, Dey, and Sun already prove that K = {0} implies conv S = Rn
[7, Proposition 2.14]. For n ≥ 2, their separable case, with all Qi diagonal, also already gives the
certificate characterization [7, Theorem 2.12].
    It is essential to distinguish this certificate question from a complete description of the hull.
In the aggregation literature, a good aggregation is a nonnegative nonzero multiplier λ such that
conv S ⊆ {x : fλ (x) < 0} and Qλ has at most one negative eigenvalue. The function fλ need not be
globally convex. Blekherman, Dey, and Sun prove that these good aggregations describe conv S
under HHC, n ≥ 3, and ∅ ̸= S with proper hull [7, Theorem 2.9]. Thus an existence certificate and
a family of hull-defining aggregations address different questions.
    The two-constraint case belongs to established theory. Dines’ theorem gives convexity of the joint
image of two homogeneous quadratic forms [14], and therefore HHC for a pair of homogeneous forms
and their restrictions. Yildiran characterizes the hull of a nonempty set defined by two strict quadratic
inequalities using at most two aggregations [26]. For three quadratic inequalities in dimension
n ≥ 3 with a nonempty proper hull, Dey, Muñoz, and Serrano obtain an aggregation description
when the homogenized matrices Q1 , Q2 , Q3 admit a positive definite real linear combination [12].
Here “positive definite linear combination” allows real coefficients of either sign; it does not mean
a nonnegative aggregation. More recently, Blekherman and Dunbar use the spectral curve and
algebraic topology to obtain further three-quadratic hull descriptions [6]. Their available preprint
treats finiteness and related spectral conditions [5]. These results set the context for the certificate
question, while neither the classical two-form convexity theorem nor an elementary separation
lemma is itself a new contribution of the present work.

Remark 3.8 (What the limit proves). Normalizing the weights in the simplex alone can give a
negative constant aggregation in the limit. Normalizing the nonconstant coefficient pair prevents
this loss of information. Bound (3.5) removes the constant before normalization; no bound on ck /ρk
is assumed. The final weights need not be a limit of the original weights, or of their rescalings.
Their existence follows from closedness of the finitely generated cone P .
Remark 3.9 (Convexity along one normal). The hard implication needs convexity of f h (Hα,s ) at
arbitrarily large levels for only one α satisfying (3.2). AHC is a convenient condition that can be
stated without knowing a supporting normal. For fixed α, the hyperplanes Hα,s approach E as
s → +∞; convexity of f h (E) alone is not the hypothesis used in the proof. There is no restriction
beyond n, m ≥ 1. For one constraint, a negative quadratic direction gives a whole-space hull by
Lemma 3.4; the remaining cases follow from the easy implication or a negative constant. For two
constraints, Dines’ theorem makes HHC automatic, recovering the known certificate case discussed
in Section 2.

4     Consequences for closed systems and semidefinite relaxations
The certificate theorem determines whether a hull is the whole space. Its consequences below
concern that dichotomy; they do not identify every supporting inequality of a proper hull.

7.4   Relation to previous infinite examples and finite bounds
Infinite necessity itself predates the HHC framework. Dey et al. [12, Proposition 2.8 and Section 7.4]
give a closed two-variable example with defining functions

                  g1 = x2 − 1,      g2 = y 2 − 1,        g3 = −(x − 1)2 − (y − 1)2 + 1.

It fails HHC: on the homogeneous hyperplane y = 0, the image is (x2 − t2 , −t2 , −x2 + 2xt − t2 ). The
images of (x, t) = (1, 0) and (0, 1) have midpoint (0, −1/2, −1), which would require x2 = t2 = 1/2
and xt = 0. Moreover, their displayed multipliers (a2 , (1 − a)2 , a2 − a + 1), 0 < a < 1, have
leading matrix diag(a − 1, −a) and therefore at least two negative homogeneous eigenvalues. That
family is outside the good-aggregation class used here. Another infinite necessity result, Dey et al.
[13, Theorem 2], aggregates boxed bilinear equalities with signed multipliers and convexifies each
aggregated set before intersecting. It concerns a different aggregation operation.
    The relevant distinction is therefore precise: the construction in (7.1) has HHC and still requires a
continuum of good rays for its strict hull, with the stronger original-space obstruction in Theorem 7.4.
To the best of our knowledge, this gives an explicit resolution of the HHC finiteness question in
Blekherman et al. [7, Conjecture 3.1]. We do not claim that infinite aggregation in general, or a
continuously varying family of active inequalities, first arises here.

    Semidefinite convexification is also established prior theory. Quadratic matrix programming was
studied by Beck [2]. The convex-hull result of Wang and Kılınç-Karzan [25, Section 4.1] applies
to (7.1) for r ≥ 3: take zero objective, three constraints, replication count r, and the positive definite
sum A1 + A2 = I2r to meet their definiteness assumption. Their paper appeared as [24]. This
comparison concerns the closed feasible-set hull, obtained from the zero-objective epigraph, rather

Corollary 1] also derive a related inner-product hypograph hull over two balls. Fixing the level after

r = 2 and both choices of strictness; its finite lift is not claimed as a new SDP convexification
principle.
    Finally, the example is outside the homogeneous definiteness assumptions of known finiteness
results. For any signed λ, positive definiteness of the two-by-two block in (7.3) implies λ1 + λ2 > |λ3 |,
whereas positivity of the scalar block requires λ3 /2 > λ1 + λ2 . These are incompatible. Thus

8.4   Comparison with approximation theory
The exponent two is familiar in convex approximation. Rote [21] proves optimal O(N −2 ) error
bounds for piecewise linear upper and lower approximations of univariate convex functions on
compact intervals with finite endpoint slopes, using function values and supporting slopes, and
treats planar convex figures as well. This is particularly close context for sampling a one-dimensional
parameter family. His tangent-and-chord approximations are different from the prescribed quadratic
sublevel sets considered here. The classical polyhedral bounds of Bronshteyn and Ivanov [10], and
the modern area-sensitive bounds and comparison in Arya et al. [1, Section 1 and Theorems 1–2],
concern approximation by vertices, linear halfspaces, or piecewise affine functions. They provide the
appropriate context for quadratic local error from sampling a one-dimensional family; they do not
directly give Theorem 8.1 for this prescribed cone of quadratic cuts in 2r dimensions.
    The specific addition is a two-sided bound for the HHC example, including a lower bound
against arbitrary interior good multipliers, constants uniform in the replication dimension, and an
exact rational construction. It quantifies the size of a finite collection of good aggregations as an
alternative to the exact finite SDP lift in Theorem 7.3. No new general approximation exponent,
optimal constant, numerical conditioning guarantee, solver improvement, or runtime lower bound is
asserted. Proposition 8.4 also prevents interpreting a uniform representation bound as a bound for
one objective.

11     Discussion
The results distinguish three kinds of information obtainable from quadratic aggregation. Under
asymptotic hyperplane convexity, a single nonconstant globally convex aggregation detects whether
a nonempty strict system has a proper hull. Complete hull descriptions require a larger class of


                                                   33
good aggregations. Their size depends on geometry that the certificate alone does not record:
homogeneous PDLC for three rows gives a sharp four-bound, whereas HHC without that definiteness
can require uncountably many strict quadratic inequalities even in four variables. The latter example
still has a small exact semidefinite lift and quantitatively controlled finite approximations.
     Several distinctions persist at the boundaries of these results. Strict feasibility supports the
certificate theorem and the comparison with the Shor projection; closed systems without a strictly
feasible point need separate treatment. Closure also changes representation cardinality: the explicit
open hull needs uncountably many strict quadratics, while a countable dense good family suffices
for its closed hull. Under PDLC the limiting negative components must retain their orientation
to obtain a valid closed conic description. None of these operations can be replaced in general by
changing inequality signs.
     The statements are structural rather than complexity guarantees. No general procedure for
recognizing HHC or for efficiently selecting the four PDLC multipliers is established here. The exact
SDP characterizations use exact optimal values, and the rational approximation families control
coefficient size without analyzing numerical conditioning. For a three-dimensional span with many
extreme rays, the directional facet bound improves the known count under its stated cone condition;
the counterexample in Appendix B rules out a general constant bound from the complementary
condition alone. These limits define the reach of the proved results without being premises on which
any theorem in the paper remains contingent.


A     An elementary application to local infeasibility certificates
Convex aggregation can also turn a local affine proof into a globally valid convex inequality. We give
the exact certificate and a small example to delimit this use. These are applications of established

nonlinear relaxation rows. Dong [15] explicitly allows aggregation of original quadratic inequalities
or quadratic rows reconstructed from a lift before convexification. The construction below selects
nonnegative weights for the original quadratic rows while retaining an affine box margin.

A.3    Limits of this application
If the quadratic rows are purely bilinear, their quadratic matrices have zero diagonal. Any positive
semidefinite aggregate then vanishes: each 2 × 2 principal minor gives 0 ≤ −A2jk , hence Ajk = 0 for
every j, k. Signed combinations of purely bilinear equalities have the same obstruction. Cancellation
can produce an affine aggregate but cannot produce nonzero positive semidefinite curvature.
    Finally every positive semidefinite aggregate is implied by the common Shor lift, by (4.4); its
tangents are implied as well. The example demonstrates strength beyond the termwise relaxation,
not beyond the full Shor relaxation. The existence theorem does not guarantee a positive-margin
diagonally dominant solution on a prescribed local support. No solver implementation or empirical
performance conclusion is needed for the validity statements above.
    The accompanying script supplement/check_examples.py uses rational arithmetic to check the
displayed polynomial identities, margins, lifted witnesses, and selected matrix calculations. These
checks supplement the proofs; finite test instances do not prove the universal geometric statements.


B     Many constraints in a three-dimensional matrix span
The number of generators matters when the homogeneous matrices span a three-dimensional
space but more than three constraints are present. This appendix gives an elementary directional
refinement of the known facet bound and an obstruction to extending the two-aggregation case to
arbitrary polyhedral coefficient cones. These are supporting consequences and examples, rather
than a new topological aggregation theorem.
    Let n ≥ 3, L = span{Q1 , . . . , Qm } have dimension three, and suppose that L contains a positive
definite matrix. Assume S ̸= ∅ and conv S ̸= Rn . Define the matrix cone

                         CL = cone{Q1 , . . . , Qm } ⊂ L,    PL = L ∩ Sn+1
                                                                       + .

We distinguish CL from the multiplier cone K in (2.2). For M ∈ L, write qM (x) = (x, 1)T M (x, 1).
Evaluation at a fixed feasible lift is negative on every nonzero nonnegative combination of the
generators. It follows that CL is pointed. A section on which the negative of that evaluation equals
one is the convex hull of the normalized generators, hence is a compact polygon. If CL has k extreme
rays, it consequently has k facets, each generated by two extreme rays. We may retain one original
matrix on each extreme ray: every omitted row is a nonzero nonnegative combination of the retained
rows, so the strict set and the available aggregation matrices are preserved.
    Blekherman, Dey, and Sun give the resulting 2k bound [7, Proposition 2.22]. Their proof uses
the facets of this three-dimensional cone. The next proposition records how the count depends on
the chosen positive definite direction; its three-generator antecedent is Blekherman and Dunbar [5,
Proposition 8.6].

                              CL = {M ∈ L : ℓj (M ) ≥ 0, j = 1, . . . , k}.

description therefore applies [7, Theorem 2.9].

boundedness assumption on the original weak feasible set.

regular compact weak systems. This disproves that possible shortcut to improving the facet count.

smaller. Our established general upper bound remains the cited 2k, with (B.1) and the directional
count when their hypotheses apply. No optimal count for arbitrary cones is asserted. Nor can one
obtain such a count simply by triangulating the coefficient cone and intersecting subsystem hulls:
convex hull does not commute with intersection, and goodness is relative to the original full feasible
set. The inertia condition also fails to be closed under addition: the two matrices diag(−4, 1, 1, 0)
and diag(1, −4, 1, 0) each have one negative eigenvalue, whereas their sum has two. Consequently
their individual inertia bounds cannot justify a reduction that adds good matrices arbitrarily.
