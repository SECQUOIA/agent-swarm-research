\begin{abstract}
Nonnegative aggregation of quadratic inequalities can certify that a
feasible set has a proper convex hull, describe that hull exactly, or
approximate it with finitely many inequalities. We separate these three
questions. First, a nonempty strict quadratic system with hidden hyperplane
convexity has a proper convex hull if and only if it admits a nonconstant
globally convex aggregation. The proof works under a weaker asymptotic
hyperplane condition and allows aggregations whose quadratic and linear
parts cancel. Second, the Gram map $(X,t)\mapsto(XX\transpose,t^2)$ has
hidden hyperplane convexity exactly when the number of columns of $X$ is
at least its number of rows. This yields a three-inequality example in four
variables whose open hull requires uncountably many strict quadratic
inequalities, even allowing inequalities unrelated to the original system.
Its closed hull admits no finite conjunction of nonstrict quadratic
inequalities in the original variables, but has a finite semidefinite lift. We give exact hull formulas,
a two-point decomposition, and dimension-independent $\Theta(N^{-2})$
Hausdorff bounds for approximation by $N$ good aggregations. Third, three
homogenized quadratics with a positive definite real linear combination
need at most four good aggregations for any nonempty proper strict hull;
four is sharp in dimensions at least three. The proof transfers the known
four-bound for regular nonstrict systems to arbitrary strict systems.
Together these results resolve three conjectures of Blekherman, Dey, and
Sun. A portable Lean supplement verifies the certificate theorem and
specified consequences, the infinite-aggregation example, its exact hulls,
and the approximation rate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Quadratic inequalities are a basic source of nonconvexity in optimization.
Combining their rows before relaxing them can reveal convex information
that is absent from any individual row. The resulting aggregation is
simple to state: multiply each inequality by a nonnegative weight and
add. The difficult questions concern the geometry of the aggregated
quadratic and whether the resulting inequalities recover the convex hull.
An existence certificate, an exact hull description, and a uniformly
accurate finite approximation impose different requirements on this
operation. Understanding their differences is useful both for structural
convexification results and for interpreting relaxation certificates.

The classical starting point is convexity of the joint image of two
homogeneous quadratic forms, proved by Dines \citep{Dines1941}.
Yildiran's two-inequality theory gives hull descriptions with at most two
aggregations \citep{Yildiran2009}. For three forms, positive definite real
linear combinations support hidden-convexity results \citep{Polyak1998}
and aggregation descriptions \citep{DMS2022}. A positive definite real
linear combination permits signed coefficients; a valid aggregation of
inequalities uses nonnegative coefficients. Keeping these two roles
separate is essential.

Blekherman, Dey, and Sun introduced hidden hyperplane convexity (HHC):
the image of the homogenized quadratic map is convex on every linear
hyperplane \citep{BDS2024,BDSpreprint}. Under nonemptiness, properness,
and their dimension assumptions, the hull is the intersection of all
\emph{good aggregations}. Such an aggregation contains the ordinary hull
in its strict sublevel set and has at most one negative homogeneous
eigenvalue. It need not be a globally convex function. Their framework
leaves three specific questions: whether HHC can still require infinitely
many good aggregations, whether six can be necessary for three forms
satisfying homogeneous positive definite linear combination (PDLC), and
whether properness always has a nonconstant globally convex aggregation
certificate. These are Conjectures~3.1, 3.2, and 3.3, respectively, in the
May~29, 2023 version of \citep{BDSpreprint}. We use that version for all
numbered locators; \citep{BDS2024} is the journal publication.

Our first result answers the certificate question affirmatively
(Theorem~\ref{thm:certificate}). For a nonempty strict system, HHC can be
weakened to convexity on arbitrarily distant sweeping hyperplanes for each
normal. Properness is equivalent to a nonnegative aggregation whose
quadratic part is positive semidefinite and whose quadratic and linear
parts do not both vanish. The point requiring care is a degenerating
sequence of separation multipliers: a nonzero multiplier may produce only
a negative constant. We eliminate the constant using a strictly feasible
point and normalize in the finite-dimensional cone of quadratic and linear
coefficients. A nonzero positive semidefinite limit then remains an actual
nonnegative aggregation. This treats the cancellation case left beyond
the previously established zero-cone criterion. Consequences include an
exact test using $2n+1$ semidefinite objectives and equivalence of the
whole-space hull and whole-space Shor projection under the stated
hypotheses. Explicit examples delimit both the hyperplane condition and
the need for closure when relating the Shor projection to convex
aggregations.

Our second result answers the HHC finiteness question affirmatively and
quantifies it. The Gram map $(X,t)\mapsto(XX\transpose,t^2)$, with
$X\in\R^{k\times r}$, has HHC exactly when $r\geq k$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:gram-hhc}). Its hyperplane images are expressed through
the classical squared-fidelity function; we use established matrix
variational formulas rather than claim a new fidelity inequality.
For $r\geq2$, consider
\[
 S_r=\{(u,v)\in\R^r\times\R^r:
         \|u\|<1,\ \|v\|<1,\ u\transpose v>1/2\}.
\]
The Gram criterion proves HHC. Nevertheless, every exact strict good
aggregation description of $\conv S_r$ must contain a prescribed
continuum of rays (Theorem~\ref{thm:indispensable-rays}). More strongly,
no countable conjunction of arbitrary strict quadratics describes this
open hull; no finite conjunction of arbitrary nonstrict quadratics
describes its closure (Theorem~\ref{thm:no-finite-quadratics}). These are
statements in the original variables. Both hulls have explicit finite
semidefinite lifts, and a direct two-point decomposition proves the strict
hull formula already in four variables (Theorem~\ref{thm:ball-hulls}).

For the same example, the best Hausdorff error using at most $N$ good
aggregations is $\Theta(N^{-2})$, with explicit constants independent of
$r$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:approximation-rate}). The lower bound allows all good
multipliers, including interior ones. The upper bound has an exact rational
version with coefficients of logarithmic binary size. The exponent two
is familiar from approximation of univariate convex functions
\citep{Rote1992}; the result here concerns a prescribed cone of quadratic
cuts in an arbitrarily large ambient dimension. A single aggregation
chosen for one fixed linear objective can nevertheless attain its exact
optimal value (Proposition~\ref{prop:one-objective}). That consequence of
convex duality explains why the uniform approximation lower bound is not
an optimization-iteration lower bound.

Our third result answers the proposed six-necessary PDLC example
negatively. For three homogeneous matrices admitting a positive definite
real linear combination, four good aggregations suffice for every
nonempty proper strict hull in every dimension $n\geq1$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:strict-four}). We use the four-bound established by
Blekherman and Dunbar for regular nonstrict systems
\citep{BD2025,BDpreprint}, obtain such systems by compact inward
perturbations, and control the negative components when taking limits.
The original matrices may be dependent; the original weak system need
not be regular or bounded. The four-necessary example is due to
Blekherman, Dey, and Sun, and supplies sharpness for $n\geq3$.
Dunbar's dissertation already states a stronger regular nonstrict
four-bound without a no-points-at-infinity assumption
\citep{Dunbar2025Thesis}. Our contribution is the complete transfer to
the unrestricted strict setting, not the first four-bound or the first
statement dispensing with that infinity assumption.

These distinctions also delimit our novelty claims. Infinite aggregation
was known before HHC \citep{DMS2022}, and semidefinite convexification of
quadratic matrix programs has established antecedents
\citep{Beck2007,Beck2009,WangKilincKarzan2024}. To the best of our
knowledge, the results above provide the stated resolutions of the three
HHC/PDLC conjectures, the sharp Gram-map HHC criterion, and the stated
cardinality and quantitative refinements for this explicit example.
The detailed comparisons in the relevant sections identify the precise
hypotheses and operations that differ from prior work. We make no general
claim that aggregation, infinite necessity, semidefinite lifting, or the
inverse-square approximation exponent originates here.
