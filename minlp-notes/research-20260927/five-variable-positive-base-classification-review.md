# Independent review of the low-degree positive-base classification

Date: 2026-09-28. Scope: Sections 2 and 4–6 of
[the positive-base draft](five-variable-positive-base-bound.md), including
completeness of the reduced-support cases, local complete intersection,
and global generation of the twisted ideal sheaf. The residual-intersection
lemma and the convex-SOS application are outside this review's scope.

**Finding:** the classification is complete under the stated weighted
degree bound and absence of real points on positive-dimensional support.
Every case supplies a reduced real-defined local complete intersection
\(Y\subset W\) for which \(\mathcal I_Y(2)\) is globally generated.
The largest resulting value of \((2H-E)^5\) is 22. I found no missing
configuration or counterexample in this part of the argument.

## 1. The weighted budget is enough to exhaust the possibilities

Assume

\[
\sum_B 2^{\dim B}\deg B\leq9,
\]

where the sum is over maximal positive-dimensional reduced irreducible
components of the base. Complex conjugation permutes these components
and preserves their dimension and degree.

An invariant component has even degree: an invariant odd-degree projective
variety has a real point, by a general real linear section of complementary
dimension. A component that is not invariant occurs with a distinct
conjugate of the same degree. Thus the total degree in each dimension
is even. This observation rules out components of dimension at least
three, whose smallest possible total weight is 16.

If a surface occurs, the total surface degree is two. Its weight is
eight; there is no budget for a further maximal curve component, whose
smallest conjugation-invariant total weight would be four. The surface
support is either an invariant integral degree-two surface or two
conjugate planes.

Otherwise the total curve degree is two or four. Choose one orbit of
irreducible components. Its possibilities are:

- an invariant integral conic;
- an invariant integral quartic;
- two conjugate lines;
- two conjugate integral conics.

This selection suffices. It does not require the entire curve support
to be a local complete intersection. For example, a configuration of
four lines may have complicated intersections, but selecting a line
and its own conjugate always gives a disjoint pair. A fixed cubic is
excluded by odd degree, while a nonfixed cubic would require degree six.
Thus degree partitions such as three plus one provide no extra cases.

Taking reduced support does not require the base scheme to be reduced.
If a reduced \(Y\) is contained in the set of common zeros, every original
quadric vanishes scheme-theoretically on \(Y\), since its ideal is radical.
Nilpotents and embedded components of the original base do not change
this implication.

## 2. Surfaces and invariant integral curves

For an integral variety of dimension \(r\), its span has dimension at
most \(r+\deg Y-1\). In particular, an integral degree-two surface
spans a three-dimensional projective space and is a quadric there.
A real singular quadric has a nonempty invariant linear singular locus,
which has a real point. Thus the real-point-free surface is smooth.
Its embedding in \(\mathbb P^5\) has complete-intersection type
\((1,1,2)\).

Distinct conjugate planes have invariant intersection. If that intersection
were nonempty, it would be a real projective linear space and contain
a real point. Hence they are disjoint. Their union is a smooth disjoint
union of regular embeddings, and in complementary coordinate blocks
its ideal is generated by the mixed quadratic products.

An integral conic is smooth over \(\mathbb C\) and has embedding type
\((1,1,1,2)\). A real integral quartic spans at most \(\mathbb P^4\).
The draft's three span cases are exhaustive:

- In a plane, a nonzero quadratic equation cannot vanish on an integral
  degree-four curve. All original quadrics would contain the entire
  real plane.
- In \(\mathbb P^3\), if there are at most one independent quadric
  through the curve, the original base contains the entire span or
  a real quadric surface. If there are two independent quadrics, they
  have no common hypersurface component. Indeed, a reducible quadric
  containing an integral curve forces it into a plane, contrary to
  its span. Their intersection is a complete intersection of degree
  four. The given reduced integral quartic accounts for all that degree;
  generic multiplicity is one, and the complete intersection has no
  embedded components. Hence the schemes are equal.
- In \(\mathbb P^4\), the curve is a rational normal quartic, with its
  usual quadratic ideal and a linear equation for its span in
  \(\mathbb P^5\).

The minimal-degree statements used here are consistent with Theorems
0.1–0.2 and the degree-versus-span inequality in
[Eisenbud–Green–Hulek–Popescu](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0404517).
Only their integral cases are used; no classification of nonreduced
schemes is needed.

There is also a short direct check for the last curve case. On the
normalization, the pullback of the hyperplane bundle has degree four
and at least five independent sections. A degree-four line bundle
on a smooth curve has at most five sections. Removing any three
points leaves a degree-one line bundle with at least two sections.
It has no base point and gives a degree-one map to \(\mathbb P^1\).
The normalization is therefore \(\mathbb P^1\), and the five sections
give its complete fourth Veronese embedding. This proves both
smoothness and the rational-normal description in this case.

## 3. Conjugate lines and conics

Conjugate distinct lines cannot meet: their intersection would be a
real point. Their disjoint union is smooth, and its ideal in its
\(\mathbb P^3\) span is generated by products between two coordinate
blocks. Adjoining the linear equations of the span gives the claimed
ideal in \(\mathbb P^5\).

For distinct conjugate integral conics, each conic is smooth. Their
planes have exactly the four intersection types listed in the draft.

**The same plane.** It is an invariant plane. A quadratic restriction
vanishing on two distinct integral plane conics is zero, so the whole
plane is in the original base. This is excluded.

**Disjoint planes.** Use coordinate blocks \(u\) and \(v\). The mixed
products \(u_iv_j\), together with the separate conic equations
\(c(u)\) and \(c'(v)\), generate the union's homogeneous ideal.
The conics are disjoint, so local complete intersection is immediate.

**Planes meeting at a point.** Their intersection point is real and
therefore lies on neither conic. In the draft's coordinates, normalize
both conic equations to take value one there. The mixed products
\(x_ix_j\), with \(i\in\{1,2\}\) and \(j\in\{3,4\}\), cut out
the union of the planes. Adding

\[
Q=c(x_0,x_1,x_2)+c'(x_0,x_3,x_4)-x_0^2
\]

cuts out exactly the two conics as a projective scheme. Away from the
intersection point this follows by restriction to one plane; at the
intersection point \(Q\) is a unit. This proves ideal-sheaf generation,
including first-order equations along the conics, rather than just
equality of zero sets. Since the conics are disjoint, their union is
again smooth and a local complete intersection.

In fact the displayed homogeneous ideal also equals the intersection
of the two conic ideals. One can see this in the fiber-product ring
of the two planes over \(\mathbb C[x_0]\). If a pair
\((cf,c'g)\) agrees on their common coordinate axis, then
\(x_0^2f|_{\mathrm{axis}}=x_0^2g|_{\mathrm{axis}}\); cancellation
shows that \((f,g)\) already belongs to the fiber-product ring. The
sheaf argument alone is sufficient for the draft.

**Planes meeting in a line.** Their span is a real \(\mathbb P^3\).
Some original real quadric restricts nontrivially to both planes;
otherwise both planes, including their real common line, are in the
base. Its restrictions are nonzero multiples of the two irreducible
conic equations. Intersect this quadric with the equation of the union
of the planes. It is a proper \((2,2)\) complete intersection. Its
two conics have generic multiplicity one and account for the full
degree, and there are no embedded components. This proves equality
with the reduced union, even at the intersections of the conics.

The shared line is not a component of the quadric restriction, since
each plane conic is integral. Its intersection with that quadric has
length two. The absence of real points forces these to be two distinct
nonreal conjugate points; a double root of a real binary quadratic
would be real. Although the local-complete-intersection proof already
suffices, this also excludes hidden tangency at a real intersection.

In every case the chosen union is fixed by conjugation and therefore
defined over \(\mathbb R\). Its normal-bundle and generation properties
can be checked over \(\mathbb C\), as done above.

## 4. Global generation and the numerical bound

An ideal generated by linear and quadratic homogeneous forms has
globally generated \(\mathcal I_Y(2)\). For each linear generator
\(\ell\), include all products \(x_j\ell\). At any projective point
some coordinate \(x_j\) is invertible after trivializing the twisting
line bundles, and these products recover the local ideal generator.
Thus the linear generators do not create a gap between generation
in degrees at most two and the exact degree-two sheaf assertion.

The following values agree with direct Chern-series expansion. For
a smooth curve use \(e=4d-2g+2\); for the complete intersections use
their displayed normal bundles. Contributions add on disjoint unions.

| Chosen subbase | \(e(Y)\) | \((2H-E)^5=32-e(Y)\) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Two disjoint planes | 32 | 0 |
| Smooth quadric surface | 22 | 10 |
| Smooth conic | 10 | 22 |
| Two disjoint lines | 12 | 20 |
| Quartic of type \((1,1,2,2)\) | 16 | 16 |
| Rational normal quartic | 18 | 14 |
| Two disjoint conics | 20 | 12 |

The possibly intersecting conjugate conics use the complete-intersection
quartic row. These seven rows exhaust the selected subbases, so the
required upper bound 22 follows.

## 5. Targeted verification performed

I ran one inline `python` command using SymPy. It independently expanded
\((1+2h)^5/\prod_j(1+a_jh)\) for the complete-intersection normal
degrees above and checked all seven numerical rows. It also computed
the exact intersection of two explicit conic ideals by an elimination
Gröbner basis and verified that the external-point construction's five
quadrics generated that intersection.

The explicit conics were
\(c=x_0^2+x_1^2+x_1x_2+2x_2^2\) and
\(c'=x_0^2+x_3^2+2x_3x_4+3x_4^2\). The ideal intersection had five
Gröbner-basis generators, each reducing to zero modulo the displayed
quadratic ideal. The exact numerical residuals printed were
\(0,10,22,20,16,12,14\) in the command's case order.

These finite calculations supplement the general arguments above;
they do not replace the classification proof. No project-wide checks
or CI checks were run, and the author draft was not edited.
