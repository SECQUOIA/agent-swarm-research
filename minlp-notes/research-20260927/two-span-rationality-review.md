# Independent review of the two-span rationality boundary

Date: 2026-09-28. Status: the complete proof in
[the two-span frontier note](two-span-rationality-frontier.md) passed an
independent attempt to find a counterexample. This review concerns rational
feasibility, not rationality of arbitrary optimal values. The subsequent
polynomial witness bound also passed the dependency audit in Section 8;
it uses the established quadratic-map sampling theorem and a strict-slack
lift.

## 1. Claim examined

The claim is that every nonempty system of rational native PSD quadratic
inequalities whose Hessians span a space of dimension at most two,
together with rational affine rows, has a rational feasible point.

A finitely generated cone of PSD matrices is pointed. If its linear span
has dimension two, it has two extreme rays, each generated by an input
matrix. Thus every input Hessian has a nonnegative rational expression in
two rational PSD generators. Introducing an epigraph variable for each
generator reduces feasibility to two PSD quadratic inequalities and a
rational polyhedron. This reduction preserves rational witnesses in both
directions.

The hard case is therefore two convex quadratics meeting without a common
strictly feasible point. The following argument settles that case. It also
identifies a potentially misleading use of the characteristic determinant.

## 2. The scalar rational function

Use the convention

\[
 q_1(x)=x^TAx+2a^Tx+c,\qquad
 q_2(x)=x^TBx+2b^Tx+d,
\]

where all coefficients are rational and \(A,B\succeq0\). Suppose a
positive combination \(q_1+t_0q_2\), with \(t_0>0\), has minimum zero
and some minimizer satisfies \(q_1=q_2=0\).

Let \(W=\ker A\cap\ker B\). This is a rational subspace. If either
linear term is nonzero on \(W\), the necessary condition

\[
                  (a+t_0b)^Tw=0\quad(w\in W)
\]

immediately gives a rational value of \(t_0\): choose a rational basis
vector on which one of the two linear terms is nonzero. The existence of
the displayed equality ensures that the corresponding denominator is
nonzero.

Otherwise both quadratics are constant on translates of \(W\). Pass to
a rational complement. Then \(A+B\succ0\), and

\[
 f(t)=\min_x(q_1(x)+tq_2(x))
     =c+td-(a+tb)^T(A+tB)^{-1}(a+tb)                 \tag{1}
\]

is a rational function in \(\mathbb Q(t)\), regular for \(t>0\).
At the assumed tangency,

\[
                         f(t_0)=f'(t_0)=0.          \tag{2}
\]

The derivative identity follows by differentiating (1), or by observing
that the derivative of the minimized expression is \(q_2\) at its unique
minimizer.

A real simultaneous congruence sends \(A+B\) to the identity and
diagonalizes \(B\). Its eigenvalues lie in \([0,1]\). Polynomial
division in each resulting scalar term shows that

\[
 f(t)=u+vt-\gamma t^2-
                   \sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{r_j}{t-\rho_j},          \tag{3}
\]

where \(\gamma\ge0\), all \(r_j>0\), and the actual poles satisfy
\(\rho_1<\cdots<\rho_p\le0\). Repeated poles have been combined;
zero residues have been removed. The numbers in (3) need not be rational.
Rationality is retained in the original expression (1).

The possible quadratic term is essential. It comes from components in
\(\ker B\), where the square of an affine function of \(t\) is
divided by a positive constant. Omitting that term would leave singular
Hessians untreated.

## 3. Why the positive repeated root is rational

Assume first that \(f\) is not identically zero. On every interval
\((\rho_j,\rho_{j+1})\), the limits of \(f\) are \(-\infty\)
and \(+\infty\), respectively. Its reduced numerator therefore has
at least one root of odd multiplicity in each of these \(p-1\)
intervals.

If \(\gamma=0\), the reduced numerator has degree at most \(p+1\).
The root \(t_0>0\) supplied by (2) has multiplicity at least two.
These roots already account for \(p+1\) degrees. Consequently all
interval roots are simple, \(t_0\) is exactly double, and there are no
other roots. In particular, \(t_0\) is the unique repeated root.

If \(\gamma>0\), the numerator has degree \(p+2\). There is one
additional odd-multiplicity root in \(( -\infty,\rho_1)\), because
the limits there are \(-\infty\) and \(+\infty\). Again the
mandatory roots and the positive double root exhaust the degree. When
\(p=0\), the same conclusion follows directly from the quadratic
polynomial (3).

The remaining case \(p=0,\gamma=0\) is affine. Conditions (2) force
\(f\equiv0\), contrary to the case assumption.

Write (1) in lowest terms as \(N(t)/D(t)\), with \(N,D\in
\mathbb Q[t]\). The argument proves that
\(\gcd(N,N')\) is a nonzero constant times \(t-t_0\). Thus
\(t_0\in\mathbb Q\). Equivalently, an irrational repeated root
would force its distinct algebraic conjugates also to be repeated roots.

If \(f\equiv0\), choose \(t=1\). The rational minimizer
\(-(A+B)^{-1}(a+b)\) has \(q_2=f'(1)=0\) and \(q_1=0\).
Moreover, every feasible point belongs to the rational affine minimizer
set of \(q_1+q_2\). This handles the identically zero case even when
additional affine inequalities are present.

For a rational \(t_0\), the minimizer set of \(q_1+t_0q_2\) is a
rational affine space. Each original quadratic restricts to an affine
function there, since its Hessian vanishes on \(W\). Adding
\(q_1=q_2=0\) and the original affine rows therefore gives a nonempty
rational polyhedron, which has a rational point.

## 4. Affine rows and zero multipliers

The active affine rows cannot simply be discarded without explanation.
Choose a feasible point in the relative interior of the smallest face of
the rational polyhedron containing the feasible set. Pass to the rational
affine hull of that face. All other affine rows are locally strict.

If the two quadratics have a common strictly feasible point in this
affine space, a sufficiently short segment from the chosen feasible point
toward it preserves the remaining affine rows. Rational density then
gives a rational feasible point.

Otherwise the convex alternative supplies a nonzero nonnegative
combination of the two quadratics which is globally nonnegative on that
affine space. Its minimum is zero because the original system is
feasible. If both multipliers are positive, Sections 2–3 apply. If only
one is positive, that rational convex quadratic has a rational affine
minimizer set. Restricting to it leaves one quadratic inequality and
rational affine rows. The classical rational small-solution theorem for
one quadratic inequality applies.

This analysis covers zero-dimensional faces, singular Hessians, a common
kernel, affine native quadratics, and absence of Slater's condition.

## 5. Do not use the raw determinant's repeated roots

The uniqueness conclusion in Section 3 applies to the **reduced scalar
numerator**. It need not apply to the augmented characteristic
determinant. For example, in three dimensions take

\[
 q_1(x)=\|x\|^2-1,\qquad
 q_2(x)=\|x-2e_1\|^2-1.
\]

The two balls meet at \(e_1\). Here

\[
 f(t)=-\frac{(t-1)^2}{t+1},\qquad
 \det M(t)=-(t-1)^2(t+1)^2,
\]

where \(M(t)\) is the augmented matrix of \(q_1+tq_2\). The raw
determinant has two double roots. Cancellation against
\(\det(A+tB)=(1+t)^3\) removes the irrelevant repeated factor.

This is also a prior-literature caveat. Jia, Choi, Mourrain, and Wang,
*An algebraic approach to continuous collision detection for ellipsoids*,
Computer Aided Geometric Design 28 (2011), 164–176,
[Theorem 3.10 in the author-hosted full text](https://i.cs.hku.hk/~ykchoi/quadrics/CAGD_algebraic_ellipsoids.pdf),
explicitly includes external tangency with positive and negative double
roots of the raw characteristic determinant. Its statement does not
contradict the reduced-numerator argument above. The source was examined
directly, including the proof of Theorem 3.10.

Earlier ellipsoid contact and separation criteria are therefore relevant
prior art for the spectral mechanism. This limited audit does not
establish whether the arbitrary-dimensional rational-witness conclusion,
including polyhedral restrictions and singular Hessians, is new.

## 6. Bit bounds and scope

The real diagonalization in Section 2 is a proof device. It must not be
used as though its entries were rational or had already established small
descriptions. The rational expression (1), computed using adjugates,
has numerator and denominator degrees \(O(n)\) and coefficient bits
polynomial in the rational input size. Once rationality of \(t_0\) is
proved, the rational-root bound applied to a nonzero numerator gives
polynomial numerator and denominator bits. Rational Gaussian elimination
then gives polynomial-size affine restrictions. The common-kernel case
has the same bound by its explicit rational ratio.

For the strict-feasibility branch, a polynomial-size rational witness
additionally needs a quantitative radius and strict-margin bound.
Those are supplied by the existing fixed-span algebraic precision
results; qualitative density alone is not a bit bound. This dependency
should remain explicit in a full small-witness theorem.

No assertion here concerns rational optimal points when the optimal value
is irrational. If a PSD quadratic objective over a native system of
Hessian span at most one has an attained **rational** optimal value,
appending its rational objective threshold gives span at most two and
therefore a rational optimizer. This is a consequence of the feasibility
claim, not a statement about general quadratic optimization.

The argument also implies density of rational points in every nonempty
two-span feasible set: add arbitrarily small rational boxes about any
feasible point. Such boxes leave the Hessian span unchanged. Hence the
affine hull of the feasible set is rational. Neither conclusion survives
the verified three-span irrational singleton in the companion boundary
note.

## 8. Follow-up audit: polynomial-size rational witnesses

The strict-slack construction supplies the missing quantitative step. This
section independently checks its complete dependency chain. The final
Section 5, Theorem 4 of the frontier note was read after it was written
and passes this review. It uses the established sampling theorem, not the
later bounded-value or finite-infimum arguments in the repository.

Start with the rational active affine chart used in Section 4. Its
coefficient encoding has length polynomial in the original input length
\(N\): select independent active rational affine rows and use rational
Gaussian elimination. In the strict branch there is a chart point where
both quadratic inequalities and all remaining nonidentically-zero affine
inequalities are strict. The short-segment argument establishes this
simultaneously. Identically zero affine rows are already enforced by the
chart and must not be included among the strict rows.

Write the chart coordinate as \(u\), and its remaining affine rows as
\(\ell_j(u)\le0\). Introduce real variables \(s,y\) and the closed
quadratic system

\[
 \begin{aligned}
 q_i(u)+s&\le0 &&(i=1,2),\\
 \ell_j(u)+s&\le0 &&\text{for each remaining affine row},\\
 -s&\le0,\\
 1-sy&\le0.                                             \tag{4}
 \end{aligned}
\]

It is nonempty: choose a common positive slack at an existing strict
point and take \(y=1/s\). Conversely, its last two inequalities force
\(s>0\) and \(y>0\). Its Hessian span is at most three. Two Hessian
directions come from the original chart quadratics, and one comes from
the \(sy\) term. Every other added row is affine. All coefficient bits
remain polynomial in \(N\).

The small algebraic point corollary in
[Section 3.1 of the nonconvex note](nonconvex-hessian-span-frontier.md#31-the-shorter-established-route-to-a-feasible-certificate)
applies to (4), including its indefinite Hessian. For this audit I read
the primary statement and representation conventions of
[Grigoriev and Pasechnik, Theorem 1.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0403008v3),
from the local source text on pages 2–3, and reconstructed the following
reduction independently.

Lift a rational Hessian basis into at most three quadratic equations and
retain every inequality in a rational polyhedron. Choose a minimum
dimensional face of that polyhedron meeting the quadratic variety. The
variety misses the relative boundary of the selected face. A connected
component meeting that face is therefore wholly contained in it, by the
open-and-closed argument. A rational chart for the face has polynomial
encoding length. Theorem 1.2, applied with the outer polynomial equal to
the sum of the squared quadratic equations, supplies a sample in every
connected component. One sample is feasible for the whole polyhedron.
This reasoning needs neither compactness nor nonsingularity; lineality
and zero-dimensional faces cause no exception.

With at most three quadratic equations, the sample's univariate degrees
and coefficient bit lengths are polynomial in \(N\). Standard
univariate root and nonzero-denominator bounds therefore give a common
coordinate magnitude bound

\[
                     |u_k|,|s|,|y|\le R=2^{N^{O(1)}}.
\]

There is no need for a separate optimal-value or nonzero-slack theorem:
\(sy\ge1\) immediately gives

\[
                           s\ge1/y\ge1/R.                 \tag{5}
\]

On the chart box \(\|u\|_\infty\le R+1\), every first derivative
of every \(q_i\) and \(\ell_j\) has magnitude at most
\(L=2^{N^{O(1)}}\). Choose \(L\ge1\). For chart dimension
\(d>0\), round each coordinate of the sample to a dyadic rational
with error at most

\[
                  \delta\le\min\{1,1/(2dLR)\}.
\]

The mean-value estimate bounds the change in every original chart row
by \(dL\delta\le1/(2R)\). Equations (4)–(5) then preserve all
original inequalities. Both the rounded coordinate magnitudes and the
common dyadic denominator have polynomial bit length. Mapping back through
the rational chart preserves this bound and enforces the active affine
equalities exactly. Dimension zero directly gives a rational chart point.

The non-strict branches also have polynomial bounds. When both exposing
weights are positive, Lemma 2 gives a polynomial-bit rational multiplier;
its gradient equations give a polynomial-bit rational affine space, and
restriction leaves a rational LP. When only one weight is positive, the
rational gradient equations leave one quadratic inequality and rational
affine rows. The classical small rational witness theorem applies. I read
Theorem 1 and Section 2.2 in the local primary text of
[Del Pia, Dey, and Molinaro](https://arxiv.org/abs/1407.4798), which also
states the underlying continuous result of Vavasis. Each branch uses only
a constant number of rational restrictions, so composing their polynomial
encoding bounds remains polynomial.

Finally, the two-generator epigraph reduction has polynomial encoding
length: its nonnegative coefficients are solutions of two independent
rational matrix-entry equations. Thus the argument proves a polynomial
bound for a rational feasible witness in the full native-PSD
\(h\le2\) theorem. It is stronger than qualitative rational density,
and its proof does not presume that a successful face has been found
algorithmically. Constructing such a point by an efficient deterministic
procedure is a separate claim from this witness-size result.
