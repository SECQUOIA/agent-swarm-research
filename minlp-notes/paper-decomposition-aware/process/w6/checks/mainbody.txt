Decomposition-aware global optimization:
certified coordinate grids, conditional recourse,
and structural limits

Abstract
Small treewidth alone does not make nonconvex mixed-integer optimization tractable: boxconstrained nonconvex quadratic programming is strongly NP-hard at treewidth two. We show
that two further quantities make tree decompositions useful for certified global optimization:
upper bounds Li on the curvature of the objective F along each coordinate, and quadratic growth.
Subtracting Li w2 /8 at each grid node whose longest adjacent grid interval has length w turns
exact dynamic programming over a product grid into a valid lower bound. Min-marginals then
filter the grids, and the stage grids form a certificate, checked by recomputation, √
that needs no
growth assumption. Under growth in the Li -weighted norm, graded grids keep O( κ̄ log(n + 2))
nodes per coordinate at every accuracy, where κ̄ is a scale-invariant ratio of curvature to growth.
For mixed-integer box quadratic programs with bag size p and input length I, this certifies
accuracy 2−q in f (p, κ̄)(I + q + 1)5 bit operations; under growth at a unique minimizer x∗ , with
the Euclidean ratio κ ≥ κ̄, an exact minimizer takes f1 (p, κ)I O(1) . The growth constant is never
needed. Growth with ratio κ forces the Hessian block of the interior continuous coordinates
to have smallest eigenvalue at least 2 maxi Li /κ, and every other local minimizer x′ to satisfy
F (x′ ) − min F ≥ maxi Li ∥x′ − x∗ ∥2 /κ. Unless P = NP, neither parameter can be dropped

to exact partial minimization, totally unimodular coupling constraints and several minimizers,
and test an exact-arithmetic implementation.

Introduction

Optimization models in process systems engineering, energy networks and optimal control are often
built from local pieces. A flowsheet links unit models along streams, a multiperiod model links
consecutive time stages, and a network model links quantities at adjacent nodes. The objective is
then a sum of terms that each depend on a few variables, and the graph recording which variables
share a term often has small treewidth. For variables with finitely many values, small treewidth is
exactly what dynamic programming needs: nonserial dynamic programming, bucket elimination
and junction-tree message passing find a global optimum in time exponential only in the width of a
tree decomposition [20, 50, 114].
Mixed-integer nonlinear models have continuous variables and nonconvex terms, and there
the situation is less clear. Spatial branch-and-bound solvers relax individual nonconvex terms by
convex underestimators and branch on variable domains [16, 17, 139, 171]; they do not use a tree
decomposition to organize the search. Discretizing the continuous variables and running a dynamic
program gives neither a valid bound nor a running time that scales well with the accuracy: a uniform
grid of mesh h on a unit interval has Θ(1/h) points, so tables grow like h−p in the bag size p. Sparse

[24, 121, 184]; the known bounds on their size are polynomial in the inverse accuracy [24, 117].
Small treewidth alone does not make continuous nonconvex problems tractable. Del Pia
and Khajavirad [53, Theorems 1–3] recently showed that box-constrained nonconvex quadratic
programming (box QP) is solvable in strongly polynomial time on forests, but strongly NP-hard at
treewidth two even with small integer coefficients, and that quartic minimization is strongly NP-hard
on paths. This paper asks which additional quantitative information makes tree decompositions
useful for certified global optimization of mixed-integer problems, and what fails when it is absent.

Choose finite grids in the coordinates and subtract, at each node v of coordinate i, the unary

Conditional recourse. A stiff convex term inflates the condition number even when the subproblem it defines is easy. Exact minimization over some variables for fixed values of the others (recourse)
preserves upper coordinate curvatures and growth, so the certificate and the growth analysis apply
to the value function whenever it is a sum of factors that can be evaluated exactly in polynomial
time (Theorem 7.2). Piecewise-affine optimal responses of private convex quadratic blocks certify
a reduction of the coordinate curvatures of the value function that is the largest possible for the
block (Theorem 7.9 and Proposition 7.8), but recourse does not reduce the parameter to the ratio
of negative curvature to growth in general (Proposition 7.11). For a quadratic whose recourse
problem is concave in each coordinate and submodular after sign changes, each recourse value is one
minimum cut with a flow certificate (Theorems 7.15 and 7.18), and for balanced box quadratics the
grid problem itself is a minimum cut, so no tree decomposition is needed (Theorem 7.24). Parametric quadratic programming, cut representations of submodular quadratic functions and threshold
encodings of ordered labels are classical; what is new is the curvature and growth accounting that
keeps them inside the certificate.
Coupling constraints. The certificate rests on rounding each coordinate independently, which
breaks under coupling constraints. Totally unimodular (TU) coupling constraints with mesh-aligned
data admit exactly feasible correlated rounding, which restores the certificate and, for quadratics,
exact output. Under set growth with finitely many optimal values per coordinate, the algorithm
is polynomial for fixed bag size p when the condition number κc (built from a curvature bound
for the continuous variables and the set-growth constant), the box widths measured in mesh units
(s/η) and the number r of optimal values per coordinate are polynomially bounded (Theorem 8.9).
Because its meshes are uniform, it is not fixed-parameter tractable in (p, κc ) (Section 8.7), and its
pseudopolynomial first grid cannot be removed unless P = NP (Proposition 10.10).

Related work

Tractability of sparse nonconvex quadratic programs. For binary quadratic and pseudoBoolean optimization, bounded treewidth gives polynomial algorithms by nonserial dynamic programming [20, 42, 50] and exact lift-and-project or linear formulations of size exponential only in
the width [25, 122, 181]; sparse moment and sum-of-squares hierarchies exploit the same structure
for polynomial problems [121, 184]. For continuous box QP, the tractable classes of unbounded
treewidth of Del Pia and Khajavirad [53] and of Khajavirad [110] take variables with nonpositive
diagonal to be binary (Remark 7.10 compares Khajavirad’s elimination of the continuous components
with our value factors). The exact piecewise-quadratic messages of Del Pia and Khajavirad’s forest
algorithm, also used for total-variation problems on trees [116, 120], are compared with our grids in
Section 10.4. Box QP is also polynomial for fixed Hessian rank [93]. For convex quadratics with
indicator variables, Bhathena et al. give dynamic programs on trees [22] and on graphs of bounded
treewidth, the latter linear in n for fixed width, margin, volume growth and condition numbers [21];
this is the closest treewidth-and-conditioning result we know (see also [23]). For integer QP, bounded
treewidth together with bounded domains gives fixed-parameter tractability [65]; parameterized
by the number of variables, the problem is fixed-parameter tractable with bounded coefficients
[126] and W[1]-hard without them [91]; see [74] for integer linear programming. In fixed dimension,
integer QP in the plane [54] and, according to a recent preprint, mixed-integer QP (MIQP) [6]
are solved exactly in polynomial time. Approximation schemes polynomial in 1/ε exist in fixed
dimension [49, 92], for sparse polynomial problems with constraints [24, Theorem 4], for QP with
a fixed number of negative eigenvalues in exact real arithmetic [178], and for MIQP with fixed
rank and a fixed number of integer variables [51] or, in the bit model, a fixed number of negative
eigenvalues and of integer variables [52]. Several of these works explain why, in their settings, the
dependence on 1/ε cannot be polylogarithmic unless P = NP [24, 52, 178], from the NP-hardness of
Subset Sum or of QP with one negative eigenvalue [151]. Running times that depend on a condition
number of the instance are standard in numerical computation [33].
Grid and vertex bounds, filtering and certificates. Lower bounds from function values
on a grid are classical for Lipschitz functions [147, 157, 167]; with second-order information the
error is quadratic in the mesh [30, 48]. Separable quadratic perturbations that convexify a function

the 2n vertices of a box [12]. Piecewise-linear and sawtooth relaxations of squares apply the same
second-order error termwise inside a mixed-integer program (MIP) [13, 14, 59].
Removing a region whose relaxation value exceeds an incumbent is optimality-based bound
tightening and branch-and-reduce [16, 79, 158, 163], cost-based domain filtering in constraint pro6

gramming [70] and dead-end elimination in protein design [57, 72]; thresholded min-marginals,
computed by two passes of message passing [3, 47, 113, 183], prune states in structured prediction
cascades [186]. Exact rational MIP [41, 66] and independently checkable branch-and-bound certificates [37] guard MIP results against floating-point error, and our path certificate follows the
latter, with a tree that is a path; interval methods and safe bounds control rounding errors instead
[86, 87, 149]. Rational sum-of-squares certificates give exact lower bounds for polynomial objectives
[107, 155], but these bounds are values of relaxations, whereas a path certificate brackets F ∗ within
any prescribed 2−q .
Accuracy-independent work under growth. With second-order convergent bounds, the
number of branch-and-bound boxes near a nondegenerate minimizer is independent of the tolerance
(the cluster problem), but local, asymptotic and possibly exponential in the dimension and in the
conditioning of the Hessian [61, 108, 148, 185]. Lemmas 5.4 and 5.5 are a global, non-asymptotic form
of this phenomenon in which the exponential dependence falls on the bag size, and 8κ̄θ2 ≤ 1 plays
the role of the bound on the second-order prefactor. Optimistic optimization attains exponential
rates when the growth order at the optimum matches the assumed smoothness [141]; for Lipschitz
functions with small near-optimal sets, branch-and-bound needs O(log(1/ε)) function values [154],
and Bachoc, Cesari and Gerchinovitz characterize, instance by instance, the number of evaluations
needed to find and certify an ε-optimal point [11]. Quadratic growth and error bounds give linear
convergence of local methods [60, 109, 130, 145], and restarts remove the need to know the growth
constant [162], as our capped trials do.
Proximity and scaling give algorithms logarithmic in the domain size for linear and separable
convex integer programs [40, 67, 80, 96, 100]. The closest classical analogue of our stages is the scaling
algorithm of Hochbaum and Shanthikumar. For a separable convex objective over {x : Ax ≥ b},
each iteration minimizes a piecewise-linear interpolation of the objective on a grid of O(n∆A ) points
per variable, where ∆A bounds the absolute values of the subdeterminants of A. The grid lies in
a box around the previous solution, and a proximity theorem allows the box to be halved. For
continuous variables, ⌈log2 (s/(2ε))⌉ such grids, with s the initial box width, give a point within
ε of an optimal solution in the maximum norm, guaranteed but not certified [96, Section 1.3 and
Theorems 1.1, 3.8 and 4.4]. For totally unimodular A and integral data, the integer problem is
solved by one linear program over the interpolation at all integer points [137], or by one such linear
program per scale [96, Theorem 4.3]; Lemma 8.3 uses the same integrality. Our objectives are
nonseparable and nonconvex. The retained region comes from min-marginal filtering, which needs
no assumption and yields a certificate; growth only bounds its size. The grid problems are solved
by dynamic programming over a tree decomposition.
Adaptive discretization. Coarse-to-fine dynamic programming refines superstates with optimistic costs along the current optimal path, so that each coarse problem is a lower bound [68, 160].
Interval methods give discrete lower bounds for continuous Markov random fields [153] and, for
optimal power flow on trees, a number of bound evaluations polynomial in 1/ε [62, Theorem 2].
Decision diagrams give outer approximations of integer nonlinear programs [45] and, on partitions
of continuous domains with spatial branch-and-bound, a global method for bounded mixed-integer
nonlinear programs [46]. Continuous distributed constraint optimization [94, 175], variable-resolution
control [142], corridor methods [90], two-level dynamic programming [124], adaptive multivariate
partitioning with bound tightening [144] and re-centered discretizations of fixed size [83] also refine
discretizations adaptively. None of these methods certifies a given accuracy with a number of states
independent of that accuracy.

Exact rational output. A rational QP that attains its minimum has a global minimizer of polynomial encoding length [55, 177]; the stationary-polytope argument of Section 6 follows Vavasis, and
Remark 6.11 discusses NP membership. Rounding to an exact rational is classical [82, Section 5.1],
[119], and in linear programming the optimal face is identified once the gap is below a threshold set
by the encoding length [135, 188]; Lemma 6.8 does this for box QP, with set growth turning a small
gap into proximity to the optimal set. Uniqueness of a QP minimizer implies quadratic growth [39],
and growth towards the optimal set of a QP over a polytope follows from the error bound of Luo
and Sturm [129, Theorem 3.3]. For polynomials of degree three or more, irrational minimizers and
encoding-size phenomena limit exact output [26].
Value functions and multiparametric programming. Eliminating private variables through
their optimal response is standard in Benders decomposition [19, 78] and in condensing for model
predictive control [7, 27, 105]. Multiparametric quadratic programming gives piecewise-affine
minimizers and piecewise-quadratic values on polyhedral critical regions [18, 172], also with nonunique
minimizers [152, 169]; value functions of parameters that enter the objective linearly over a fixed
feasible set are concave [69, 133], and infima preserve semiconcavity [34]. We use these facts for a
global upper coordinate curvature bound of the reduced objective. Low negative inertia with convex
recourse is treated by spatial branching in [128, 178].
Cuts and submodularity. Binary quadratic functions with nonpositive pair coefficients are
minimized by one minimum cut [29, 103, 115, 156], also after sign switching when the signed
interaction graph is balanced [84, 88, 150]. Variables with nonpositive diagonal can be fixed
to endpoints [53, 110, 161], so continuous quadratics that are submodular and concave in each
coordinate reduce to such cuts [32], and pairwise problems submodular with respect to label orders
reduce to cuts on threshold encodings [95, 102, 164]. For general continuous submodular quadratics,
Burer, Natarajan and Willemsen show that a semidefinite relaxation is exact up to dimension three
and give a four-variable gap; whether the problem is polynomial when the dimension is part of
the input is open [32, footnote 2] (for each fixed dimension it is, by enumerating faces [177]). A
sign-restricted case is exact [112], and discretization methods [8–10] are polynomial in 1/ε; for
balanced box quadratics under point growth, Theorem 7.24 is polynomial in I, κ and log(1/ε), which
does not settle the open question because κ can be exponential in I. Partial minimization preserves
submodularity [173, 174], which underlies the mixed continuous–discrete methods of [31, 85], with
certificates from greedy extreme bases [43, 63, 104].
Constraints, optimal sets and limits. Integrality of totally unimodular fibers is the Hoffman–
Kruskal theorem [97, 165], and marginal-preserving rounding under hard constraints is classical
[35, 73]; Remark 8.11 compares Section 8 with the linear programs of Bienstock and Muñoz [24].
Section 9.2 relates our description of the optimal set to Rosenberg’s face criterion and to tree
reparameterizations [161, 183, 187]. Diagonal Lagrangian certificates for box QP are given in
[15, 106, 125], and exactness of relaxations of box QP is characterized in [159]; sparse second-order
cone and semidefinite formulations are exact under graph conditions [58, 111, 168], and chordal
sparsity guarantees positive semidefinite completions [81, 176]. Marginals that agree on separators
glue into a joint measure on tree-structured families [180, 182], but locally consistent moments
need not [166, 182], and complete parametric descriptions can have exponentially many pieces
[77, 131, 143].

Exact output

A certified gap below 2−q is not an exact solution. For rational quadratics, a global minimizer
and the optimal value have bounded rational height, so a sufficiently small certified gap can be
converted into an exact minimizer that is checked against the original data. This section proves a
height bound for minimizers and for the optimal value (Section 6.2), gives an acceptance rule that
needs neither uniqueness nor growth, and shows that an exact minimizer can be recovered from
any feasible point close to the set of minimizers (Section 6.3). Under point growth this yields exact
output in f1 (p, κ) poly(I) bit operations (Theorem 6.14). Section 6.5 gives an acceptance test that
uses the filtering history, and Section 6.6 treats explicit polynomial factors. Several minimizers are
treated in Section 9.

Remark 6.10 (Set growth is automatic). Every rational mixed box QP has set growth with some
constant gS > 0. On an integer slice whose continuous box contains a minimizer, Luo and

point growth, no useful lower bound on gS follows. Theorem 6.9 shows that exactness costs only
log2 (1/εS ) ≤ poly(I) + max{0, log2 (1/gS )} bits of certified accuracy; the cost of reaching that
accuracy is a property of the approximation algorithm.

Remark 6.11 (Relation to rational-witness results). Corollary 6.4 is the box case, with explicit
constants, of a classical fact: a quadratic program with rational data that attains its minimum has
a global minimizer of polynomial encoding length [177]. Vavasis used it to show that the decision
version of quadratic programming is in NP, and Del Pia, Dey and Molinaro extended NP membership
to mixed-integer quadratic programs with unbounded integer variables [55]; on a bounded mixed
box the integer coordinates have polynomial length, so that extension is not needed. These results
certify F ∗ ≤ t. Deciding F ∗ ≤ t for box QP is NP-complete, and NP-hard already at treewidth two
[53], so certificates of F ∗ > t of polynomial size, checkable in polynomial time, for all instances
would place an NP-complete problem in coNP. The certificates of Theorem 6.14 below have size
f1 (p, κ) poly(I): they exist for bounded (p, κ), and they are checked without trusting growth. The
explicit constants R and Ω are what make the stopping test computable.

Conditional recourse

The condition numbers of Section 5 are built from the coordinate curvatures Li of F and a growth
constant. Stiff convex terms, which are easy to optimize, can make the curvatures large. A residual
problem with combinatorial structure causes two other difficulties: nearly tied endpoint assignments
of its coordinates make the point-growth constant small, and dense interactions among them make
the bags large. This section minimizes exactly over part of the variables for fixed values of the
others; we call this recourse, as for the second stage of a two-stage program, although nothing here
is random. The coordinates split into a retained set K and a recourse set R = [n] \ K, with v = xK
and y = xR . For given v, y is chosen optimally, and CT (Algorithm 5.6) runs on the value function
V of (7.1).
Section 7.1 shows that V inherits coordinate curvature and growth from F , so that Sections 4–6
apply to V (Theorem 7.2). Section 7.3 eliminates private convex quadratic blocks and certifies
the curvature of their value factors (Theorem 7.9). Section 7.4 eliminates residual problems that
are concave in each coordinate and submodular after sign changes, whose conditional values are
minimum cuts (Theorem 7.15). These are the main results. Section 7.2 shows that a correction
charged to one bag is valid with exact or certified recourse (Theorem 7.4), but not with grid
min-marginals (Proposition C.2 in Appendix C); Section 7.4 uses its Lemma 7.3 and Theorem 7.4.
Remark 7.21 extends the cut oracle to concave–convex residuals. Section 7.5 does not use recourse:
for balanced quadratics the grid problem of CT is itself a minimum cut, so no tree decomposition is
needed (Theorem 7.24).

Coupling constraints

The corrected-grid bound of Proposition 4.2 follows from rounding each coordinate independently to
an endpoint of its grid interval, and every rounded point is feasible because the domain is a product.
A coupling constraint, even one as sparse as x1 = x2 , destroys this: independent rounding leaves the
feasible set, and the minimum over feasible grid points need not be a lower bound. This section shows
that totally unimodular (TU) coupling of the continuous variables, with data aligned to a uniform
mesh, admits a feasible mean-preserving rounding. Filtering, certificates, accuracy-independent
node counts and exact output then carry over. The price is visible in two places. The rounding is
correlated, so a bound on the full continuous Hessian replaces coordinate curvature; Example 8.5
shows that this is necessary. The analysis uses a uniform aligned mesh, so the number of nodes
√
per coordinate is of order nc κc instead of logarithmic in n; Section 8.7 shows that the algorithm
incurs this cost, and that two natural non-uniform alternatives, misaligned coordinate grids and a
common graded pattern, give invalid bounds with curvature-only corrections. Section 8.5 states
when the algorithm is polynomial and why it is not fixed-parameter tractable. Its levels resemble
the scaling phases of Hochbaum and Shanthikumar [96] for separable convex objectives; Section 2
compares the two.

Structural limits

The algorithms of Sections 4–6 rest on four hypotheses: a product domain, upper bounds on
coordinate curvature, weighted or point growth, and a correction that charges for rounding every
coordinate. Sections 7–9 relax some of them: Section 7 replaces the corrections of eliminated
coordinates by exact or certified value functions, Section 8 replaces the product domain by a feasible
rounding law for totally unimodular constraints, and Sections 8 and 9 use set growth instead of
growth at one minimizer. This section proves Theorem 1.2 and shows what fails when a hypothesis
is dropped without such a replacement. Section 10.1 proves part (a), Section 10.2 part (d), and
Section 10.3 parts (b) and (c). Section 10.4 shows that exact messages can be exponentially large;
Section 10.5, that set growth does not bound the grids produced by filtering, and that under set
growth a deterministic algorithm that queries values and derivatives needs at least of order ε−n/2
queries; Section 10.6, that product domains are essential; and Section 10.7, that bag-local corrections
combined with grid min-marginals are not valid lower bounds, and that matching separator moments
of any finite order gives no local error bound in terms of negative curvature.
Del Pia and Khajavirad prove that continuous box QP is strongly NP-hard at treewidth two,
even with bounded integer coefficients, and that quartic box problems on paths are strongly NP-hard
[53]. Their instances need not have a unique minimizer, so their result does not by itself show that
κ must enter the running time.

the negative part: for a continuous box quadratic with ∇2 F ⪰ −νI one would like an error bound
in which L is replaced by ν. Such a bound holds for relaxations that condition all coordinates on
one global cell (Lemma G.3), but a global cell index can take K n values. Sparse relaxations keep
exact information only on bags and match moments across separators, cell by cell (Definition G.4).
Proposition G.5 shows that this does not suffice for any fixed order k of the matched moments:
for every k there are instances with bag size three, a unique minimizer, ∇2 F ⪰ −2I and a growth
constant that depends only on k, whose order-k relaxations have feasible points more than 1/8 below
F ∗ that occupy only cells of arbitrarily small length. A ν-based local error bound must therefore
use information beyond finite-order separator moments, for example inequalities that couple both
sides of a separator. Appendix G.4 gives the definitions, the construction and the proofs.

Implementation and computational illustration

The experiments in this section test whether the predicted grid sizes appear in practice and whether
the certificates are cheap enough to check. They are not a benchmark of competitive performance:
the implementation is a reference code in exact rational arithmetic, and all instance families with
continuous coordinates are synthetic (planted, random or constructed). The only library instances
we ran are binary (Section 11.8), so we do not test whether modelled instances with continuous
nonconvex terms have small width and a moderate condition number. Appendix H contains the
tables and details that this section summarizes.

Implementation

The reference implementation is written in Python with exact rational arithmetic (Python’s
fractions module); floating-point input is rejected. It handles explicit box QPs 12 xT Hx + bT x + c
with continuous and integer coordinates, supplied or heuristically computed (minimum-fill) tree
decompositions with arbitrary branching, and empty separators. It implements the corrected grids
of Section 4 with corrections max{Hii , 0}w2 /8, the two-node grids {ℓi , ui } for coordinates with
Hii ≤ 0, two-pass dynamic programming with all min-marginals, filtering, and CT (Algorithm 5.6)
in the common-mesh form of Lemma 5.9. It differs from the analysis in seven ways, none of which
affects validity (Appendix H lists them). In particular, all coordinates share the mesh hj = s2−j , so
the analysis that applies is Lemma 5.9 with the Euclidean condition number κ, not Theorem 5.7;
each trial starts with the center at the current incumbent, which exact coordinate descent provides
before stage 0 and may improve after every stage; and EX (Algorithm 6.13) uses a height constant
never smaller than the one in (6.1), starts at q = 4, doubles q after each round and restarts the grid
solver in every round. The “uniform” grids below are the grids of Definition 5.1 with θ = 0 around
one center, not the unions of cells of UC (Algorithm 9.2).
Experiments E1–E3 below run single trials, that is, TRIAL (Algorithm 5.3) with the common
mesh, a fixed grading θ (θ = 0 for uniform grids), no cap on the nodes per coordinate and a fixed
number of stages, in E1 also without filtering; the “theorem” grading is the largest 2−µ with 8κθ2 ≤ 1
for the certified upper bound on κ, which the algorithm itself does not know. Experiment E4 runs
EX; the default schedule of E2, experiments E5 and E6, the supplementary experiment S1, the
chain and the recourse runs use CT. In E2, E5, E6 and the chain no trial was aborted by the cap:
the only restarts occurred in E2, and each followed a trial that reached its stage limit.
Certificates store the grids, the directed messages and the filtering decisions of every stage,
which is more than Theorem 4.7 requires. A separate checker recomputes every table and message,
checks the Bellman equalities, the min-marginals, every exclusion, the incumbent values and the
final status, and never calls the grid generator, the dynamic program or the filtering code of the
solver; it shares model parsing, decomposition validation and objective evaluation with the solver.
The checker is independently written, not formally verified. On the runs of E1–E6, the chain and
the plain-grid recourse runs below with a solve time of at least 0.1 seconds, replay took between
0.81 and 2.54 times as long as the solve. Code, inputs, raw results and reproduction scripts (one
command per experiment group) accompany the paper.
All runs below used one thread per process and at most four processes on an Intel Xeon w5-2565X
under Windows Subsystem for Linux (WSL2) with Python 3.13, on a machine shared with unrelated
long-running jobs (load average 11 to 19). Timings are single measurements and indicative only.

11.5

Comparison with a global solver

Experiment E5 compares CT with SCIP 10.0 [98, 179] on 21 planted instances with κtarget = 4
(κ ∈ [3.99, 4.45]), with n = 8 to 128 and bag sizes 2 to 4. SCIP ran single-threaded on the epigraph
formulation min{t : t ≥ F (x)}, with the coefficients rounded to double precision, absolute gap 10−6
and a 20-second limit. CT used ε = 10−6 and a 60-second limit; it reached a replayed gap below
10−6 on all 21 instances in its first trial, in at most 6.9 seconds. SCIP reported its gap as reached on
the 12 instances with n ≤ 16 and stopped at the time limit on all nine paths with n ≥ 32 (Table 4
in Appendix H). Its dual bounds were valid, but its reported interval did not contain the optimum
in any run: every reported primal bound lay below the optimum, by 1.8 · 10−6 to 1.6 · 10−5 . SCIP’s
incumbents lay slightly outside the box: every coordinate that is at a bound in x∗ violated that
bound by about 10−8 , which SCIP’s feasibility tolerance permits. Moving such a coordinate outward
decreases F at the rate of its inward derivative, and these violations account for 51% to 94% of the
shortfall; a violation of the epigraph constraint by 9 · 10−7 accounts for the rest. Projected onto
the box, the incumbents were optimal to within 1.3 · 10−14 , and on the instances with n ≤ 16 their
exact gap to the dual bound was 2.8 · 10−6 to 6.8 · 10−6 , so the requested 10−6 was not reached.
This is the usual behavior of floating-point solvers, and rigorous bounds need safe computations
[149] or an exact check. A longer time limit, tighter tolerances or an objective offset did not change
these outcomes (Appendix H). The comparison uses one formulation, default settings and a family
that favors decompositions of small width; it is not a performance ranking.

Random instances without planted structure

Experiment E6 uses 40 unplanted random continuous box QPs on [−1, 1]n with path or band sparsity
(bag sizes 2 and 3), n = 8, 12, 16, 24 and five seeds each. All Hessians are indefinite, and nothing
about the minimizer or the conditioning is prescribed. CT was run at ε = 2−10 , 2−20 , . . . , 2−50 and

From ε = 2−10 to 2−50 it stayed the same on 27 of the 40 instances and changed by at most 5 nodes
on the others, while the number of stages grew linearly in log(1/ε), from 7–9 to 27–29 (Table 5 in
Appendix H). At the stationary point x̂ of the face of the final incumbent, Lemma 3.3(a) certified
point growth on 11 instances, with certified κ ≥ 4.5; there 8κθ2 > 1 for θ = 1/4, so the first trial
succeeded although Lemma 5.9 does not guarantee it. On the other 29 instances growth and κ
remain unknown; on 28 of them the localized test of Proposition 6.17 proved a candidate optimal,
and on one no candidate is proved optimal. For n = 8 all candidates agree with the enumeration.

11.7

Recourse

Six designed instances compare plain corrected grids (CT) with the recourse pipeline at target gap
2−10 and a 20-second limit (Table 6 in Appendix H). On two affine stars (n = 5 and 17), recognition
of the globally affine responses and an exact solve of the reduced problem took at most 0.07 seconds,
while plain grids stopped at the time limit with gaps 0.019 and 1.23. On the piecewise instances
ϕM − z 2 of Example C.11, the certified convex value factor removed the dependence on M , whereas
plain grids needed 498 table entries for M = 1 and 35,600 for M = 100. On a dense instance with
n = 33 whose residual is concave in each coordinate and submodular, the minimum-cut oracle of
Section 7.4 certified the target gap in 0.08 seconds; plain grids, with a decomposition of bag size 33,
stopped at the table limit before the first stage. All certificates were replayed; the checkers of the
recourse pipeline reuse the solver’s grid, table and lifting code and are therefore not independent
in the sense of Section 11.1. These are designed examples of the structures in Section 7, not hard
instances.

11.8

Limits

The cost of the method is exponential in the bag size, and exact rational arithmetic dominates
the running time; the band with bag size four already takes several seconds. On two binary
QPLIB instances [71] the first grid is exact, so the method is plain dynamic programming over the
decomposition. QPLIB_3852 (231 variables, minimum-fill bag size 20) was solved exactly in one
stage, with 5.7 · 106 table entries, 156 seconds and 4.4 GB of memory; the certified bounds coincide
at −234, the library’s value after the sign change to minimization. We did not replay this certificate,
because the reference checker is too slow at this size. For QPLIB_5881 (120 variables, bag size 94)
the first stage would need about 8 · 1028 table entries. Large width, not the accuracy, is the binding
limit; Section 10 shows that this is unavoidable in the worst case.

Conclusions and open problems

Small treewidth alone does not make sparse mixed-integer problems tractable. Upper bounds
on the coordinate curvature turn finite dynamic programming into a valid lower bound and its
min-marginals into a safe filter, and quadratic growth keeps the filtered grids at a size independent of
the accuracy. Under weighted growth, CT (Algorithm 5.6) certifies accuracy 2−q in f (p, κ̄)(I +q +1)5
bit operations, and under point growth exact output takes f1 (p, κ)(I + 1)C1 , without knowledge

69

of the growth constant; the certificates are checked by recomputation in exact arithmetic and are
valid without any growth assumption. By Theorem 1.2, neither parameter can be dropped unless
P = NP, and under rETH the exponent of κ in a bound f (p)κa(p) poly(I) cannot be o(p).
A corrected-grid bound on a subbox is a valid node bound for spatial branch-and-bound, and the
min-marginal filter is certified bound tightening; whether either pays off inside a solver is untested.
A replayable certificate also turns a floating-point incumbent into a certified one: the exact value
of its projection onto the box and a grid certificate give a rigorous gap, which matters because
floating-point solvers satisfy bounds and constraints only within tolerances (Section 11). In the
experiments, grid sizes stay nearly constant as the accuracy grows, also on random instances on
which growth could not be certified, and the width of the available decomposition, not the accuracy,
is the binding limit.
Several questions remain open.
(1) Negative curvature. Let g be a point-growth constant, ν = max{0, −λmin (∇2 F )} and κν =
max{1, ν/g}. Can continuous box QP be solved to accuracy 2−q in time f ′ (p, κν ) poly(I + q)
for a computable function f ′ ? Theorem 7.9 and Corollary C.10 (Appendix C) give such a
bound, with p the bag size after elimination, when certified responses of private convex blocks
reduce every coordinate curvature to at most C0 ν for a constant C0 . Proposition 7.11 shows
that recourse alone cannot ensure this, and Proposition G.5 (Appendix G.4) that matching
local moments of any finite order is not enough. These obstructions suggest measuring
curvature only where near-optimal points can lie, or using inequalities that couple both sides
of a separator.
