\section{The output is part of the optimization problem}
\label{sec:contracts}

Let $f$ be a rational polynomial and let $P$ be a rational polyhedron.
When the minimum is finite and attained, write $f_* = \min_P f$ and
$S=\argmin_{x\in P}f(x)$. This report distinguishes the following tasks.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item A \emph{value-gap point} is a feasible rational point $x$ with
$f(x)-f_*\le\varepsilon$. A certified value interval additionally provides
a justified lower bound $\ell\le f_*$ with $f(x)-\ell\le\varepsilon$.
\item A \emph{point approximation to the optimizer set} satisfies
$x\in P$ and $\dist(x,S)\le\varepsilon$. Its objective guarantee is a
separate statement unless a continuity bound is also provided.
\item A \emph{fixed-selector oracle} approximates the same point $p\in S$
for every requested precision. In the convex setting our selector is the
unique point of minimum original Euclidean norm in $S$.
\item An \emph{exact comparison} decides an order or equality relation,
including cases with zero slack. A sufficiently small numerical residual
does not decide equality.
\item An \emph{exact representation} describes the optimizer or optimum
without numerical error. A polynomial system with a unique designated
solution can be short even when expanded rational fractions or algebraic
coordinates are long.
\end{enumerate}

Our precision parameter is $q$, with $\varepsilon=2^{-q}$. Polynomial
dependence on $q$ is different from polynomial dependence on
$1/\varepsilon$. A fixed-selector oracle is a Cauchy name: the algorithm
returns rational approximations at successively requested accuracies,
without claiming a short expanded representation of the limiting point.

\paragraph{Input and computation.}
Rational coefficients and constraint entries have ordinary binary encoding.
An explicitly sparse polynomial lists each nonzero coefficient and its
exponent vector; repeated monomials are combined. A bound polynomial in the
numerical degree $D$ is a bit-polynomial bound when $D$ is unary encoded or
polynomially bounded by the remaining input size. It is not a bound
polynomial in a binary degree encoding. A succinct arithmetic circuit is a
different input format and is not silently substituted for the explicit
coefficient list.

An ordinary bit algorithm manipulates explicitly encoded numbers. A shared
arithmetic circuit stores the operations defining a number without expanding
it. The problem $\PosSLP$ asks whether the integer output of a circuit with
constants $0,1$ and operations $+,-,\times$ is positive. A polynomial-time
reduction to this problem is not an ordinary polynomial-time algorithm for
exact comparison. Nor does $\PosSLP$-hardness assert NP-hardness or that the
problem lies outside P.

\paragraph{Structural promises.}
Convexity on all of $\R^n$, convexity only on $P$, and a supplied positive
strong-convexity modulus are different assumptions. A theorem with a
convexity promise does not include a free algorithm for recognizing that
promise. A supplied rational matrix identity can sometimes give a
polynomially checkable certificate; its encoding and verification cost then
belong to the input contract. For nonconvex core models, a supplied joint
quadratic convexifier is stronger than convexity of every residual fiber.

\paragraph{Relationship with prior convex polynomial optimization.}
Slot, Steurer, and Wiedmer prove effective solution bounds and a
polynomial-time value-gap algorithm for globally convex polynomials over
rational polyhedra. Their structural decomposition uses the polynomial's
directions of invariance and linearity. Those tools and the classical convex
value-oracle framework are prior work. The point-output questions here ask
for effective conversion from objective error to distance, and for a
consistent selected point. The precise comparison and the scope of any
extension must be made at the theorem level.
