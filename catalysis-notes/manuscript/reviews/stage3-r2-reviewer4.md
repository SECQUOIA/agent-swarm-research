## Resolution of previous findings

**The pressure-enabled saving claim now has the necessary comparator.** `manuscript/sections/03-polymer.tex:77–83` crosses both pressures with all three existing makeup doses and explicitly retains the nonzero-dose tests regardless of the zero-dose response. This avoids closing a partial-substitution question from a negative complete-substitution test.

At lines 97–99, the predictor retains pressure–dose interaction, and the withheld dose is validated independently at both pressures under matched charge-1 histories. Both predictions are frozen before either result is collected. The requirement that the lower-pressure policy pass while the same reference-pressure dose informatively fails an acceptance criterion now distinguishes pressure-enabled dose reduction from discovering a lower adequate dose at either pressure. The both-pass and unresolved-reference outcomes are interpreted correctly. The design claims no global optimum over unmeasured doses.

**The longer-run extension now defines an executable policy.** Lines 101–103 specify the intervention trigger, common accepted-output target, maximum duration and charge count, full-mixture removal and accounting, fresh replacement inventory, and restart of the assigned validated schedule. Failed charges and all replacement time and solids enter the comparison. The text preserves the common first-charge history where that history was used in validation and avoids assuming selective W replacement or a qualified regeneration method.

The common target supplies the credited denominator, with collected excess reported separately. An arm that cannot reach the target within the bounds receives no extrapolated credit. This supports a bounded comparison of sustained catalyst demand under the stated replacement rule; it does not imply indefinite service or an optimized replacement policy. The correction fully addresses the original ambiguity between stopping at the first intervention and measuring the costs that follow it.

## Whole-program assessment

The sequence remains coherent: reproduce the documented reuse benchmark, qualify its transfer to semibatch operation, measure the finite pressure–dose response, validate one prospective catalyst-use decision, and test that decision under a defined replacement policy. The added comparisons address the actual inferential gaps without creating a broad pressure or regeneration campaign.

The contribution is consequential if successful. It concerns reduced fresh catalyst solids per accepted polymer-derived carbon output at specified quality and complete time. It is not another demonstration of fresh-Na rescue, ethylene-pressure sensitivity, or heterogeneous catalyst reuse. The chapter fairly credits those precedents and keeps the stronger mechanistic contribution conditional.

Carbon-origin and inventory accounting remain sound within the stated qualifications. The He tracer now has an explicit detector route and method-qualification requirements; enriched-ethylene attribution still requires consistent source enrichment, complete isotopologue calibration, bounded discrimination, and classified carbon inputs. Neither residual-carbon disappearance nor an ethylene-derived increase in propylene can silently become credited polymer recovery.

The chapter also maintains the right limits on causal interpretation. Fresh-Na rescue does not prove unchanged W function; lower ethylene is an operating intervention rather than an intrinsic reaction-order measurement; workup-sensitive chain distributions cannot be labeled hot-state observations; and matched unsaturation or small-molecule probes require qualification before supporting a selective-initiation claim. A useful operating result can survive failure of those mechanism claims.

The stop rules now match the questions. A zero-dose null closes only rescue without makeup; broader closure requires informative crossed results at the tested doses. An unresolved attribution prevents a polymer-carbon saving claim. Failure of a withheld prediction remains failure, and replacement costs can erase an initial inventory advantage. Unknown effect sizes, available measurement precision, and actual reactor access remain experimental dependencies rather than defects in the proposed design.
