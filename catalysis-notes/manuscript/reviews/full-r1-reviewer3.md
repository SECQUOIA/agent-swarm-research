**Minor finding 1: state the stability condition when calling the feedback coefficient a relaxation rate.** Location: `manuscript/sections/01-water.tex:99`, in Experiment 3, the sentence deriving `(G − ∂q/∂c)/B` from Eq. `water-pool`.

The derivation is correct. With a fixed boundary and a perturbation about a stationary state, however, decay requires `B > 0` and `G − ∂q/∂c > 0`. Equality gives no restoring term in the linearized equation; a negative numerator produces growth, not relaxation. The present wording calls the expression a relaxation rate without stating that condition. This matters specifically because the paragraph introduces reaction feedback as a reason not to infer conductance from an observed pole.

Proportionate remedy: append “for a stable stationary state, where `G > ∂q/∂c`,” with the derivative evaluated at the operating state. No additional model or experiment is needed. This is a mathematical-domain clarification, not a failure of the transient argument or the proposed stopping rule.

**Assessment of the central inference contracts.** The water compartment equations conserve total water when the pool and gas balances are added. The two-pool construction is a valid structural nonidentifiability example: outlet concentration and total inventory can remain unchanged while the producing pool's excess concentration differs. The distinction between pool-to-gas and pool-to-inlet concentration prevents an erroneous threefold claim about total exposure. The functional validation also correctly recognizes that full-recovery integrated deficits can coincide despite different time constants.

The cyclic chapter balances the carbonate/hydroxide reactions and gives the correct equilibrium quotient. It separates ordinary-cycle steam compatibility, performance after a special reset, and paid operating recovery. The direct wet timing contrast and its interaction with dry timing answer different questions; the closing rules preserve that distinction. The time-productivity inequality is algebraically correct for positive comparator output and positive elapsed cycle times. Carbonate capture/release prevents assigning regeneration CO2 directly to coke, and lithium export is not treated as identification of a vapor species.

The polymer isotope equation follows from two-source carbon-atom mixing. Keeping ethylene enrichment constant throughout conditioning and reuse makes source attribution compatible with retained carbon from earlier charges; the manuscript correctly restricts the primary endpoint to cumulative sequence output. The crossed dose/pressure design and validation at both pressures can distinguish an adequate lower makeup dose from a saving enabled by pressure. Full-mixture replacement, failed charges, discarded residue, and complete time enter the bounded extension, so a short three-charge improvement is not automatically presented as sustained catalyst savings.

The Ag integral and preparation-cost inequality have consistent units and boundaries. Recovery benefit is calculated against the same material's matched continuation, then converted to absolute campaign output before ranking all tested admissible policies. This avoids treating a large within-material recovery gain as proof of the best catalyst. Untreated operation remains in the comparison; preparation damage is not hidden by a favorable Ni/sham contrast. The symbolic economic boundary is appropriate without verified prices or equipment costs.

Across the overview and shortlist, priority is presented as a development judgment rather than a measured industrial ranking. Consequential differences, independent experimental units, balance uncertainty, finite horizons, and withheld predictions recur consistently. Pilot-selected thresholds are appropriate in a proposal where reproducibility and measurement precision have not yet been established. I do not recommend inventing numerical power, lifetime, or economic claims to fill those experimental dependencies.
