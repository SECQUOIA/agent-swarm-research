\section{Physical water management: when does a neighboring hydrophobic material protect a catalyst?}
\label{sec:water}

\textbf{Summary.} Cobalt Fischer--Tropsch synthesis converts CO and hydrogen (syngas) to hydrocarbons and water; C\textsubscript{5+} denotes hydrocarbons with at least five carbon atoms. On graphite-supported cobalt, externally supplied water at very low conversion, followed by a dry return, has been linked to oxidation, sintering, and lost function.\cite{wolf2019} Physically mixing hydrophobic polydivinylbenzene (PDVB) with a catalyst has promoted CO hydrogenation on Co--Mn carbide and \COtwo{} hydrogenation on copper.\cite{fang2022,li2023} On Co/SiO\textsubscript{2}, Fang and colleagues (2026) found that 0.025\,g PDVB per 0.5\,g catalyst kept CO conversion near 44\% and C\textsubscript{5+} selectivity at 88--90\% for 1200\,h, while a quartz-diluted reference declined strongly.\cite{fang2026} From their Source Data, we calculate a C\textsubscript{5+} output proxy over 25--585\,h of 1.89 times the reference's (1.80 per unit catalyst-plus-polymer mass). A simple storage model shows why outlet water transients alone cannot identify active-site water exposure. What is new is adding PDVB late, after the catalyst has run without it (common conditioning), and asking whether it still protects the catalyst and whether measured water and catalytic responses predict output under a new disturbance. We propose comparing late PDVB with matched quartz under a controlled water challenge with time-matched controls and, conditionally, predicting C\textsubscript{5+} output under a withheld humidity pulse. The outcome would be a controlled protection test at one catalyst state and disturbance and, if prediction succeeds, a predictive rule within the calibrated range.

\subsection{What is known and what remains open}

PDVB promotion of Co--Mn carbide in syngas conversion showed mixing-distance and water-cofeed effects, and the carbide recovered after PDVB removal performed comparably to the original; physical promotion also works for copper-catalyzed methanol synthesis.\cite{fang2022,li2023} Physical mixing, post-use additive removal, transfer to another reaction, and contact-angle correlations are therefore not new.

Fang's Co/SiO\textsubscript{2} test ran at 220\,$^\circ$C and 2\,MPa with separately prepared 40--60 mesh granules; the quartz-diluted reference, at matched nominal bed volume, initially converted more CO. Water-cofeed, heat-transfer, material-state, and mixing controls support a substantial functional effect,\cite{fang2026} but a mechanism requires separating retained water, water transport, reaction rate, and catalyst history.

The output proxy integrates CO conversion $X_{\mathrm{CO}}$ times the published carbon-based C\textsubscript{5+} selectivity $S_{\mathrm{C5+}}$ over the common 25--585\,h interval,
\begin{equation}
 Q^*=\int X_{\mathrm{CO}}(t)S_{\mathrm{C5+}}(t)\,dt
 =116.0\ \text{h (reference)},\quad 219.8\ \text{h (PDVB)},
 \label{eq:water-output}
\end{equation}
using sheet ``Figure 1'' of Fang's Source Data workbook (equal nominal CO feed), piecewise-linear interpolation of each series, and the combined time grid. The 1.80 ratio divides by the 5\% larger catalyst-plus-polymer mass; quartz is not counted. The proxy omits the first 25\,h and is not a complete carbon balance, an uncertainty interval, or a lifetime extrapolation; it does not show superiority to an optimized cobalt formulation or regeneration policy.\cite{fang2026}

Water history and pore environment also have precedents: water pretreatment versus later cofeed and dry return on alumina-supported cobalt;\cite{hanssen1997} activation-water effects on later cobalt performance;\cite{paterson2024} silica-shell geometry and deactivation, separating water-related from heavy-hydrocarbon-related behavior;\cite{yang2020} and strongly retained oxygenates that change water affinity during operation,\cite{sengupta2025} so a dry fresh-material contact angle cannot be assumed to represent a working catalyst. What is new here is \emph{late addition after common conditioning}.

\subsection{Why outlet water transients cannot locate active-site water exposure}

Faster water clearance alone does not show that active sites see less water. Surfaces of different wettability hold different amounts of water at one chemical potential, but this alone creates no steady gradient across an otherwise equilibrated, connected gas; only reaction, imposed flow, or another source or sink can. Wettability then acts through storage, liquid configuration, and transport resistance, so equilibrium loading differences alone cannot explain continuous drying.

\paragraph{One pool: a shorter transient need not mean lower exposure.} A shorter transient can come from less storage or from faster exchange; only faster exchange lowers the steady water excess:
\begin{equation}
 B\frac{dc}{dt}=q-G(c-c_b),\qquad
 \tau=\frac{B}{G},\qquad c_{\mathrm{ss}}-c_b=\frac{q}{G}.
 \label{eq:water-pool}
\end{equation}
Here $c$ and $c_b$ are local and boundary concentrations expressed as equivalent gas-phase concentrations, $B$ is differential storage capacity (volume), $G$ is exchange conductance (volume/time), and $q$ is water production (amount/time), for a locally linear, time-invariant compartment with a specified source. Smaller $B$ leaves the steady excess unchanged and can raise exposure early in an upward step while shortening the tail, so less storage need not improve durability. Fang's syngas-to-Ar decay times, about 80\,s for the PDVB mixture and 175\,s for Co/SiO\textsubscript{2} alone, cannot separate these cases, because that switch also changes water production and adsorbed species.\cite[Fig.~3d]{fang2026}

\paragraph{Two pools: the outlet does not locate the source.} Even absolute inventory plus several outlet transients need not identify where water is produced. Two pools have concentrations $c_i$, capacities $B_i$, and conductances $G_i=\lambda B_i$ ($\lambda$: common exchange rate constant, inverse time); $B_T=B_1+B_2$ and $W=B_1c_1+B_2c_2$. With total water production $q_T$, inlet concentration $c_{\mathrm{in}}$, a well-mixed gas volume $V$ at concentration $c_g$, and actual volumetric flow $F$,
\begin{align}
 \dot W&=\lambda(B_Tc_g-W)+q_T,\\
 V\dot c_g&=F(c_{\mathrm{in}}-c_g)+\lambda(W-B_Tc_g).
 \label{eq:water-two-pool}
\end{align}
Of the pool properties, only $B_T$, $q_T$, and the aggregate inventory $W$ enter the outlet $c_g$. If pool 1 produces all the water, its steady excess over the gas, $c_{1,\mathrm{ss}}-c_{g,\mathrm{ss}}=q_T/(\lambda B_1)$, falls threefold when $B_1$ moves from $B_T/4$ to $3B_T/4$, with no change in any outlet response for identical aggregate initial conditions. (The pool-to-inlet excess does not generally scale threefold, because it includes the unchanged $c_{g,\mathrm{ss}}-c_{\mathrm{in}}=q_T/F$.) This constructed example, not fitted to PDVB, shows that a correct outlet fit establishes only a formulation response. An active-site claim needs a source-specific or spatial constraint, which isotope signals do not automatically supply. A stationary transport explanation must also locate the relevant resistance: changing an external resistance cannot remove an unchanged internal one, and granule size alone cannot separate them.

\subsection{Experiment 1: qualify the platform and the late-addition procedure}

Reproduce the low-dose, separate-granule contrast with measured inventory, bed volume, temperature, pressure drop, carbon balance, and useful-product rate; the source's powder co-granulate imaging and some high-dose controls do not describe this packing. The source restart is incompletely specified (synthesis includes reduction and passivation, but Supplementary Note~10 calls the standard test hydrogen-pre-reduced), so establish one reproducible activation sequence rather than silently adopting the 300\,$^\circ$C analytical pretreatment.\cite{fang2026}

Condition independently preweighed charges from one preparation, each keeping its identical initial quartz dilution throughout handling, without PDVB in syngas (initially H\textsubscript{2}/CO/Ar $=64/32/4$, 220\,$^\circ$C, 2\,MPa). A pilot on the parent (PDVB-free) Co/SiO\textsubscript{2} catalyst sets the duration beyond startup from rate and selectivity drift over a defined observation window, with tolerances from pilot variability; fix it and the complete operating schedule before randomization, and report pre-handling drift and retained liquid. This defines an operationally conditioned age, not microscopic stationarity or stable water affinity.

Assign charges randomly to the four conditions in Table~\ref{tab:water-design}. Recover each complete catalyst-and-quartz zone under inert gas with its retained liquid and, after showing quantitative solid recovery, normalize to the original dry catalyst inventory and record liquid loss separately. Cooling, transfer, and restart are identical for all charges, with no solvent wash or new reduction before the challenge.

Repack each whole charge with 0.05\,g PDVB/g catalyst or the same measured bulk volume of quartz, to equal final bed volume, with fixed granule size, no selective removal of initial diluent, and measured void volume and pressure drop in every repacked bed rather than assumed equal hydrodynamics. A handling qualification pilot compares restarted parent charges with an uninterrupted parent control (packing, heat and flow behavior, solid and liquid recovery). A reproducible shift can define the post-handling starting state; uncontrolled solid or liquid loss or an erratic restart ends the late-addition work. Handling cannot recreate uninterrupted operation; this limits interpretation.

\subsection{Experiment 2: distinguish protection from an activity penalty}

Select one water challenge in a parent-only feasibility study, starting from 220\,$^\circ$C and 20\,bar (2\,MPa) total with inlet $p_{\mathrm{H2}}=10$\,bar and $p_{\mathrm{CO}}=5$\,bar, and water raised from 0.5 to 4\,bar by replacing Ar at fixed inlet molar flow. These are design choices, not a demonstrated damage threshold for Co/SiO\textsubscript{2}. Fix the plateau and recovery durations before the four-condition comparison; the plateau must outlast measured equilibration and leave measurable but noncatastrophic residual loss in the parent. If no such disturbance exists within the qualified operating range, stop the damage test.

Target at most 2\% CO conversion in the more active arm. With one water per CO converted,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dot n_{\mathrm{water,gen}}}{\dot n_{\mathrm{water,in}}}
 \simeq\frac{X_{\mathrm{CO}}y_{\mathrm{CO,in}}}{y_{\mathrm{water,in}}}
 =0.025\ \text{at 4 bar water},\qquad 0.20\ \text{at 0.5 bar water},
 \label{eq:water-source}
\end{equation}
so external water dominates far more on the high plateau than during recovery; balances (CO\textsubscript{2}, oxygenates, gas contraction, unresolved oxygen storage) give the actual ratios. Low conversion limits whole-bed reactant changes, not necessarily intragranular gradients; before fixing the flow, check that a modest flow increase leaves the mass-normalized diagnostic rate materially unchanged. For equipment sizing only, and assuming the source's nominal promoted CO consumption persists (not a rate prediction), roughly 68\,L\,(g catalyst h)$^{-1}$ is needed for 2\% conversion at inlet CO fraction 0.25: for a 50\,mg charge, about 3.4 standard L/h of gas and 0.54\,g/h of supplied water at 20\% steam (0\,$^\circ$C volume convention). Reducing inventory makes the test practical without reducing production per granule.

For each charge, measure C\textsubscript{5+} carbon-production rates before the challenge and after recovery ($r_{\mathrm{pre}}$, $r_{\mathrm{post}}$, after defined settling windows). With $R=r_{\mathrm{post}}/r_{\mathrm{pre}}$, the primary protection contrast is
\begin{equation}
 D=(R_{\mathrm{PDVB,challenge}}-R_{\mathrm{PDVB,sham}})
  -(R_{\mathrm{quartz,challenge}}-R_{\mathrm{quartz,sham}}).
 \label{eq:water-interaction}
\end{equation}
A positive $D$ means that PDVB reduces the fractional loss of function associated with the challenge, beyond time-matched drift. The usefulness endpoint is the difference in integrated C\textsubscript{5+} carbon output per complete bed volume over the fixed restart--challenge--recovery horizon, including the initial activity penalty, startup product inventories, and collection delays; report catalyst-plus-additive mass productivity separately. Because PDVB stays in the bed during recovery, better retention shows that the complete formulation works, not how many Co sites survive or that the avoided loss would have been irreversible.

A positive $D$ after late addition rules out an explanation confined to the original conditioning, not effects during restart, the water transient, or ongoing liquid or chemical changes. The alternative that PDVB acts only by changing how much reaction water the bed makes is rejected only if parent-only runs span both formulations' measured water histories, with uncertainty, in both challenge and recovery; the plateau alone is not enough, because generated water matters relatively more during recovery. Otherwise keep that alternative and report the formulation contrast. A null result after successful reproduction and qualification limits the supported benefit of late addition in this state; it does not show that startup protection caused the published result.

\subsection{Experiment 3: predict output under a withheld humidity pulse}

Pursue prediction only if Experiment~2 or the reversible water response poses a resolvable functional question. Zero-length-column work constrains water exchange in silica gel from storage data and flow-dependent transients, but its passive, low-temperature coefficients do not transfer to a reacting, liquid-bearing cobalt bed.\cite{brandani2022}

Qualify water-specific blanks (heated transfer lines, pressure reduction, detector memory) at operating temperature, pressure, and composition; an inert tracer gives gas delay but not water-specific storage. On conditioned parent and PDVB packings, apply small upward and downward humidity steps at two flows with unchanged inlet H\textsubscript{2} and CO partial pressures, plus a reduced-amplitude repeat and baseline returns, integrating absolute water rates and measuring product rates fast enough to bound the changing chemical water source. Correct lower uptake per total mass for dilution: for additive/catalyst mass ratio $a$, noninteracting uptake is $(u_{\mathrm{cat}}+a u_{\mathrm{add}})/(1+a)$ even at unchanged catalyst loading.

Fit the simplest response model consistent with both step directions, both flows, the source balance, and independent packings. A measured decay rate is not automatically $G/B$: in a reacting compartment, linearizing Eq.~\ref{eq:water-pool} with $q=q(c)$ gives $(G-\partial q/\partial c)/B$, for $B>0$ and a stable stationary state with $G>\partial q/\partial c$ there. If source feedback, blank uncertainty, or a changing liquid state prevents a meaningful coefficient, drop the conductance claim and test only an empirical input--output model, whose coefficients are not local transport properties.

The withheld test is a water pulse shorter than full equilibration. Fix in advance a horizon from pulse onset (the available production window) and an acceptable C\textsubscript{5+} carbon-output deficit relative to undisturbed operation, so the endpoint says whether that excursion stays within budget. Calibrate each formulation's nearby reversible product response, including product-detector and liquid-collection delays, and choose a pulse duration for which surviving models predict importantly different deficits over that fixed horizon; distinct water traces are not enough, since different time constants with equal steady gains can give the same deficit integrated through complete recovery. Predict the water trace and the deficit with an endpoint interval narrower than the smallest important difference. The baseline uses inlet humidity, the calibrated steady product response, and measured apparatus delays. Success supports an operating-history rule within the calibrated state and amplitude range; if transient characterization does not beat the baseline, claim only the empirical rule. Neither outcome validates a damage law at much higher water exposure.

\subsection{Decisions and stop rules}

The results decide whether a simple formulation change pays for its initial penalty under the tested conditions, and when faster water clearance is an inadequate selection criterion. Stop the affected line of work if the source effect cannot be reproduced, handling destroys interpretability, the challenge has no measurable dynamic range, or the predicted functional difference is below measurement resolution. If fractional protection is real but cumulative output is worse, report the tradeoff and claim no practical advance. A useful formulation result stands without an identified mechanism; a mechanism paper would need a constraint that locates the producing region and stays valid when the additive changes liquid and catalyst state. A well-fitted water curve without an important functional prediction does not justify a larger materials or isotope campaign. Commercial claims need a later complete-cycle comparison with an optimized catalyst, including regeneration, additive inventory, bed volume, and replacement time.
