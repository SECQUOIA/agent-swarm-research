# Minimum accounting for peroxide generation coupled to epoxidation

2026-09-15. Conditional stoichiometric calculation supporting the practical opportunity screen. This is not a catalyst-performance prediction or process energy estimate. No experiment was performed.

## One common boundary

Consider olefin epoxidation consuming one molecule of H2O2 per epoxide, with H2O2 generated by a two-electron O2-reduction step and no external epoxide feed. Over the same complete operating interval, let `G` be gross electrochemical peroxide formation, `A` externally supplied peroxide, `P` net target epoxide produced, `D` peroxide consumed without leaving the target product, `X` peroxide exported unused, and `ΔH` the increase in all peroxide inventories inside the boundary. Here `D` includes peroxide destruction in the generator, decomposition and competing oxidation downstream, and peroxide spent on epoxide that subsequently becomes a different product. Then

`G + A = P + D + X + ΔH`.

Include startup, shutdown, tanks, recycle and retained liquid. A measured catalytic utilization greater than one can result from emptying an initial peroxide inventory if that term is omitted. Net epoxide production also needs a product inventory balance; exported saleable product and net product produced are separate measures when there are collection or purification losses. If glycol or another coproduct is useful, report it separately and state the product objective rather than silently adding it to epoxide yield.

With no external peroxide and no net inventory change, define delivered charge efficiency

`η_product = 2 F P / Q`,

where `Q = integral I(t) dt`. Under the specified stoichiometry this equals the Faradaic efficiency of gross peroxide formation times the fraction of that peroxide that reaches the desired product. Gross formation may be experimentally inseparable from immediate destruction; measured net peroxide leaving a generator is a different quantity. If net delivered peroxide is used instead, exclude the already deducted generator losses from the downstream loss term. That factorization is useful only when both quantities describe the **same coupled operating state and interval**. Multiplying the best efficiencies measured in independently optimized generator and catalyst tests does not predict the coupled result. Electrolyte return, substrate crossover and altered residence times can change either factor. The direct product-to-charge ratio avoids requiring that partition.

The same two-electron product accounting can apply to anodic peroxide formation from water, provided that this is the counted source and the opposite electrode does not supply a second peroxide stream. It does not imply the same chemistry or energy demand as O2 reduction. If another oxygenation route contributes, both electrodes generate useful peroxide, or a chemical reductant generates peroxide outside the counted electrode reaction, the assumed electron-to-product relation must be revised. A zero applied bias does not mean zero resource or energy input: consumption of a chemical reductant and its products belongs in the comparison.

## Electrical energy and liquid throughput

For constant full-cell voltage `V`, the cell electricity per mass of epoxide is

`E_cell = 2 F V / (M η_product)`.

Use the measured full-cell voltage, not an electrode potential relative to a reference electrode. With time-varying operation, calculate `integral V(t) I(t) dt` directly. Pumps, gas supply, temperature control and separations are additional terms; heat or chemical-reductant inputs cannot be hidden in this cell-only expression.

**Illustration only:** for propylene oxide, `M ≈ 0.05808 kg/mol`, a 2 V full cell at unit delivered charge efficiency requires approximately **1.85 kWh/kg PO** at the cell boundary; 3 V gives approximately **2.77 kWh/kg**. At half that efficiency the respective values double. These voltages and efficiencies are chosen arithmetic examples, not reported operating points or commercial benchmarks.

For one-pass peroxide solution concentration `c` in mol/L and target utilization `u`, the liquid volume carrying the stoichiometric oxidant per kilogram PO is

`L_per_kg = 1 / (M c u)`.

At `u = 1`, hypothetical 0.1 M and 0.01 M streams carry about **172 and 1722 L/kg PO**, respectively. This is hydraulic throughput, not net water consumption or evaporation duty. Recirculating electrolyte and extracting or stripping product may substantially change those burdens; conversely, salt purge and crossover may prevent perfect recycle. A practical comparison must measure those streams before claiming solvent or separation savings.

## What changes a catalyst decision

Report target-product output, peroxide loss, charge, full-cell energy and chemical feed consumption over the same period. Account for organic solvents, chemical reductants and their carbon-containing products separately from the olefin carbon balance. A more peroxide-tolerant catalyst is useful only if it increases delivered output or reduces a consequential recurring burden after generator/catalyst coupling. Higher PO selectivity among carbon products can coexist with poor peroxide utilization. A high peroxide Faradaic efficiency can coexist with low delivered product efficiency. Neither metric alone establishes a useful tandem process.

This accounting does not claim novelty. It is the minimum comparison needed to decide whether a proposed catalyst intervention addresses a real coupled-process loss.

Independent check: the peroxide opportunity reviewer verified the stoichiometry and numerical examples, and identified the need to distinguish net product from epoxide later hydrolyzed, gross from delivered peroxide, and anodic from cathodic generation. Those qualifications are incorporated above.
