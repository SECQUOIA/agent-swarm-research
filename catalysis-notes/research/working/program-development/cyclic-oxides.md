**Recommendation after the uploaded-literature audit: retain a narrower, conditional reserve on steam-purge compatibility of the specified coated LSF.** A general distinction between prevention and failed later carbonate recovery is already demonstrated in adjacent molten-salt work; it is not the original contribution of this proposal. The documented loss and a credible intervention justify development before any positive pilot result exists. The first commitment is the defined steam/CO2 timing comparison below, with a dry Ar reference and full material balances. A separate recovery campaign on representative Ar-aged material is conditional follow-on work. Neither assumes shell chemistry can be varied independently of ferrite state or morphology. The program's value would be a causal explanation and an operating rule that avoids costly irreversible loss; identifying the mechanism is part of the research objective.

## Final scope and order of work

The completed proposal is a **conditional reserve experiment**, consistent with the [latest portfolio assessment](../../reviews/program-development-portfolio-review.md#reconsideration-after-the-concrete-first-tests-and-comparator-audits-2026-09-16). No further salt, core or material search is proposed. The [timing experiment](cyclic-oxide-causal-intervention.md) is the first concrete test: does concurrent CO2 during a post-oxidation steam exposure prevent a persistent coating/function change that an equal later CO2 dose cannot restore? The thermodynamic direction is supported in related melts; hydrolysis rate, Li escape and useful protection of LSF remain unestablished.

The [prevention-value calculation](../../calculations/cyclic-oxide-prevention-value.md) makes the practical decision explicit. Complete restoration of the reported Ar-aged C2+ yield would allow only about **2.02 added minutes per 16-minute cycle** before losing its throughput advantage, even before charging gas and heat. That is a conditional benchmark for the documented Ar loss, not an estimate of steam damage. The dilute-steam diagnostic is not a steam-rich purge; maintaining its steam/CO2 ratio in a binary mixture would require one-third CO2. No protective dose is known. Dry Ar is the demonstrated comparator; recycled CO2 and promoter makeup have not been validated as superior alternatives for this material.

Proceed in this order:

1. Confirm access to a reproducible coated LSF, high-temperature gas switching with measured steam/CO2, direct product/water analysis, and defensible Li inventory recovery. Actual access and achievable limits are unknown. If the balance cannot resolve a consequential change at realistic exposure, state that limitation before commissioning long aging.
2. Perform the single equal-dose timing pair, with intermediate sister specimens, a dry reference, common measured final carbonation endpoint and CO2 washout. Retain both resolved effects and useful null bounds. This compares treatment order; it does not explain the existing Ar-protocol decline.
3. If the result supports a consequential difference, test that pair at one explicitly chosen steam-rich gas-handling boundary in actual reaction cycles. Compare sustained product, loss, net gas use and additional time against delayed recovery. A measured carbonate replacement step becomes relevant only if persistent material loss makes it a real comparator. Do not start a gas-composition library.
4. Use representative Ar-aged material and the four-arm campaign detailed below only if it is available and its recovery question remains consequential. That is a distinct causal branch, not an additional mandatory first matrix. Extend to a held-out operating history only after evidence identifies the state change that should predict its response.

**Continue** toward a substantial program when exposure changes recoverability or imposes a meaningful limit on steam-process translation, and measured chemistry/inventory narrows the cause enough to change an operating or replacement decision. **Stop the protective intervention** if ordinary recovery already restores the useful output at lower burden, if the gain disappears after CO2 washout and equivalent conditioning, or if process-relevant exposure produces no consequential loss within a useful bound. A null result at one gas dose does not exclude all protection chemistry. Do not replace a failed practical hypothesis with a trace-Li characterization project or claim universal irreversibility from a finite reset.

## The consequential question

On Li2CO3-coated La0.8Sr0.2FeO3 (LSF) during ethane chemical-looping oxidative dehydrogenation, **which exposures change loss of selective function from recoverable to irrecoverable, and what material change controls that boundary?** Reversible carbonate/oxide/hydroxide chemistry, coupled wetting or redistribution, permanent Li loss, and ferrite transformation are competing or sequential causes. Can measured inventories and phase changes predict how much useful production a specified regeneration treatment will restore after a different operating history?

The decision this would change is concrete. If a retained promoter can be restored chemically or redistributed beneficially, a regeneration atmosphere could recover function without replacing lithium or catalyst. If Li leaves the relevant system or a persistent interfacial phase forms, shell retention, synthesis, or replacement becomes the appropriate lever. Some restructuring can itself be reversible, so chemistry and morphology must not be equated with recoverability and irrecoverability. Full-cycle productivity determines whether recovery is worth its added time and gas consumption.

This is connected directly to Iglesia's quantitative oxide-redox kinetics and distinction between thermodynamic oxygen inventory and kinetically available oxygen. It also uses the broader mechanistic practice shared with Gounder: quantify retained function separately from total material, perturb a proposed cause, and require an independently predicted response. It is not an extension of the bare-ZrO2 Lewis acid–base/vacancy dispute. The promoter-coated, reducible ferrite has a different material state and operating temperature.

## Evidence and important limits on novelty

**The foundational material is established.** Gao et al. report a molten carbonate shell, strong ethane-ODH selectivity, and about 0.42 wt% oxygen consumption in six minutes at 700 °C. Their account assigns selective oxygen delivery to peroxide transport and Fe4+/Fe3+ redox. They explicitly leave the declining release rate as a combination of transport, driving force, and surface reaction. Their isotope exchange, phase measurements, and calculations already make generic oxygen-shuttling and shell-barrier proposals prior art. Main article read in open HTML; [Gao et al., Science Advances 2020](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aaz9339), Figs. 2–5 and associated text.

**The longevity demonstration is substantial but qualified.** Brody et al. use 300 g in a heated packed bed. The first 1,000 h comprise one minute of ethane, five minutes of Ar, five minutes of O2/Ar, and five minutes of Ar per cycle. C2+ selectivity falls from 85.1% to 79.4%; C2+ includes products besides ethylene. Later operating-condition changes improve performance, followed by further decline. Thus high selectivity at the later optimum was not maintained throughout 1,200 h. Measured exotherms support an autothermal process model; the experiment is not proof of a continuously producing, heat-self-sufficient plant. Surface characterization suggests less carbonate but does not distinguish its transformation from redistribution or loss. Main PDF read, especially printed pp. 3–4 and 7–9; [Brody et al., Energy & Fuels 2022](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.energyfuels.2c01293), [open manuscript](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/2001472).

Several source details affect reproduction. The major selectivity decline appears around 300–400 elapsed hours, so short fresh-material tests may miss it. The first 1,000 elapsed hours contain about 62.5 scheduled hours of ethane contact. The methods identify nominal 10 wt% Li2CO3, whereas the manuscript conclusion says 20 wt%; use the methods-based 10 wt% provisionally and retain the discrepancy. The process model uses steam purges, unlike the experiment's Ar purges; effects of that change on the material remain unverified. These points were checked against the manuscript and the independent review.

**A declining rate before complete reduction is already explained mechanistically on a different oxide.** Hwang, Getsoian, and Iglesia describe CZO reduction using nonideal solid-solution thermodynamics and composition-dependent adsorption/transition-state energetics. Repeating “nominal capacity is not useful capacity” on LSF would be inadequate. The transferable opportunity is to test whether a separate promoter state is necessary, beyond the oxide state and transport already required by such a model. [Local read note](../../../literature/papers/hwang2024-kinetics-mechanism-and-thermodynamics-of/paper.md), pp. 6–8, 15–18; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acscatal.4c04771).

**The carbonate is not presumptively inert.** Molten-salt-mediated ethane conversion with carbonate/hydroxide cycling and CO2 recapture is established in [Liu et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrp.2021.100503) and developed in [Vogt-Lowell et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/cssc.202401473). These are adjacent reaction media and conditions, not proof that this chemistry controls aging of the thin LSF coating at 700–735 °C. The uploaded Liu main text and retained Vogt-Lowell full text now confirm this chemistry directly (Liu Figs. 2–3; Vogt-Lowell Results and Experimental Section). The proposed reaction Li2CO3 + H2 → 2 LiOH + CO is therefore a prior-art candidate pathway, not a new mechanistic proposal or an established description of this catalyst.

**The closest dissertation overlap is now resolved.** Chacko's [2025 dissertation](../../../literature/papers/chacko2025-molten-salt-promoted-namgwo-promoter/original.pdf), Chapter 4.1, printed pp. 95–102 (PDF pp. 110–117), tests 45 wt% mixed carbonate on porous LSF at 800 °C with 20% ethane and approximately 3% O2/15% CO2 regeneration. It reports carbonate decomposition during inert purges and H2 during regeneration, assigned to reaction of reduced Fe with water generated by hydroxide carbonation. This establishes coupled carbonate/oxide regeneration and a specific route by which a nominally dry CO2 reset can change the core. It does not test the proposed equal-dose steam-timing comparison on the lower-loading Li2CO3 coating. The chapter explicitly qualifies its COx allocation by assumptions about steady operation, reforming and carbonate decomposition. Its approximately 63–65% ethylene yield is not a directly matched benchmark for Brody's material or protocol.

**Prevention versus incomplete recovery is also prior art.** [Fereres et al. 2018](../../../literature/papers/fereres2018-molten-carbonate-salts-for-advanced/original.pdf), §3.6/Fig. 9, printed p. 125, switches a Li/Na/K carbonate mixture from air to pure CO2 after 30 days at 640 °C. Further mass loss stops without restoration of its initial state. The same work shows strong dependence of measured loss on exposure duration and apparatus geometry (§§3.4–3.5). Thus neither delayed CO2 failing to restore a carbonate nor using inventories to separate evaporation from decomposition is a new general concept. This weakens the broad program claim while supporting a focused test of whether the *specific modeled steam purge* creates a consequential compatibility problem in coated LSF. No five-minute loss rate follows from the bulk-melt data.

**The other uploaded originals reinforce existing boundaries.** [Chan et al. 2018](../../../literature/papers/chan2018-enhancing-the-capacity-of-oxygen/original.pdf), Figs. 5–9 and Discussion, already couples selective oxygen capacity to phase cooperation and oxygen delivery. [Chacko et al. 2025](../../../literature/papers/chacko2025-effective-cox-suppression-by-a/original.pdf) ties promoter dispersion and suppressed oxygen release to lower COx. [Chacko et al. 2026](../../../literature/papers/chacko2026-unveiling-the-role-of-oxygen/original.pdf), Figs. 4–6, directly changes post-oxidation purge and regeneration conditions to control Fe–Mn phase/oxygen state and selectivity. [Morales et al. 2026](../../../literature/papers/morales2026-bridging-steady-state-and-chemical/original.pdf), Figs. 7–10, combines experiments and modeling of dynamic operation; a generic oxygen-state timing optimization is occupied territory. [Luongo et al. 2022](../../../literature/papers/luongo2022-oxygen-uncoupling-and-chemical-looping/original.pdf), Fig. 5 and Methods, already measures Na retention and stable function over 200 cycles for a different solid-carbonate-coated oxygen carrier/M1 system at 500 °C. That result prevents treating coating loss as inevitable; it does not establish Li2CO3/LSF steam stability at 700 °C.

## Competing explanations and decisive observations

| Explanation | Expected observation under a controlled intervention | What would weaken it |
|---|---|---|
| Retained promoter changes chemical form and loses its protective/selective function | Recovery varies with CO2/water treatment and accompanies restored promoter chemistry; a direct chemical assignment additionally requires evidence restricting coverage and ferrite changes | Restored carbonate inventory gives no functional recovery, or recovery is explained by another measured change |
| Li-containing material is lost or migrates away from relevant interfaces | Li moves to other bed regions, supports, walls, or deposits; recovery tracks restored coverage or replacement rather than carbonate amount alone | Repeated recovery with retained Li and no resolved persistent redistribution weakens permanent-loss explanations, but does not prove fixed interfacial coverage |
| Ferrite redox or phase history causes the apparent memory | Recovery follows the independently measured Fe/oxide state, irrespective of shell carbon inventory | Different selective rates persist after resolved ferrite changes are constrained and track restored promoter state; unresolved interfaces still limit exclusion |
| Ordinary solid/gas transport produces the history effect | Differences relax during an inert dwell or vary systematically with particle size, bed dilution, or rate | Differences persist after validated relaxation and transport controls, tracking shell chemistry instead |
| Coke masks sites or contributes carbon products | Carbon balance and oxidation products account for the apparent change; controlled burn-off recovers function | Recovery requires promoter restoration after equal coke removal |

These are predictions, not observations. Different mechanisms can coexist. In particular, hydroxide formation could change wetting and cause subsequent migration, so chemical and structural histories may become inseparable after severe aging. A coupled treatment mechanism is a legitimate result; exact equality of hidden material states is not a prerequisite for measuring useful recovery. Equal mean Fe valence and similar microscopy cannot establish equal interfaces.

## Conditional follow-on: recovery of representative Ar-aged material

This campaign is specified for the separate Ar-aging branch. It is not to be run in parallel by default with the timing test.

1. **Obtain representative loss.** Prepare methods-based nominal 10 wt% Li2CO3/LSF and verify composition, ferrite phases, and coating behavior. Use the published 700 °C cycle initially, with direct product/water measurement and inert-bed thermal-conversion controls. Retain fresh coated material and bare LSF controls. Budget for the sustained aging needed to reach the reported loss, rather than assuming a short test will reproduce it. A representative aged sample from the original program would help, but access is unverified. Parallel small beds or independently aged replicates are preferable to sequential treatments on one drifting bed. If an aged bed is split, preserve axial positions and split within positions; do not crush or homogenize away the spatial history. Verify that the small-bed loss resembles the reference in products and material changes before using accelerated aging.

2. **Run one four-arm regeneration comparison.** Cross low versus added CO2 with dry versus defined water content, at common O2 partial pressure, temperature, total flow, and exposure duration. The dry/low-CO2 arm is the ordinary oxidation reference. Select the two gas levels using thermodynamic screening, equipment bounds, and blank tests; the proposal does not assume universal concentrations. Apply the same four treatments to fresh material so that a preparation effect is distinguishable from recovery of aging. This is the initial matrix: defer temperature, salt-composition, particle-size, and isotope series.

   Water is an intervention because the candidate equilibrium Li2CO3 + H2O ⇌ 2 LiOH + CO2 couples the two chemical potentials. Its importance here remains unvalidated. The alternative H2/CO reaction also requires checking the actual gas environment. A common oxidation step may reduce coke/redox differences only if it preserves the operational loss. Fixed O2 pressure constrains ferrite changes but does not establish equal ferrite state: measure oxygen uptake and suitable Fe/phase indicators. If final conditioning is added to standardize a probe, verify that the proposed material contrast survives it. A contrast erased by conditioning cannot support a mechanistic rejection.

3. **Measure persistence and cost of recovery.** Probe all arms with the same ethane cycles, local temperature history, and analytical response correction; follow several cycles rather than one peak. Report useful-product recovery together with conversion, then use matched-conversion comparisons where needed to interpret selectivity. If a treatment produces persistent recovery, repeat it after renewed operational decline. That distinguishes reusable regeneration from a one-time change in preparation. A null result sets a bound for the tested treatment window, not for all possible regeneration. Include downtime, gases, and heating in the recovery benefit before selecting a larger campaign.

4. **Use material balances to limit the causal claim.** Analyze sister samples before treatment, after treatment, and after any final conditioning using controlled transfers. Integrate C, H, and O through every cycle segment, with calibrated switching residence times and direct water recovery through heated lines. Separate ferrite oxygen uptake from carbonate/hydroxide mass changes; TGA alone cannot do so. Close Li over catalyst, support/grit, sampled walls, and downstream traps where relevant. State unclosed inventory and detection limits. Use Li-sensitive mapping where localization is necessary; ordinary EDX cannot establish Li distribution, and SIMS or electron-spectroscopy results need stated resolution and calibration limitations. Fe-sensitive spectroscopy and phase measurements constrain oxide changes; room-air-exposed XPS alone cannot identify the operating state. Quantify residual coke rather than assuming equal oxidative exposure removes equal carbon.

The immediate result is the CO2 effect, water effect, and their interaction on persistent recovery after a defined ordinary oxidation reference, with material balances constraining interpretation. The initial experiment need not identify a chemically independent promoter state. Recovery coupled to a change in wetting or coverage remains valuable; report that coupled mechanism rather than claiming fixed-coverage chemistry.

Only then add the discriminator the unresolved mechanism requires. A 13CO2 or labeled-carbonate experiment can track promoter carbon, but needs net uptake/release and isotope-exchange blanks to separate turnover from exchange, including Sr/La carbonate and coke reservoirs. A dwell/particle-size comparison can test relaxing oxygen distributions, but crushing must not create fresh uncoated ferrite. Use 18O only if a remaining source ambiguity is consequential and exchange does not erase the diagnostic. No large isotope, size, or model campaign is part of the first test.

The initial scientific success criterion is a reproducible recovery or a meaningful bound on recovery, with persistent behavior and a material balance that narrows plausible causes. Stop the specifically independent-promoter-state framing if it cannot be identified; that does not automatically stop a useful coupled-regeneration program. Stop expansion if the treatment benefit is too brief or small to repay its full-cycle burden, unless the measured failure establishes a consequential mechanistic limit that warrants a separate focused question.

## The substantial program and its staged investment

The program thesis is that regeneration can become ineffective before the oxygen carrier loses redox capacity because operating exposures change the promoter and its interfaces. A proposed sequence is chemical conversion followed by persistent redistribution or interfacial reaction; alternatives include direct Li loss and ferrite restructuring. None is established as the controlling sequence here. Finding the mechanism and an avoidable boundary would give a basis for selecting regeneration atmosphere and reduction depth together. A rigorous negative result could bound the role of chemical history and redirect design toward physical retention.

Use “irrecoverable” operationally: loss not restored by a stated range of process-compatible treatments. Report separately a recoverable loss whose treatment burden exceeds its benefit; poor economics does not establish irreversible material damage. A failed four-arm comparison does not establish absolute material irreversibility. After the first treatment comparison, select one implicated aging exposure—such as water history or reduction depth—and compare its effects on recovery at successive aging states. Match other exposures and test only the causal alternative supported by the initial balances. This links the loss mechanism to a forward prediction without launching a broad screening matrix.

Construct the smallest predictive state description supported by the first measurements: ferrite reduction extent, a measured promoter chemical inventory, and only those coverage/phase variables independently shown necessary. Include gas and solid transport where required. Do not treat a different fitted diffusivity for every history as a mechanism.

The experimental sequence would then answer three linked questions:

- Which operating exposures convert a reversible promoter change into irreversible migration or loss?
- Can regeneration recover selective oxygen delivery at the same or lower total cycle burden?
- Does that causal rule predict a held-out operating history on the same material?

Train a model on one set of reduction depths and recovery conditions, then predict useful-product rate and recovery for a different history before measuring it. Compare against an oxide-state-only model and a simple empirical aging model. An additional state variable earns its place through prediction, not descriptive flexibility. Transfer to another material is outside the present scope; a strong causal result in this important system can have value without that extension.

The practical output could be a regeneration rule that retains the original material's selectivity, or a defensible limit showing that the apparent reversible reservoir rapidly becomes inaccessible. Either advances knowledge beyond optimizing cycle duration, provided it identifies the controlling loss and changes a realistic material or operation choice.

## Fair full-cycle comparison

Measure cumulative desired product per initial total catalyst inventory and total elapsed time, including both purges and all regeneration. For an ethylene objective report ethylene separately from C2+, and include light and heavy byproducts, coke, and shell-derived carbon. Brody's original downstream objective includes liquid fuels; report the relevant C2+ measure alongside ethylene so that this change in product objective is explicit. Compare at matched ethane conversion or report the conversion–selectivity–productivity tradeoff. Track oxygen, CO2, steam, catalyst/Li makeup, and regeneration heat. The Ar-to-steam purge change in the process model is an unvalidated materials exposure; the first water comparison addresses part of that uncertainty, not full process translation.

A measured increase in catalyst lifetime has value only if the treatment's time, gas, heating, separation, and materials burdens do not overwhelm the retained production. Changing the original long purges may improve throughput, but that is a process improvement requiring its own gas-transition validation; it is not evidence for the proposed chemistry. Do not infer heat self-sufficiency from exothermic half-cycles alone.

The first comparator is the same LSF material under ordinary regeneration. Further comparisons should include mature steady ODH and the alternative of separating oxygen release from the selective catalyst, as in [Luongo et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1002/aenm.202200405). No claim of economic advantage over steam cracking is warranted by the pilot.

## Confidence, central challenge, and decision

| Axis | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Scientific hypothesis | **Moderate plausibility for exposure-dependent regenerability; low confidence in a dominant carbonate-only cause.** Chemical conversion and structural changes may be sequential or coupled. Their causal connection to the observed decline remains unresolved. |
| Experimental feasibility and informative value | **Moderate for an informative timing test, conditional on platform access; moderate to lower for a full causal attribution.** Steam switching, direct water and Li balances, controlled transfer and suitable Fe/phase measurements are material dependencies. Actual loss rates and analytical recovery are unknown. Representative Ar aging adds hundreds of hours unless suitable material is available. Isolating chemistry at unchanged morphology may be infeasible and is not required. |
| Meaningful practical improvement | **Low to uncertain.** Documented Ar loss gives a finite output opportunity, but its cause is unresolved; extra steam damage, useful protective dose and makeup burden are unknown. Recovery or dry purging may dominate prevention. Full-cycle time and gas thresholds, not trace Li retention alone, determine value. No numerical probability is supported. |

The central challenge is **identifiability of coupled material states**, not finding a more elaborate redox model. The strongest contrary evidence is that active carbonate chemistry and combined regeneration already exist, while reported aging can plausibly arise from physical shell redistribution. The thesis and current related papers may further narrow the remaining contribution.

The independent review strengthens the program framing: identifying a recoverability boundary can be substantial science even when the molecular cause is initially unknown. I agree with that assessment. The earlier text gave too much weight to the absence of a distinctive pre-specified transition mechanism. A treatment catalogue would remain weak. After reading Fereres, exposure-dependent loss and failure of later CO2 recovery must also be treated as established adjacent concepts. Added knowledge now requires a consequential steam-compatibility result for this coating, an identified material change, and a fair full-cycle comparison.

The remaining reservation is practical and epistemic, not an objection to program development: representative aging may take hundreds of hours, multiple states change under regeneration, and the newly read dissertation and cover-gas studies substantially narrow the unoccupied question. These limit confidence in priority and novelty. There is no unresolved disagreement with the review's bounded-development recommendation. The final investment sequence is the single steam/CO2 timing test, one process-relevant prevention-versus-recovery comparison if warranted, and then a mechanism-directed history test. The representative Ar-aging/four-arm route is conditional follow-on, not a second simultaneous first campaign. A positive pilot or a second material is not a prior selection requirement; no broad material redesign is proposed.
