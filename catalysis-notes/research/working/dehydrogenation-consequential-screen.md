# Propane dehydrogenation: can a chemically defined regeneration endpoint improve useful output?

Screened and independently reviewed 2026-09-16. No new experiments. This is an opportunity assessment, not a claim that existing catalysts or mechanisms have been disproved.

The [fresh independent review](../reviews/dehydrogenation-fresh-value.md) recovered the exact Wu and Malizia supporting information, patent originals and Cheng benchmark artifacts. Its corrections and the [post-upload audit](../reviews/post-upload-pdh-ammonia-audit.md) are incorporated below. Modern benchmark main texts are now available; source access and actual experimental documentation gaps are distinguished at the end.

## Decision

**Do not promote a substantial new PDH program on the present evidence. Retain a conditional, finite Ga-state/regeneration study if a suitable platform and benchmark materials already exist.** The remaining question is potentially important: can one distinguish reversible loss of productive Ga chemistry from hydrogen-containing surface deposits and persistent Pt/Ga restructuring well enough to predict a better complete reaction/regeneration cycle? A useful answer could reduce unnecessary regeneration severity or identify when stronger regeneration is required. This opportunity does not require demonstrating improvement before proposing experiments.

The negative selection instead rests on direct prior art. Ga reduction, hydride promotion/inhibition, Pt-assisted hydrogen removal, coke relocation, durable redispersion, and separating combustion from catalyst reactivation have all been studied. Patents already target shorter Pt/Ga reactivation despite little coke. A new oxygen-uptake correction or another optimized oxygen treatment would not constitute the desired substantial advance. The surviving contribution would have to be a validated chemical state relationship that predicts a materially better operating choice under an untested history, beyond existing oxygen, water and temperature policies.

The connection to Gounder/Iglesia is direct in method: quantitative site requirements, adsorption and inhibition, kinetic discrimination, confinement, and the distinction between catalyst inventory and productive sites. Existing repository work includes [Ga/H-MFI kinetics](../../literature/papers/phadke2019-mechanism-and-kinetics-of-propane/paper.md), Pt–In confinement, and hydrogen effects on Pt. This screen does not claim that Ga hydrides or a hydrogen-dependent mechanism are new.

### 2. Ga chemistry is consequential, but hydrogen-state tuning is already a research program

[Sun et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41557-023-01392-x) assign beneficial H2 effects to metastable Ga hydrides on supported oxide catalysts. [Peng et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jpclett.6c00673) report opposite H2 effects on α- and β-Ga2O3, with different hydride persistence/reactivity. Their main texts are now available. Sun provides convergent spectroscopy, kinetics and calculations for its supported oxide; Peng concerns distinct oxide phases. These findings do not support a universal hydride rule or transfer their kinetic assignments to Wu’s Pt/Ga material. [Earlier Ga2O3/SiO2 work](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1872-2067(21)63900-1) instead reports improvement by removing inhibitory Ga hydrides using CO2. These are different materials and conditions, not a demonstrated contradiction.

The repository's [Phadke et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1021/jacs.8b11443) also supports a specific Ga-hydride-like center in Ga/H-MFI with H2 inhibition. Transferring one oxide or zeolite mechanism to every Ga catalyst would erase distinctions in support, coordination, oxidation state, metal proximity and coverage.

### 3. A concrete assay ambiguity survives, with independent evidence on both sides

[Wu et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/anie.202506704), main text pp. 3–8, compares impregnated Pt/Ga/alumina with mixed-GaAlOx-supported Pt. It assigns oxygen consumed beyond CO2 production to GaOx reoxidation, reporting 0.42, 0.97 and 1.30 µmol O2 after 4, 12 and 20 min of PDH. CO/H2 pretreatments, spectroscopy, transient kinetics and calculations separately support a role for reducibility and hydrogen release. The mixed oxide with 500 ppm Pt retains performance through a 45-cycle sequence; the nominal reaction and regeneration segments are each 20 min. Reported 550 °C propylene STY is 8.7 kg kg-catalyst−1 h−1. The caption calls the conversion initial but specifies measurement after 20 min. The exact SI is now recovered and independently inspected. Its reported subtraction explicitly omits H2O and CO; their actual contribution remains unmeasured. Fig. S2 labels the middle aging condition 10 min, versus 12 min in the main text. The pulse method’s CO2 channel at AMU40 appears to be a reporting typo, not proof of a wrong experiment. Fig. S14 spans 81 h, which cannot be reconstructed from the nominal 45 × (20 + 20) min description; do not infer complete-cycle output from those nominal segments. SI Table S1’s 8.7 value is a reaction-condition productivity, not a full-cycle average. The strong mixed oxide has 8 wt% nominal Ga versus 2 wt% in the impregnated reference, so this is a practical material comparison rather than a preparation-only causal contrast.

**Our inference, not the paper's result:** excess O2 beyond CO2 is not inherently a selective Ga-redox census. Hydrogen in retained hydrocarbons, hydrides or other surface species can consume oxygen to form water; CO and oxygen-bearing deposits also change the balance. Ignoring these terms can bias the assigned redox inventory. This does not establish a meaningful bias in the actual measurements, let alone invalidate their other evidence or the improved catalyst.

For a hydrocarbon deposit containing C and H atoms and ending in CO2/H2O,

`n(O2 consumed) = n(C) + n(H)/4`.

The reported excesses could therefore be matched, stoichiometrically, by 1.68, 3.88 and 5.20 µmol of retained H atoms. These are **equivalent amounts, not measured deposit compositions**. They set a useful analytical sensitivity target. The calculation cannot decide whether those hydrogen inventories existed, survived purge, or reacted during the reported pulses.

A more general oxygen balance over an oxidation step is

`ΔO_solid = 2 n(O2 consumed) − 2 n(CO2) − n(CO) − n(H2O) − Σ oxygen atoms in other effluent products`,

after accounting for oxygen-containing inlet species and matching the initial/final boundary. This gives net solid oxygen change, not specifically oxygen at productive Ga. Hydroxyl condensation, support oxygen, initially oxygenated deposits and changes during purge require explicit treatment. Merely adding an uncalibrated water mass-spectrometer channel would not solve the attribution problem.

Nor does a longer H2 residence time in a transient prove that H2 release controls steady catalytic turnover: a slowly exchanging nonproductive reservoir could produce a delay. The recovered SI explicitly models simultaneous H2/propylene production with Knudsen diffusion and predicts peak-time ratios around 0.47–0.54, below the measured ratios. This supports additional hydrogen residence beyond that transport null, while leaving productive and exchange-reservoir explanations to distinguish. An alternative model must reproduce the same observed responses and steady rates, not merely fit a different transient.

### 4. Regeneration sequencing and its practical motivation are not new

[US10654035B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10654035B2/en) separates Pt/Ga catalyst combustion, an oxygen-containing treatment and stripping. Its specific intervention is chlorine-assisted oxidative conditioning, with 21 and 61 ppm monochloroethane examples on Ga–Pt–K/silica-modified alumina. Conversion is measured after 20 s and after 30 cycles at a selected air-soak duration; intervening long treatments reactivate the catalyst. It is direct shorter-conditioning prior art, not a complete-cycle productivity test. [US20220126281A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20220126281A1/en) explicitly seeks to reduce or eliminate a long air treatment through enriched oxygen, including catalysts that lose activity despite negligible coke. Its examples vary oxygen and wet regeneration, with endpoint conversion comparisons after prior cycling. These disclosures directly occupy “restore Ga state rather than merely burn coke” and “shorten the regeneration step.” They do not provide a uniquely identified elementary mechanism or a complete equal-output process comparison.

In a different industrially relevant catalyst, [Malizia et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1021/acscatal.5c00593) use spectroTAP to separate Cr oxidation and carbon removal. Their recovered original manuscript shows Cr oxidation before most carbon combustion and continued CO2 production after gaseous O2 disappears; the interpretation involves chromate-mediated coke oxidation. This is a close methodological precedent. It also cautions that retaining some carbon may be compatible with activity and useful for cyclic heat supply. Generic coupled spectroscopy/pulse balances or discovering redox before coke burnoff would not be a new contribution.

## The remaining testable program, if a platform makes it inexpensive

### Specific hypothesis and value

**H1:** on a defined Pt/Ga catalyst, the useful regeneration endpoint is controlled by restoration of a particular productive Ga/Pt state plus removal of a small blocking hydrogen/carbon population. Bulk carbon disappearance and gross oxygen consumption are poor predictors. Once this state is restored, extending oxidation mainly consumes time or changes the next induction/selectivity history. A quantitative state model could select a shorter or less severe cycle while retaining propylene output.

**Strong alternatives:** the published redox assignment is already adequate; the slow step is Pt redispersion rather than Ga reoxidation; carbon removal and productive-state restoration cannot be separated at usable conditions; purge/water inhibition explains recovery; or a simple established oxygen/temperature policy predicts output as well as the proposed state model. Another possibility is that retained carbon supplies needed regeneration heat, so a laboratory duty-cycle improvement disappears in an integrated process.

The proposed contribution is **not** simply that H1 can occur. It is a measured relationship that predicts an operating choice across a new history, with full-cycle benefit. A negative result can rule out unnecessary spectroscopy or an ineffective shortened regeneration policy.

### Stage A — determine whether a consequential decision exists

1. Use the now-reviewed Wu SI and patent examples, resolving the remaining pulse calibration and cycle-timing details needed for replication before fixing the comparison. Reproduce one impregnated Pt/Ga/alumina material and the mixed-GaAlOx reference, including independent preparations, actual metal contents, activation, temperature and useful-output histories. Use the strong material as a benchmark from the start.
2. On the functioning material, compare the documented regeneration with one credible shorter protocol and a credible stronger/oxygen-enriched reference informed by the patents. Keep reaction feed, thermal histories and purges matched where causal interpretation requires it. Separately permit each policy its own useful practical setting.
3. Measure all reaction and regeneration time, propylene, cracking products, H2, CO/CO2 and actual water delivery/recovery. Measure bed temperature during oxidation; a pulse that heats the solid is not a purely chemical perturbation. Confirm flow, particle-size and dilution independence at a diagnostic rate condition as well as operation at useful conversion.
4. The central output is integrated net propylene per total catalyst inventory and elapsed cycle time, accompanied by carbon selectivity, H2 balance, gas use and heating/cooling burden. A return to the same initial rate is not an equal-output comparison. Industrial continuous regeneration may alter required catalyst circulation/inventory rather than shutting down the plant for the laboratory regeneration fraction.

If a short protocol already works but a simple oxygen-dose/temperature rule predicts it, the result may be useful engineering but does not justify this substantial mechanistic program. If the strong reference removes the consequential loss at acceptable resources, prefer it. Neither outcome negates the importance of PDH.

### Stage B — separate retained hydrogen/carbon from solid oxidation

Use separate sacrificial histories for partial regeneration endpoints. A propane activity assay can itself reduce Ga, change retained species or initiate coke; repeatedly assaying one specimen between oxygen doses would mix the observation with the intervention.

Compare a propane-aged specimen, a coke-free reduced specimen and a fully oxidized blank, with support and Pt-free controls only where they answer a needed assignment. Quantify O2, CO, CO2, H2O and H2 using calibrated recovery through the actual hot bed and lines. Resolve water adsorption tails and blank hydroxyl release. Confirm that the analytical uncertainty can distinguish a meaningful fraction of the approximately micromole oxygen inventory. If it cannot, report an interval rather than a corrected Ga-site count.

Use D labeling only after the ordinary balance proves informative: C3D8 can constrain hydrocarbon-derived retained hydrogen, but H/D exchange with support OH and isotope effects require paired controls. Label in water is not uniquely evidence of blocking coke. Directly measured gas inventories plus an independently calibrated oxidation-state marker are more useful than assigning every water molecule to one reservoir. Bulk XAS may miss the small active fraction; UV–vis intensity or an XPS fitted component must not silently become a site count.

Fit the simplest model that separates a reversible chemical inventory from persistent material loss, with known reaction equilibrium and product inhibition included. Compare it with a carbon-only model and a redox-only model. Demand a withheld prediction, not just a lower fitting error from additional states. If the oxygen/hydrogen correction is analytically real but does not change the operating decision, preserve the methodological finding without promoting a major catalyst campaign.

### Stage C — test useful prediction

Train the state/output relation using only the first spent history. Before measuring complete-cycle outcomes for the reserved second history, freeze the model, candidate policies, decision margin and predictions for its second duration or defined propylene/H2 burden. Test the prediction against ordinary operation, the strong mixed-oxide reference and the best simple oxygen policy from Stage A. Require retained selectivity and useful output through enough repeat cycles to distinguish induction from persistent decline. Do not extrapolate those cycles to industrial lifetime.

An illustrative accounting identity explains the causal opportunity without forecasting performance. If reaction production is unchanged, reducing a 20 min regeneration to 5 min during a 20 min reaction cycle changes the ideal laboratory production fraction from `20/40` to `20/25`, a factor of 1.6. This assumes no purge/heating overhead, no production loss, no added gas penalty and no heat deficit. Actual benefit must include all those terms; the identity is not a predicted 60% improvement. A circulating industrial unit needs its own heat and catalyst-inventory calculation.

**Expansion criterion:** a reproducible chemical distinction changes which material or complete regeneration policy is best and predicts that choice at a new condition. This would support a substantial program on transferable state control across selected Ga formulations. It would not justify indiscriminate extension to Co, Cr, PtSn or isobutane before their distinct chemistry is established.

### Minimum campaign and stopping decisions

The minimum campaign has two stages, with no requirement to synthesize every literature benchmark. First reproduce the strong mixed GaAlOx reference and the intended comparison material on an existing platform. Use at least two independent preparations of the material taken forward; repeat the reference cycle sufficiently to estimate ordinary drift and run-to-run scatter before defining a decision threshold. Prespecify two spent histories, but compare the documented regeneration, one shorter policy and the best credible simple oxygen/temperature policy initially only at the first history. Reserve the second history’s complete-cycle outcomes for prospective validation; do not measure them during Stage A or use them to choose the model or policies. Record integrated propylene, total elapsed time, all gases and thermal duty. Fresh matched specimens or demonstrated full resets are needed when histories carry over. Set the smallest useful contrast from that measured scatter and a concrete inventory, gas-use or duty decision; do not choose it after seeing the favored treatment.

**Stop before mechanistic expansion** if the complete-cycle comparison has no resolvable useful loss to recover, if the strong reference or simple policy performs equivalently within the prespecified margin, or if heat/catalyst-circulation accounting removes the apparent benefit. If a contrast survives, use sacrificial specimens at partial regeneration endpoints and calibrated C/H/O recovery, then fit one productive-state/output relationship. Use only the first history to select and fit the model. Freeze the model, candidate policies, decision margin and predicted second-history policy ranking/output before measuring any second-history complete-cycle outcomes. Then run that second history as Stage C, including the ordinary, strong-material and best-simple-policy comparisons. A useful result must predict which policy gives better complete-cycle output on this prospectively held-out history, while retaining selectivity and staying within gas and heat constraints. A good uptake fit without this prediction does not pass.

If the required water/oxygen recovery or working-state marker cannot resolve the relevant inventory, report an uncertainty bound and stop that mechanistic branch. A negative result may still select a simpler policy. No finite campaign establishes industrial catalyst lifetime. An external Pt benchmark is used as a source-grounded complete-cycle comparison first; replicate it only if its comparison could change the choice and its full protocol is available.

## Confidence and limits

- **Scientific:** high confidence in the need for complete elemental accounting; low-to-moderate confidence that the omitted retained-H term is material in Wu's experiment, despite the now-confirmed omission from the reported calculation. Moderate plausibility that regeneration contains separable chemical events, but the specific Ga state relationship remains unvalidated and has close precedents.
- **Experimental:** moderately feasible for a laboratory already equipped for high-temperature PDH and quantitative wet-gas switching. A useful initial cycle comparison is easier than a selective productive-site inventory. Dilute Pt spectroscopy and micromole water recovery may be limiting.
- **Practical:** potentially consequential if the state relationship removes a real regeneration/circulation burden on a competitive material. Present confidence is low because durable references, established oxygen policies, heat integration and shaping may erase the advantage. No facility, sample access or commercial cost advantage is established.
- **Originality:** provisional and currently insufficient for portfolio promotion. The recovered SI clarifies the assay but leaves its bias and complete cycle chronology unresolved. Newly read modern benchmark main texts strengthen the comparison rather than removing these questions. An assay qualification alone would be a smaller methodological result, not the user's desired outcome.
