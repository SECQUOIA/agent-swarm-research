# Supported-metal candidate: methane control in Ru-catalyzed polyolefin hydrogenolysis

Screened 2026-09-16 under the [direction-search brief](README.md). No experiments were performed. This is an opportunity screen, not a selected program and not a claim that any published catalyst has been disproved.

## Decision

**Do not promote a substantial program. Retain at most one bounded diagnostic**: does methane suppression on dilute bimetallic Ru catalysts arise from scarcity of contiguous Ru ensembles (then it is insensitive to H2 pressure and to local H2 depletion), or from a change in the competence of Ru sites that still depends on H* coverage (then the published ambient-pressure advantage is condition-specific)? That distinction would change a real choice between high-pressure H2 operation on monometallic Ru, a Pt-host dilute-Ru alloy, and a cheap-dopant Ru-host alloy, and it would determine whether reactor H2 delivery still matters for gas make. Everything else that a first campaign would measure—methane origin, H2-pressure effect, particle-size effect, site isolation, hydrogen-reservoir supports, solvents, reactive separation, impurity poisoning, and process economics—has direct prior art from at least seven groups between 2020 and 2026.

Two runner-up candidates were considered and set aside (see the last section): selective alkyne hydrogenation in olefin streams and direct H2O2 synthesis on Pd.

## 1. Practical problem, scale, and connection to Iglesia

Polyolefins are produced at the scale of hundreds of megatons per year with little recycling (statement from the Sadow/Huang paper's introduction, [Wang et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1021/acscatal.3c04987)). Hydrogenolysis on supported Ru or Pt at about 200–300 °C and 1–70 bar H2 converts polyethylene (PE) and polypropylene (PP) into fuel- and lubricant-range alkanes with methane as the main by-product ([Engineering review, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eng.2025.12.041)). Methane is low-value and consumes one H2 per C–C cleavage; every cleavage that produces methane spends hydrogen on the least valuable carbon. The published economic screen in [Joule 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joule.2026.102384) estimates a minimum selling price for linear-chain naphtha from PE hydrogenolysis about 1.8-fold that of primary production, with branched naphtha and propylene from hydrocracking closer to competitive; that abstract does not isolate the methane contribution, and I could not retrieve the sensitivity analysis. The stake is therefore real but prospective: no commercial hydrogenolysis plant exists, and methane is one of several cost levers alongside H2 price, precious-metal inventory, and product slate.

The connection to Iglesia is direct in method and substance. Flaherty and Iglesia established that C–C cleavage in n-alkanes on Ir, Rh, and Pt proceeds through intermediates stripped of 3–4 H atoms, with rates inversely dependent on H2 pressure and cleavage position set by the rotational entropy of the alkyl chains attached to the cleaved bond, which explains the preference for nonterminal cleavage ([JACS 2013](https://doi.org/10.1021/ja4093743); [JACS 2014](https://doi.org/10.1021/ja5037429); [J. Catal. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcat.2013.11.026)). Hu and Iglesia's prevalence × competence treatment of contiguous bare-atom ensembles on crowded Ru surfaces ([local note](../../../literature/papers/hu2024-dynamics-of-elementary-steps-on/paper.md), p.5–12) is the natural framework for asking whether a second cleavage on an adsorbed fragment needs an ensemble whose prevalence is set by H* coverage. Fischer and Iglesia's inhibitor-scavenging reinterpretation of "spillover" ([local note](../../../literature/papers/fischer2023-the-nature-of-hydrogen-spillover/paper.md), p.11–20) bears on the hydrogen-reservoir explanation offered for WOx-promoted Ru. The connection to Gounder is weak (acid-assisted hydrocracking is adjacent but not the question here); this screen does not claim otherwise.

The repository already holds polyolefin work on W/Na ethenolysis; that is a different reaction with a different catalyst and a separate decision, so this screen does not duplicate it. Local read notes on [Jaydev et al.](../../../literature/papers/jaydev2023-consumer-grade-polyethylene-recycling-via/paper.md) (1 nm Ru/TiO2, ammonia poisoning) and the unread [Kita et al. 2026](../../../literature/papers/kita2026-poisoning-effects-of-impurities-on/paper.md) impurity study constrain the poisoning branch.

## 2. The consequential uncertainty

Several groups now report that diluting Ru with a second metal, isolating Ru atoms, or embedding Ru in a hydrogen-reservoir support lowers methane. The mechanistic assignments differ, and the difference matters for what to buy and how to run the reactor:

- The Vlachos group assigns low methane on Ru9Pt91 to the absence of a neighboring Ru site for the second (terminal) scission of an adsorbed fragment; the first scission is Ru-catalyzed, the second would need adjacent Ru, and surface H* "does not directly participate" in that second step. The claimed consequence is methane selectivity independent of H2 pressure, demonstrated from ambient to 2 MPa ([Nature Commun. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-54786-x)).
- The ETH group reports Co10Ru90/TiO2 (isolated Co atoms in a Ru host) cutting methane on PP from 58% to about 11% at 513 K and 20 bar, attributing it to Ru–Co–Ru motifs that "moderate" scission and suppress late-stage over-cleavage; no H2-pressure variation is reported ([Adv. Mater. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/adma.73643)).

**Inference, not a published finding:** a surface that is nominally 90% Ru retains abundant contiguous Ru pairs and triples, so a purely geometric ensemble-scarcity model cannot explain a 5-fold methane reduction on Co10Ru90 unless Co is strongly surface-enriched or the required ensemble is much larger than a pair. Either the two systems suppress methane by different mechanisms, or the geometric explanation for Ru9Pt91 is incomplete and a change in Ru-site competence (fragment binding, hydrogenation/desorption versus further dehydrogenation) that still depends on H* coverage contributes. This is the uncertainty worth resolving, because:

- If ensemble scarcity governs, a dilute-Ru catalyst keeps low methane wherever local H2 is depleted—viscous high-Mw melts, flow reactors where H2 bubbles create dry zones ([Feng et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ceja.2024.100701)), and ambient-pressure operation—so the catalyst choice substitutes for compression and for aggressive H2 delivery, and only a Pt-host (or another host that isolates Ru) design should work.
- If competence still tracks H* coverage, the ambient-pressure result is condition-specific, the cheap-dopant Ru-host route can be equally good at pressure, and reactor H2 delivery remains a design constraint regardless of catalyst.

Generic "characterize the alloy more" is not proposed. The question is a specific kinetic discrimination with a material and operating consequence.

## 4. Hypotheses and discriminating observations

- **H1 (geometric prevalence).** Methane at matched conversion tracks the prevalence of contiguous bare Ru ensembles large enough to hold the second-cleavage transition state. On monometallic Ru that prevalence is set by H* coverage (H2 pressure, temperature); on a dilute-Ru alloy it is set by surface Ru fraction and is insensitive to H2 pressure and to local H2 depletion.
- **H2 (competence).** The second metal alters the Ru site's balance between fragment hydrogenation/desorption and further dehydrogenation; methane still declines with H2 pressure on the alloy, only from a lower baseline. Local H2 depletion then raises methane on alloys too.
- **H3 (extent and product-phase artifact).** Alloys mainly lower total cleavage rate; at equal time they sit at lower conversion, where less volatile product has re-entered the melt. At matched conversion and matched product-vapor exposure the advantage shrinks.

Discriminators: (i) methane fraction versus H2 partial pressure (1–30 bar, Ar makeup at constant total pressure so vapor–liquid partition of products is held) at matched conversion on Ru, Ru9Pt91, and Co10Ru90—H1 predicts a flat alloy response, H2 a parallel decline, H3 that the alloy advantage narrows once conversion is matched; (ii) the same comparison under reactive separation versus sealed batch, which isolates the secondary route; (iii) H2 order and H2/D2 KIE for n-C36 on each catalyst in the Gorte/Vohs flow configuration, where dry zones deliberately lower local H*; (iv) surface Ru fraction from CO-IR and H2–D2 exchange on each alloy, so H1 can be tested quantitatively rather than by nominal composition. A pressure-independent methane fraction on Co10Ru90 combined with a large measured surface-Co enrichment would rescue H1 in both systems; pressure dependence on either alloy falsifies H1 for that material.

## 5. Smallest informative first campaign

Materials: Ru/C or Ru/TiO2 (3–4 nm), Ru9Pt91/C, and Co10Ru90/TiO2 prepared per the published recipes, each from two independent preparations; particle sizes matched by STEM. Feeds: PE of Mw ~4000 (the RuPt paper's model feed) and one ~100 kDa HDPE to probe melt viscosity. Conditions: 300 °C for the RuPt comparison and 513 K for Co10Ru90's published condition; H2 partial pressures 1, 5, 10, 20, 30 bar with Ar makeup; two stirring rates and two catalyst loadings as transport checks; conversion series by time so that comparisons are made at 40 ± 5% conversion and at matched H2 consumption. Accounting: gas, liquid, and solid fractions quantified per the Nature Protocols method with carbon balance ≥95% as a design choice; H2 consumption from pressure drop or flow integration and checked against the number of cleavages implied by the product distribution (one H2 per cleavage to alkanes; less where olefins survive). Measurement guards: C4–C7 losses and headspace sampling bias are the specific failure modes documented in the retraction. Replication: triplicate at the center condition per preparation. Decision informed: whether a cheap-dopant Ru-host alloy at moderate pressure can replace either compression or Pt-host alloys, and whether reactor H2 delivery still controls gas make on dilute-Ru catalysts.

Numerical targets here are choices, not predictions.

## 6. Confidence judgments

- **Hypothesis.** H1 is plausible for Ru9Pt91 and already partly supported; that it also explains Co10Ru90 is doubtful on geometric grounds. A mixed outcome (different mechanisms in the two systems) is the likeliest, which would still be informative. Moderate confidence that the pressure test discriminates; low confidence that either pure hypothesis survives.
- **Feasibility and informativeness.** Moderate, conditional on a 300 °C stirred autoclave with quantitative three-phase product accounting and on reproducing two alloy syntheses from other groups. The measurements are standard; the main risks are irreproducible alloy surfaces and the difficulty of holding conversion matched across catalysts with 5-fold different rates.
- **Practical improvement.** Low. The published economics are unfavorable for linear naphtha regardless of methane, the methane lever is already reduced to a few percent by several routes, and the diagnostic can at best choose among existing materials. It cannot create a new one.

## 7. Fair benchmark and stop/pivot rule

Benchmark: liquid (C5–C45) yield per gram of total precious metal per hour at matched conversion and at methane ≤5% of converted carbon, compared against Ru/C at 30 bar (Kim et al.), Ru9Pt91/C at 1 MPa (Vlachos), and Co10Ru90/TiO2 at 20 bar (Ge et al.), all on the same feed and with the same accounting. Reporting methane at matched H2 consumption alone, as in the RuPt paper, is not sufficient.

Stop: if both alloys show <1.3-fold change in methane fraction between 1 and 30 bar at matched conversion while Ru shows >3-fold, H1 holds and the remaining decision is economic; close. Pivot: if either alloy's methane tracks pressure like Ru, the ambient-pressure advantage is condition-specific; the useful follow-up is reactor H2 delivery, not catalyst mechanism. Stop also if the alloy advantage largely disappears under matched conversion with reactive separation (H3), or if independent preparations of either alloy do not reproduce the published methane fractions within a factor of two.

## 8. Recommendation

Do not promote. The reaction is important and the method fit with Iglesia's tradition is strong, but the field has moved fast: methane origin, the H2-pressure lever, nuclearity, site isolation in three architectures, hydrogen-reservoir supports, solvents, reactive separation, impurity poisoning, and process economics all have direct 2020–2026 precedents from Vlachos, Sadow/Huang, Delferro, Pérez-Ramírez, Rorrer/Beckham/Román-Leshkov, Gorte/Vohs, and several other groups. What survives is a single cross-architecture kinetic discrimination (Section 2) that both the Vlachos and ETH groups are better placed to run and may already be running. Retain it as a bounded diagnostic only if a suitable platform already exists; otherwise drop it. An informative negative (alloy methane depends on pressure after all) would still be useful because it would bound the ambient-pressure claim.

## Runner-up candidates considered

- **Selective alkyne/diene hydrogenation in olefin streams.** Mature industrial practice (Pd–Ag tail-end and CO-modulated front-end units) and dense academic prior art, including metal-free heterolytic routes on CeO2 (Pérez-Ramírez, 2012 onward) and defect-rich BN ([Nature Commun. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-64886-x)). The repository's ZrO2 acid–base-pair hydrogenation ([local topic](../../../literature/topics/metals-oxides-cox-ft-and-lohc.md)) operates far above converter temperatures. The consequential uncertainty—green-oil formation under real front-end H2/CO—rests on proprietary plant data that an academic screen cannot benchmark fairly. Not chosen.
- **Direct H2O2 synthesis on Pd.** Flaherty's group has established the heterolytic proton–electron-transfer mechanism and the roles of solvent, pH, halides, and cluster size ([JACS 2016](https://doi.org/10.1021/jacs.5b10669); [ACS Catal. 2018 perspective](https://doi.org/10.1021/acscatal.7b04107)). The remaining questions are that group's own program, and a fair benchmark against the anthraquinone process requires selectivity under safe H2/O2 ratios that published work already targets. Not chosen.
- **Co/Ru FTS with water and pellet transport (landscape Priority 12)** was not selected because water effects, olefin readsorption, and intrapellet liquid evolution are established (Iglesia 1990s; Claeys/Wolf; Gladden MRI) and the repository's existing FTS opportunity cards already cover it without identifying a decision that a bounded study would change.
